# World Bible: Nacre and the Soul Network

## The shape of the world

Nacre is a modern coastal city of roughly two million people, built on the broad salt shelf of Nacre Sound. Its oldest neighborhoods sit above a buried layer of utility tunnels and resonance relays. Glass transit arcs cross the water; freight runs inland along the Meridian Corridor; farms and small towns in the Verge maintain their own clocks, work crews, and techniques.

The city is a city-republic rather than a kingdom. The **Nacre Assembly** passes public budgets and safety rules, while the **Continuity Office** manages the Civic Spine during ordinary emergencies. The formal network is called the **Civic Weave**. People who talk about its strange collective quality call it the Soul Network. “Soul” is a social word, not proof that the network stores or steals a person’s essence.

The setting is contemporary: phones, electric transit, hospital monitors, delivery vehicles, online classes, and cheap sensor lamps sit beside practices that can stabilize a broken brace, repair a signal, or make a knife turn in a trained hand. The fantasy enters through attention and shared practice, not through medieval ruins or monsters in every alley.

## Geography

### Nacre districts

- **Lattice Ward:** University, laboratories, old civic buildings, and the central **Civic Spine** exchange. The public looks ordinary from the street. Beneath it, thousands of relay plates carry carefully limited practice shares.
- **Saltmarket:** The working waterfront, wholesale kitchens, repair stalls, immigrant neighborhoods, and the **Saltmarket Exchange**. It is the first place where a private technique becomes a public economic dependency.
- **Nine Bridges:** Dense residential towers and transit crossings. The bridges are named for the old routes that joined separated neighborhoods, not for an ancient magical order.
- **Ashfields:** Freight yards, foundries, equipment warehouses, and the Marrow Falls edge. Industrial techniques are more physical and less socially supervised, which makes them useful for hiding hinges.
- **Quiet Quarter:** A mixed-income district with weak signal coverage and a strong culture of practical privacy. Its residents are not anti-technology; many simply distrust institutions that call a choice a “default.”
- **Crown Terrace:** Administrative offices, wealthy clinics, research leases, and the old **Crown Vault** beneath the former continuity hall. The name is historical, not a claim that Nacre has a monarch.
- **The Lower Wards:** Flood-prone blocks where several unofficial nodes share repair crews. Their informal practices are neither harmless nor automatically illegal; their records are poor.

### Outside Nacre

- **The Meridian Corridor:** Freight, factories, grain terminals, and the routes that connect Nacre to the inland plateau. Threadline’s training kits and replacement parts travel here.
- **The Verge:** A loose coalition of towns that kept independent techniques after the Hush. They trade knowledge selectively and have no obligation to join a Nacre institution.
- **The Salt Road:** A coastal route of small clinics, kitchens, schools, and repair depots. It is the setting for many low-visibility cases and for the first inter-city public network.
- **Pale Harbor:** A working harbor with memory-heavy civic architecture. It matters in the middle volumes because the network’s oldest physical routes meet there, not because it introduces a new supernatural realm.

## Daily life

People learn techniques for work and safety. A nurse practices a brace that reduces the force of a fall. A courier learns a balance step for wet bridges. A locksmith studies how pressure changes a pin. A cook shares a way to judge heat without touching a pan. A student may use a network to improve a technique, but must still attend classes, pass examinations, earn a license, pay rent, care for relatives, and live with the aftertaste of a difficult share.

The network is useful in ordinary life because it is unevenly distributed. A wealthy clinic has a dedicated relay and a trained ombud. A courier has a cracked phone and a friend who remembers a dangerous step. A student may practice with a classmate who has a different body, fear, or goal. The same technique can help one person and harm another if its boundaries are copied without context.

Licensing matters. The **Solo Seal** proves a person can perform a core technique alone; it does not confer permission to work through partners. A **Working Share** permits a person to use a limited practice with named partners and requires a separate witnessed assessment. A **Civic Practitioner** can maintain public infrastructure, but only after a local council or institution accepts responsibility for the surrounding network.

## History

### Before the Weave

Resonant practice existed as local, embodied knowledge. Teachers passed sequences directly, printed manuals recorded “safe” forms, and families kept private repair rituals. The practice was real but often exclusionary: talent, money, and apprenticeship decided who could learn.

### The Hush — forty-seven years before the present

An emergency synchronization experiment connected thousands of people without usable consent boundaries. Similar attention patterns merged, withdrawal became impossible for many participants, and the city experienced a nine-day cascade of involuntary technique behavior. The event is called **the Hush** because people could hear one another’s technique cues and could not silence them. The worst effects were not explosions or monsters; they were people unable to stop performing a learned action, losing the ability to recognize their own intention, and institutions that could not tell consent from compliance.

The Hush remains a public trauma. Public school exercises begin with an opt-out and an explicit stop signal. Many people distrust any network that promises perfect safety. No one uses the Hush as a casual metaphor for ordinary disagreement.

### The Civic Compact — forty years before the present

A coalition of nurses, transit workers, engineers, teachers, cooks, performers, and maintenance crews rebuilt the network. Its central rule was the **Return Path**: every shared technique had to show what it was, where it came from, how long it was being shared, and how a person could leave. The network was intentionally incomplete. It could suggest a shape, but it could not treat one person as the permanent answer.

The Compact created the Civic Weave, the public maintenance crews, the Choir of Practice, and the first consent ledgers. It also left a practical problem: emergency response still needed a way to coordinate when communication failed. Its public rule allowed active check-ins, manual signals, and explicit local fallbacks; it did not allow an unanswered person to count as agreement.

### Marrow Falls — fourteen years before the present

A storm and a fractured relay caused a citywide outage in the Ashfields. Iona Sorn, then a junior architect, added a temporary **Crown Clause** to the emergency response. The clause treated a missing reply as a provisional agreement to the last approved civic sequence. It saved some lives and prevented a bridge collapse, but it also made a person’s silence look like permission.

Iona disappeared after the inquiry. The official record says she resigned and left Nacre. The real record, reconstructed across the series, shows that she continued to maintain a hidden network of seeded clauses. She believed another Hush was coming and that only a central authority could prevent it.

### Present day

The Civic Weave is reliable enough to be ordinary and fragile enough to be political. A missing beat in a shared technique is usually blamed on poor training, student error, or a bad device. Threadline sells convenient training packages. The Continuity Office keeps emergency procedures opaque. The Quiet House publishes maps of places where people can disconnect. The Verge sends techniques and supplies without asking Nacre for permission.

The present-day opening begins when a Threadline-distributed practice kit fails in a way that exposes a missing boundary-release beat.

## Technology and society

### Resonance hardware

The Civic Spine uses **relay plates** made from mineral fiber and copper. They carry a share only when a person has made a deliberate connection. Household devices can hold a short practice echo, but cannot open a link by accident. Hospitals, schools, workshops, and transit stations have larger relay rooms called **exchange rooms**. A strong exchange room improves the range and clarity of a link but also leaves a detailed record of who connected, what scope they granted, and whether they withdrew.

A device can be damaged without destroying the person, but a damaged exchange room can turn many safe techniques into unreliable ones. This is why maintenance, custody, and consent records are political issues.

### Consent ledgers

A consent ledger is a human-readable record, not a magical ledger that judges morality. It records the purpose, duration, people involved, and permitted retention. A practitioner can inspect it, but a person may choose not to remember an old echo. A false ledger is evidence of a wrong, not proof that the network itself is lying.

### Work and education

Technique knowledge is taught in schools, apprenticeships, workplaces, households, and community groups. The public license system is imperfect. A person can know a dangerous technique without a license; a licensed person can still act badly. The system is designed to make responsibility traceable without pretending that paperwork can replace judgment.

## Factions

### Civic Spine and the Nacre Assembly

The public maintenance system and city government are not identical. The Assembly controls budgets and recognizes local institutions. The Spine’s crews keep the network alive and have a strong tradition of refusing unsafe orders. Their internal conflict is between quiet continuity and democratic accountability.

### Continuity Office

The Continuity Office certifies emergency plans and investigates network failures. Commissioner Iven Sore has used secrecy to prevent panic and to hide the early signs of the Crown Clause. The Office is capable of genuine service and dangerous when it treats the public as an audience rather than participants.

### Threadline

Threadline builds relay hardware, training kits, and commercial practice systems. It is the contracted assembler and seller of the opening institute kit, using an upstream technique bundle whose licensor the opening investigation cannot yet identify. Threadline’s public claim is that standardized techniques make work safer; its private model depends on predictable consent defaults and continuous data. Oren Vey does not know the full Crown Clause history at first, but he understands that a society which cannot leave a network is easier to contract with.

### Quiet House

The Quiet House is a decentralized privacy movement made of engineers, former nodes, caregivers, and people who have been harmed by forced sharing. It is not a single gang. Its local cells sabotage exchange rooms, publish hidden-default practices, and sometimes cut links that other people need. Leo Marr is its most visible organizer. The movement’s legitimate demand—freedom from involuntary connection—becomes morally difficult when its members make that freedom impossible for patients in an emergency.

### Choir of Practice

The Choir of Practice is a loose professional cooperative of nurses, repairers, performers, and teachers. It publishes plain-language technique lineages and maintains independent study groups. It is the social home of many recurring supporting characters, but it is not automatically democratic or free of hierarchy.

### The Verge

The Verge’s town councils and work crews share techniques with Nacre but refuse a single central license. They represent the possibility of a network with many cultures and no requirement that every community use the same institution.

### The Conductor’s Choir

The hidden network of nodes seeded with Crown hinges is called the Conductor’s Choir in later volumes. It is a coalition of people, training routines, and copied techniques rather than a separate species. Many members believe they are preventing another Hush. Some want power; some want safety; some were coerced into joining.

## Locations that must remain concrete

- **Civic Spine 7:** Marek’s night-shift repair hub in Lattice Ward. It has a repair bench, a lockable relay cabinet, a sink with warm water, and a wall of handwritten maintenance notes.
- **Nacre Institute of Applied Practice:** Marek’s college. Its practical rooms have scuffed floors, standardized training cards, and an exam office that treats a missing step as a student failure until proven otherwise.
- **Crown Vault:** A sealed maintenance chamber beneath the old continuity hall. It is reached through service tunnels and never appears as a mystical tower.
- **Marrow Falls:** The industrial edge where the outage exposed the Crown Clause. It remains a working freight district, not a sealed adventure dungeon.
- **The Saltmarket Exchange:** A busy public room where techniques, parts, and stories are exchanged. It is the best recurring example of the network affecting ordinary work.
- **The Lower Wards:** Flood-prone neighborhoods that create a public need for a mesh but lack the capital to install one.
- **The Verge exchange house:** A community-run building where Nacre practitioners learn that a public network can have several local rules.

## World rules and guardrails

1. No faction has perfect knowledge of the network. Every institution has blind spots, private records, and ordinary administrative failures.
2. The world has no hidden dungeon realm, system shop, or unexplained monster hierarchy. Investigations remain grounded in people, records, places, and techniques.
3. A person’s ability to connect does not determine their worth, intelligence, or political legitimacy.
4. A technique can be beautiful and still be unsafe if its boundary is missing.
5. The city is not a backdrop. Schools, hospitals, transit, work, families, and neighborhoods must feel affected when the network changes.
6. The story can show disagreement without making one faction cartoonishly evil. The hardest opponents often describe a real fear correctly.
7. The final public institution must have ordinary maintenance, dispute procedures, opt-outs, and limits. No ending promises that everyone will join.
