# Power-System Bible: Consent, Observation, and the Civic Weave

## Core concept

The Soul Network is a distributed practice network. It improves a technique by comparing what several people can consciously observe, perform, and explain. It does not copy a person’s power or private mind. “Soul” is a common name for the persistent pattern of attention, intention, and learned practice that makes a life recognizable; the network has no reliable way to capture that whole person.

A technique is not a spell button. It is a structured practice with a body, a purpose, and a stopping condition. The network can reveal structure, disagreements, and missing steps. The practitioner must still do the work.

## What a technique contains

Every usable technique has five parts, whether or not its teacher names them:

1. **Anchor:** the physical or sensory focus that starts the practice. It might be heel pressure, a particular smell, a thumb on a pulse, or the feeling of a train approaching.
2. **Beat:** an ordered action or recognition step. A sequence can be short or long, but each beat must have observable cues.
3. **Release:** the point at which attention ends and the practitioner returns to ordinary action. A technique without a release is unsafe.
4. **Boundary:** what the technique must not affect, such as a patient’s memories, a stranger’s privacy, a neighbor’s property, or the practitioner’s sense of identity.
5. **Aftertaste:** the temporary sensory, emotional, or physical residue left by the practice. A skilled teacher can reduce it; no technique removes it completely.

A printed card can record a shape, but it cannot carry the judgment required to apply it to a new body or situation.

## The share lifecycle

### 1. Offer

A practitioner offers a specific practice, not their whole self. The offer names the purpose, duration, people involved, and what may remain after the exchange. A person may offer to borrow a technique for one rescue, compare a practice for a class, or contribute a trace to a public lineage. Silence is not consent.

### 2. Agreement

Every participant makes a visible or otherwise unmistakable agreement. Proximity alone, a pre-signed workplace form, and a device being in range are not enough. A person can agree to one layer and refuse another. The three common permissions are:

- **Use:** the technique may be used during the named event.
- **Remember:** a faint echo may be retained after the event.
- **Derive:** later observers may compare the trace and form a new practice.

The default is use only. Emergency care may create a **care-link** when a person cannot answer, but the reason, duration, and later review must be visible to the people affected. A care-link is not permission for indefinite retention.

### 3. Exchange

The network carries observations: timing, pressure, attention, error, and boundary. It does not carry a full memory, private motive, or unchosen belief. A participant can feel the shape of another person’s practice as an aftertaste, especially when the practice is emotionally loaded. This is privacy leakage, not mind reading.

### 4. Synthesis

A temporary **shared line** appears when independent observations agree closely enough to be useful. Two observations can suggest a line. Three or more independent sources make it more stable. A single source can teach a technique directly, but it cannot establish a general network pattern by itself.

The network does not decide which source is correct. It can show a common shape, a conflict, or a missing beat. A false agreement can produce a false line when participants have shared the same teacher, device, pressure, or lie.

### 5. Withdrawal

A participant may withdraw from future use immediately. The network must stop sending the share and must not treat refusal as a failed technique. Withdrawal cannot erase a physical injury, a social consequence, or an echo already carried by another person. The moral work is to repair those consequences; the magical work is to respect the boundary.

## What can and cannot travel

| Can travel through a share | Cannot travel through a share |
| --- | --- |
| Observable technique beats and timing | Innate talent or bloodline power |
| Sensory cues and physical pressure | A private thought or hidden motive |
| Errors, uncertainty, and disagreement | A person’s entire autobiographical memory |
| A visible boundary and release condition | Ownership of a person or their future choices |
| A limited aftertaste and lineage trace | A guaranteed result or automatic mastery |
| A practice for a named purpose | Permission beyond the stated scope |

A person may become better at a technique through practice after learning its shape, but the network cannot make a body ready. Fear, disability, exhaustion, environment, and training still matter.

## Distance, relays, and privacy

- **Near links** are direct and clear. Two people touching or sharing a visible cue can exchange a short observation.
- **Exchange-room links** use relay plates and can reach across a building, clinic, or district. They are more stable but leave a consent record.
- **Long links** cross a city or region through trusted relays. They lose detail, require maintenance, and can expose the fact of a connection to a relay operator.
- **Remote operation** is possible only through established exchange rooms. It is never invisible, and it cannot reach a person who has not deliberately accepted a relay.

The network leaks the shape of a person through use. An observer may infer that someone is injured, frightened, highly skilled, or using an unfamiliar technique. Legal and social consequences follow even when the underlying content is not readable.

## Attention debt and failure

Every active thread consumes attention, sensory processing, and the ability to distinguish one person’s feeling from another. Rest, food, sleep, familiar physical objects, and ordinary practice reduce the debt. No rest action instantly clears a dangerous share.

### Low load

Temporary headache, trembling, a metallic taste, altered smell, or a brief inability to name an object. These symptoms usually pass after the practice ends and ordinary sensory grounding.

### Medium load

Borrowed emotion, phantom pain, repeated images, insomnia, or a motor skill that feels foreign for several hours. A person may be medically stable and still be unable to make safe decisions. This is why connection cannot be treated as a simple stamina bar.

### High load

Unmooring: the person cannot reliably tell which emotion, memory, or impulse belongs to them. They may repeat another person’s release cue, lose the ability to practice alone, or experience a permanent loss of a previously secure skill. Unmooring can be treated, but recovery may take months and may leave a lasting change.

### Social load

A share can expose a person’s health, employment, immigration status, testimony, or intimate skill. A team can win a rescue and still face lawsuits, lost wages, public suspicion, or family conflict. These costs are real progression gates, not optional flavor.

## Failure modes

- **False consensus:** several people repeat one source, so the network mistakes conformity for independent evidence.
- **Orphaned line:** a technique is retained after the person who gave the necessary boundary withdraws. The practice becomes unsafe to revise.
- **Cue lock:** a learner can perform only when a particular key-holder gives the missing cue. This is the usual result of a hinge.
- **Boundary bleed:** a practice intended for one body, place, or task begins altering unrelated people or objects.
- **Counterpoint paralysis:** too many legitimate disagreements make a person unable to choose. A network that respects difference can still fail in an emergency.
- **Relay capture:** a trusted relay quietly changes what it forwards, creating a false lineage.
- **Quiet coercion:** people are not connected through magic but are pressured to accept a share by a job, family, state, or crowd. The network cannot decide whether a person’s “yes” is socially free.

## Hinges and the Crown Clause

A **hinge** is an intentionally incomplete beat with a hidden completion route. It is not a new creature, a demon, or a magical infection. A hinge rewards a specific key-holder’s pattern and makes other completion routes unstable.

Hinges work in stages:

1. A learner receives a useful technique with one beat omitted.
2. The key-holder supplies the missing cue or instruction.
3. The learner succeeds and associates success with the key-holder.
4. Later attempts become less reliable without the key-holder.
5. The learner begins asking for permission, advice, or identity confirmation in situations that do not require it.

The hinge does not control every thought. It narrows practical agency by turning a successful practice into a social dependency. Its power depends on repetition, trust, and institutional cover.

The **Crown Clause** is the original hidden rule behind the seeded hinges. It says that when a civic node is silent during an approved emergency, the last civic sequence may be used as a provisional response. That emergency exception was hidden from the people whose bodies would carry it out. Iona Sorn added the clause during the Marrow Falls outage and later seeded variants into public techniques.

The **Crown Key** is the authority that can invoke the clause across linked civic systems. In physical form it is a convergence cabinet in the Crown Vault: many old return paths have been joined so that one approved response can travel through them at once. It cannot create a new technique, read a private mind, or solve a problem without a prepared pattern. It can make silence look like consent and make one response look like the only safe one. That is why it is a political weapon rather than an invincible weapon.

## Counterpoint

Counterpoint is the deliberate use of an independent difference to test a shared line. It can be another person’s observation, a physical repetition, a written record, or a practice done without the expected cue.

Counterpoint is not a truth detector. It can show that two sources disagree. It can prevent a false consensus from becoming permanent. It can also slow an emergency and cost time. A person who relies only on counterpoint may become unable to act with anyone.

Marek’s counterpoint scar began when, as a child, he was connected during a failed evacuation drill without a working return path. The experience did not give him a secret bloodline. It left his attention unusually sensitive to an unclosed route and gave him a responsibility to ask what a technique leaves out. He still needs practice, rest, and other people’s judgment.

## Marek’s stages

These are milestone descriptions, not a visible list of numbers. The System may name a milestone only when a scene has earned it.

### Stage 0 — Latent Counterpoint

Marek notices a missing or contradicted beat in a technique he is watching. He can survive one brief, willing share, but he cannot hold a useful link by himself. He becomes physically ill when a technique tries to close around an unstated assumption. A wrong counterpoint can mislead him as easily as a right one saves him.

### Stage 1 — First Knot

With one chosen partner, Marek can pool two observations and hold a temporary shared line. He can stabilize a practice he has personally attempted, but cannot invent a new one. The cost is shared aftertaste: his partner’s fatigue or fear can reach him, and his can reach them. If either person withdraws, the line collapses even if the technique is urgently needed.

### Stage 2 — Counterpoint

Marek can compare three or more independent lines, identify a missing beat, and pass a bounded observation through a relay. He can work with a small team, but every disagreement can produce static and decision paralysis. He must sometimes choose the less certain practice rather than wait for perfect agreement.

### Stage 3 — Field Weave

Marek can coordinate a trained group through a live sequence, distribute sensory load, and use a composite technique that no single person could perform. He is responsible for asking, for boundaries, and for stopping. The more people he coordinates, the more ordinary parts of his life can leak into the weave. A strong result can still be a moral failure if he has treated a team as equipment.

### Stage 4 — Civic Loom

Marek can help maintain a durable local exchange and a public technique library. He can stabilize a hospital, transit line, or neighborhood mesh across time rather than in one dramatic moment. This stage requires governance, maintenance, legal responsibility, and public trust. The network becomes visible to adversaries, and privacy losses affect people who never chose to join.

### Stage 5 — Open Weave

Marek can work across several local networks when each one grants its own consent. He no longer has a private privileged position: he can act only when enough independent people accept the plan. The cost is permanent reduced private capacity, recurring unmooring symptoms, and the loss of a stable identity boundary. He becomes more useful precisely by becoming less able to own the use.

### Final act — Unbinding

Marek can help dismantle the Crown Clause by distributing the Return Path rather than taking the Crown Key. The act permanently removes his high-level access and some of his oldest network echoes. He retains limited human-scale practice and a real life, but he cannot return to being the one person every node calls when the city needs a single answer.

## System language

The network interface is sparse, embodied, and sometimes wrong. It does not display level numbers, inventory lists, or secret objectives. A typical prompt is one short line:

> Two practices agree. One boundary is missing. Do you offer a share?

The System may add a practical warning:

> This link will carry feeling as well as technique. The partner can stop it.

It can refuse to answer when evidence is insufficient. Characters should not treat a quiet System as a moral verdict. The interface often becomes dangerous when a hidden hinge encourages it to present one source as the natural completion.

## Author guardrails

- Do not let a new technique solve an unrelated problem without a matching physical practice and cost.
- Do not let Marek access private memories, thoughts, or a person’s innate talent.
- Do not let a large network vote, act, or feel as one mind.
- Do not use a System panel more than once per chapter unless the scene specifically turns on several distinct decisions.
- Do not erase consent consequences with a convenient reset. Repair can be possible; erasure is not.
- Do not make a hinge’s influence universal. It needs a repeated practice, a trusted key, and a social situation that rewards obedience.
- Do not make the final institution frictionless. Public consent takes time, and people will leave it.
