# Chapter 630 — A Sunday, A Shop That Is Shut All Day And A Bar Put Across The Bottom Of Its Shutter At About Two Because There Was Nothing Else To Do With His Hands, A Closed Door On A Corner With A Woman Of About Thirty-Two Standing Behind The Glass And Not Opening Up, A Book Opened In A Room He Was Not In And Nine Days Old, A Covered Walkway On A Wide Road, And A Question That Took Him Longer Than About Nine Seconds

**A Sunday is a bench and nothing else. This one was a shutter that was down when he arrived at ten and had a bar across the bottom of it at two, four rooms off that service road from half past three until six, and a covered walkway on a wide road where nine people were sitting out of the weather and one of them asked him a question.**

---

**There is no shop in this city that opens on a Sunday and this one is the oldest of them and the least likely to change.** Eleven jobs went on the Monday after, all of them dated, and eleven people came over that counter, and none of that is on this page except that it will all happen and that the day sheet for the Sunday is blank in a way no other day sheet in that shop has ever been blank, and it was not left blank, it was never started.

**The heating in that flat went above its winter setting in the spring and it is above it now, four names on that one tenancy are paying the difference, and a cook is a fortnight of her own wages in the hole and has not been told.** Those four rooms are one thousand one hundred and thirty-three days, a hundred and sixty-one weeks and six days, four of them and one behind, and they are warm.

The card in the rail is one thousand one hundred and thirty-seven days, a hundred and sixty-two weeks and three days, four days past the room, and the rail holds two.

**The post at the end of that corridor is six hundred and eighty-one days, ninety-seven weeks and two days, and those four rooms are four hundred and fifty-two days older than that, which is a long time and is not a figure about either of them, and this entry has said so on every day of the last thirty and says it again on a Sunday, which is the day a person is most inclined to say it about something else.**

---

**He was in that shop from ten and the shutter was down the whole of it and nothing was collected and nothing was repaired and nothing was taken in, and four people walked past it and looked at it and two of them looked at him through the gap and one of them knocked.**

He did not open up. **He stood nine feet back from the door and said, through it, that it was a Sunday and there was no shop, and the man said *I know there's no shop* and went off, and that is the entire trade of a Sunday and there was two pounds of it.**

---

**He walked to a street he has the name of and no number on, on the off chance, at eleven, and he found the grocery by the look of it, and it was shut.**

It was shut the way a corner shop is shut, with the shutters half down and the light on behind them and a milk crate turned upside down on the step.

**The woman of about thirty-two was behind the glass at two feet from it with a cloth in her hand. She saw him. He stood in the road. Neither of them moved for eleven seconds and she did not open up and he did not go, and then she turned round and went into the back and he stood there for another two minutes and then walked off.**

**He had come to buy something. He has not got to this day what he was going to buy and has not worked it out, and there is no shop open on this day to buy it in.**

---

**At one he was back in that shop and the woman of about thirty-four was on her own and told him something before he had got the coat off.**

**"The woman from Saltmarket,"** she said, **"the one who types for the firm. She was in at eleven this morning with a sheet of paper with a thing written on it and she read it out loud to three of us in that shop and she was not showing off, she was just reading it out loud, and then she put it back in her bag."**

**"What did it say."**

**"I've been asking you that since you came in,"** said the woman of about thirty-four, **"because I have not written it down. I was standing there with a meter in my hand. It was about a tray."**

---

**He did not believe her for two hours. He is not a man who disbelieves people and he did not disbelieve that woman; he disbelieved that the sentence was in the book, and the reason is nine seconds long and it is that a line in that book is for a thing a person has not already been told, and he had been the one who told her, at a counter, at half past three, in front of four people, about a bar on the outside of a door.**

**He worked it out at three o'clock in a shop with the shutter down and nobody in it, standing at a bench, and what he worked out is that the woman of about thirty-two had not known what a return was, and had said so, in nine seconds, in front of eleven people, on a Wednesday, and the woman of about sixty had put that into a book, and four people in that room had looked at the table, and not one of them knew where it had come from, and he was not in the room.**

**He was not a good at the moment. He has not been able to explain to anybody since what was wrong with it, and the wrongness is not that he made a woman say something. It is that eleven people in a room have a sentence and one man in a shop knows where the sentence started, and that is a room with him in it, and he built it in nine seconds at a counter without deciding to.**

---

**The bar went across the bottom of the shutter at two.**

That is the whole of it and it is in its own words and it is not the end of a working day. **On a working evening the bar across the bottom of that shutter means the day is over. On a Sunday the day went by itself four hours earlier and he put the bar across anyway, because the shutter is on a wheel and his hands were empty and there is nothing else to do with your hands in a shop on a Sunday in this city.** Nobody was inside it. Nobody outside it looked. The bar is still across it and it will be across it on Monday morning and the woman of about thirty-four will take it off when she comes in and will not say anything about it and neither will he.

---

**Through the middle of it he did not open the shop and did not go out, and he was in a room with the shutter down and a cage with a man in it facing a wall, and there is nowhere in this city to go on a Sunday afternoon if you are not the kind of person who sits in an arcade.**

**He made a list. It was on the back of the docket in the fourth pocket, in pencil, in his own hand, and it was nine lines of the names of nine rooms in four districts and two towns, and it took him four minutes, and it is the first thing he has written in a month that is a list of rooms, and he looked at it for a long time and then he wrote, underneath, in the same pencil, *ask them first* — which is not an address and is not a number and is a note to himself about a thing he has to do on a Monday, and it went into the fourth pocket with four pieces of paper that are not his and a docket that has four words on the back of it, and that pocket is not a list and has never been a list and he knows exactly what it is.**

**The four rooms off that service road from half past three, and four people in for the heat on a Sunday afternoon, which is more than come on a working evening, and nobody was asked anything by anybody.** The man of about fifty-one was against that north wall. The chair nearest the door was taken at half past three by a woman of about thirty and held until six, and nobody spoke to her, nobody asked her anything, nobody has ever said sorry to her for any part of it, and the page with her name at the top of it, the eighth of eight, in a ring binder, on a shelf in the back, was shut in it and stayed shut.

---

**And at four, under a covered walkway on a wide road, nine people were sitting down because of the weather, and one of them stood up.**

**"You're the one with the cabinet."**

**"I read two lines off a card standing next to it."**

**"Right,"** said the man of about thirty-four, who works in a kitchen, and then asked the question, and it was a fair question and it took him four seconds to ask. **"Which of the nine do I ask."**

Marek did not say it in nine seconds. **He has said things in nine seconds to about four hundred and fifty people in this city in four weeks, and nine seconds has been the unit of everything he has done since the spring, and he stood under a covered walkway on a wide road and there was nothing in his mouth for eleven seconds, and then what came out was not an answer.**

**"Which nine."**

**"The nine with the ends on them. The one at the water office, the one at the school, the depot, the house with the trees, the kitchen, the one in Saltmarket, the school in the town, the store with the cold room, and the one with no name on the door."** The man of about thirty-four said it in nine seconds, in order, without looking anything up. **"I've got all nine. Somebody told me all nine on Friday and I have written them on the back of my hand and one of them is in a town and I have to be in a town on Friday."**

**Somebody had told him all nine. It was not Marek, and the man of about thirty-four said afterwards that it was a man with a clipboard at a gate, and Marek said **"about nine hundred and a half"** out loud to nobody, on a Sunday, four days afterwards, and did not finish the sentence.**

**"So which of the nine do I ask."**

---

**And he stood there, and the answer he has been carrying since ten o'clock that morning is that he does not know and that the only person who could have told him stopped in a second district in the spring, and that is a sentence a woman of about sixty-eight uses about herself four times a year, and he is not going to say it to a stranger under a walkway on a Sunday because it is the correct answer and it is about him and not about the man.**

**What he said instead, at four, was: "That's a real question and I don't have a nine-second answer to it, and I'd rather say that than give you one you'll use."**

**"That's a no answer,"** said the man of about thirty-four.

**"It's a Thursday answer,"** said Marek, **"and I have got nine of them in me and no time today, and what I am going to do with it is go and ask all nine of them the same thing, and the same thing is going to be whether they'd put their returns through one man for a fortnight, and when I've asked them all nine you'll know, and so will I, and so will about four hundred people in this city, and I am telling you now so that you can put it down that I told you first."**

He has not asked any of the nine. **It is a Monday's job and it is a fortnight's work and it is the largest thing he has ever said out loud in this city, and he said it to a man of about thirty-four under a covered walkway on a Sunday afternoon and it reached exactly one person and the man's hand with the nine written on it went into his pocket and he said *right* and sat down again.**

**Nobody thanked anybody. Nobody was taught, shown, assessed or helped. The register of correct things that changed nothing is closed at two and a Sunday does not move it, and the reason is the plainest of the ten, which is that a man who does not have an answer and says so in four seconds has done the only thing on that register that has ever been available to him, and the register was not built for it and it stays at two.** The two on it are still the same woman of about fifty-two in the first of the four towns and nothing on a Sunday improved either of them.

**And the figures, once, on the last day of this stretch of days.** He is twenty-two, and on this tenth day he ends having exactly what he started it with, and one sentence he has said out loud to one man under a covered walkway. A rail of two with the newer of them one thousand one hundred and thirty-seven days in it, unturned. Two nails and nineteen lines and no twentieth. Nine hand copies of a page front, six hundred and ninety-nine days, ninety-nine weeks and six days, eight unfinished, the first row nobody disagrees about still not found, nothing laid against anything today and nothing laid against anything for thirty days. The cast-iron thing at the end of that passage, six hundred and eighty-one days. The separation, five hundred and thirteen days, seventy-three weeks and two days, shut in a form five weeks ago, subtracted every day since, no shorter, nothing put back into it. That drawer under the bench is still shut with four sheets inside it and nobody has looked at the fourth. The four words that name the public body are not written anywhere on this page. Seven articles get spoken of in what follows and not one pair of them is ever brought face to face: a length of cloth in a woman's hand, a card standing on end, paper that came back to a room, a form with one box left empty, a job docket with nine places on its reverse, a hand copy of the front of a page, and a bar of iron with four slots.
