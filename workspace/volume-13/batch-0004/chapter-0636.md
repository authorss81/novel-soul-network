# Chapter 636 — A Wednesday, A Depot Gate Standing Open Onto A Yard, A Wheel On A Chain By That Gate That Has Not Moved Since The Spring Because Somebody Oiled It Wrong, A Woman Of About Forty-One Doing Sums Out Loud, A Man Of About Thirty-Eight Who Keeps A Warehouse Arriving With A Meeting In His Mouth, And A Ninth Line On A Van Door That Nobody In This City Has Been Asked To Own

**Wednesday: a bench until two, a counter until about six, and four rooms off that service road after that. This one is a yard behind a depot, a gate standing up on a chain, a woman doing sums out loud at a clipboard, and a question about how many stops a van has got.**

---

Eleven jobs with a date against each and eleven people over the counter, the last of them let in at about ten past four. Bench, counter, the depot, counter, service road, and the counter not handed over all day.

Those four rooms off that service road are one thousand one hundred and forty-three days old, a hundred and sixty-three weeks and two days, four of them and one behind, and they are warm. **The card standing on end in the rail is one thousand one hundred and forty-seven days, a hundred and sixty-three weeks and six days, four days on from them, and the rail takes two and has taken two for seven weeks.**

The post at the end of that corridor is six hundred and ninety-one days, ninety-eight weeks and five days. The ninth line on the board of two nails is eight hundred and thirty-nine days, a hundred and nineteen weeks and six days. **Neither of those is a day anything happened to a heating in this city and this entry says so on a Wednesday for the reason it says it on every other day, which is that a Wednesday is when a person is most inclined to say a number about something else.**

---

**The wheel is the first thing he saw and it is not a thing anybody in that yard looks at.**

It is a counting wheel on a chain, the kind that hangs beside a gate so that a driver can put a peg through and turn it, and it is a real object in this city and about four of them exist and none of them works. **This one has been sitting on the same number since the spring because the pawl had gone stiff and a man in orange overalls put oil on it in nine seconds on a morning in that season and has not been asked about it since, and the figure on the wheel is now a figure of a Friday in the spring and not a figure of this week.**

He looked at it for about four seconds and then went past it, and he has not been able to explain to anybody since why he looked at it, and the reason is that it is a machine in a city of four hundred and fifty people who could each say one true thing, **and it is the only one of the four hundred and fifty that has been given a job of counting and has failed at it silently and in public for about four months.**

---

**The depot woman had the gate up and a clipboard and she was doing it out loud, which she does, and which nobody has ever asked her to stop.**

"There," she said, before he was in the yard. "**Nine lines on that door and eight of them struck. I've been watching that door go past this gate for about four years and I have never once been in the cab, and I can tell you that the ninth has not been struck and I have been watching it since Friday and it has not been struck once, and that is either because a man has not been there or because a man has been there and did not strike it.**"

"I asked eight."

"You asked eight," she agreed, and put the clipboard down on the tailgate of something, "**and the eight are on a door in grease pencil and the ninth is the only one of the nine that is in a town you cannot get to and back from in a day.**"

---

**And she had it in four seconds, which is what she does, and what she had was not the ninth room.**

It was the number. She had carried a figure since the Thursday of week 230 and it was hers and it had never been checked, and she now had the second half of it in her hand and the two halves did not sit together.

"**About nine hundred a year across nine of you. I gave you that and I asked for somebody to have it.**" She wrote nothing down, which she never does. "**And there's four crates under a bench in a shop in Lattice Ward, and the crates came off a van that does two runs a week, and the van makes the round in about four hours if it does not stop, and if it does stop it is a day.**"

"That's right."

"**Then four hundred kilos and nine hundred a year is not a number anybody has ever put in the same sentence, and I have been saying that number for about four years and I have never once had it met by another number, and now I have.**" She turned the clipboard round and there was nothing on the back of it, and she had turned it round on purpose, and it was the first time in about four years that anybody in this city had turned a pad round at him and the second. "**So which is it. Is it nine hundred or is it four crates, because one of those is a plan and the other one is a van, and I do not run a van.**"

---

**He said he did not know, in four seconds, and that is the second time in nine days he has said that sentence to her and the reason is the same reason.**

"I don't know how many sheets come through that gate and I never have."

"**You never have,**" said the woman of about forty-one, "**and the arrangement you have built this fortnight is built out of my not knowing, and that was fine on the Thursday and it is not fine today, because a van has a ninth line on the door and the ninth line is not a number and a stop.**"

---

**And then she said the thing, and it took her about nine seconds, and it is the whole of this entry.**

"**Take the ninth off the door.**"

"Why."

"**Because the day that van comes back from that stop with a crate on it, that is a line in my book, and a line in my book is a stop the depot accounts for, and a stop the depot accounts for is a stop somebody can be found at, and there has been a room on the end of that road that nobody in this city has been to for about four years and I know that because I watch the road.**"

Nobody in that yard said anything for about four seconds. **What she had said, in one breath, was that the only way the ninth stops being a line the depot answers for is if it is taken off the van, and the reason it has not been taken off the van is that one man does not want to go four days of bus travel there and back and stand in front of a door, and both of those are correct and the first one stops the paper and the second one is about him.**

"You want me to take it off," he said.

"**I want the paper to go where it went before Tuesday, and I do not care in the least what you do with your Thursdays.**"

---

**A man of about thirty-eight who keeps a warehouse had been at the gate with a clipboard of his own and had been there long enough to hear all of it.**

He came in and put his hand on the van's nearside door, which is a thing a man does when he wants to look at a list without asking to look at a list, and he read the nine lines and the eight strikes, and he did not ask who the ninth was, and Marek has noticed that about him twice now and has not worked out what it is.

"It's the same door," said the man of about thirty-eight.

"It's the same door."

"**I've been in a warehouse for nineteen years and I can tell you exactly what happens at about four months.**" He put his hand flat on the door. "**Right now it's a fortnight and a van and eight of you are right. At four months it's a rota, and the rota has a name on it, and the name is the man who holds the van, and the name is yours, and after that nobody in any of those nine buildings has a room any more, they have a man.**"

"That's a meeting with a van instead of a room."

"That's not what I meant," said the man of about thirty-eight, **"and I said it was a meeting four months ago in a different room and you said it wasn't the same thing, and you were wrong then and you are wrong now, and I have never once said so out loud because saying it out loud makes it a thing I did, and I did not do it, I only watched it.**"

---

**What he gave up in that yard, in about four seconds, and for no reason he has been able to put to anybody, was the right to take the ninth off.**

He had it. **He could have walked to that van and put his thumb over the grease pencil and rubbed the ninth line out in about nine seconds and the depot woman would have said *right* and the man of about thirty-eight would have said *see* and the whole of it would have been over before eleven o'clock and the ninth would have gone back to being a room nobody had reached.** He did not do it, and the reason he has never given anybody is that a man who rubs out a stop because a woman at a gate said it was about to happen is a man who decides other people's rooms for a living, and he has spent four months refusing to be that, and there was a moment in a yard when the not-doing cost more than the doing would have.

**He said nothing at all, and the woman of about forty-one said nothing at all, and the man of about thirty-eight took his hand off the door, and a woman of about twenty-three on the gate node her head once and did not write anything, and that was the end of it.**

---

---

**Back at the counter at about half past three the woman of about thirty-four asked him a question she had clearly been holding since eleven and did not put it the way she usually puts things.**

"You've been to the depot twice this week."

"Yes."

"**Was the number you got on the Thursday the same as the number she had?**"

"She gave it to me."

"That's not what I asked."

"It is exactly what you asked," he said, "and the answer is no, because I did not tell her the number."

**She looked at him for about two seconds and then said, "That's the first thing in about four months you have done that isn't a refusal," and went back to the meter, and neither of them said anything else about it, and he has not worked out whether it was a compliment.**

---

**And at about five, a woman of about twenty-nine came in with a carrier bag and wanted to send a thing back and it had been round twice, and the two of them established in about nine seconds that the room on it was a room and not a number, and that it was let to a man whose name she had, and that the man had a landlord and the landlord had a room with a bar on the outside of its door and a light on a timer.**

"I've got the name of the man who lets it," she said.

"Then you've got it."

"**Yes,**" said the woman of about twenty-nine, **"and about four hundred other people in this city have got a room and not the name, and I have wanted to know what to do with it for about four years and I have just realised that knowing a name and not knowing what to do with it is exactly the same as not knowing a name, and I'd never thought of that before, so thank you for your time.**"

**She said it as though she had been let down and he had been the man who let her down and neither of them was in the wrong, and she went out, and it is the fourth thing he has said in nine days that a person has repeated to somebody else.**

---

*One thousand one hundred and forty-three and one thousand one hundred and forty-seven are four days apart, and both of them land in the same week, and a depot gate in a second district is not a figure in either of those and this entry has not joined them.*

*639. Wednesday of week 231, at ten. Eleven dated jobs, eleven people at that counter, the last let in at about ten past four, and the counter never handed over. A depot with its gate up on a chain and a wheel on that chain reading the same figure it read in the spring. A clipboard turned round on a tailgate with nothing on the back of it. Nine lines in grease pencil on a nearside door, eight struck, the ninth not struck and watched for three days. A man of about thirty-eight with a warehouse and a meeting. A request to take a stop off a van, refused by saying nothing. A counter at half past three, and a carrier bag at five.*

*Conditions and docket.* **The Wednesday carried eleven jobs each with a date against it and eleven callers, none of them let in after about ten past four, and the whole of the counter sat in one pair of hands, which has been true on eleven of the last thirteen working days.** Behind that counter: a working bench at the window; a cage whose occupant kept his face to the wall all day; the roller shutter and the wheel it runs down on; a tool roll with a fourth pocket in it; a job list gone soft inside a clear pocket; a shelf where completed work stands until somebody comes for it; a rail of two. Callers: eleven. Refusals: four, and two of them were the same person on two occasions eleven weeks apart who had asked to be told whether a room on a sheet is the room the sheet says it is. Work: eleven, four declines, nothing escalated, nothing handed back. Charge: nine pounds and then fourteen, nothing out, and the difference is a job that came apart at the bench and was not the same job by the time it went back together. The heating in that flat: above the winter's setting for a sixth month, four names on that one tenancy making up the difference between them without ever having agreed to, and a cook about a fortnight of her own wages in the hole that nobody on that street has mentioned to her. Those rooms: one thousand one hundred and forty-three days, a hundred and sixty-three weeks and two days, four of them and one behind, warm. Rail card: one thousand one hundred and forty-seven days, a hundred and sixty-three weeks and six days, four days past the room, rail of two. The board on two nails, in no order anybody reads it in: nineteenth, eight hundred and thirty-nine, a hundred and nineteen weeks and six days; sixteenth, nine hundred and thirty-three, a hundred and thirty-three weeks and two days; thirteenth, one thousand and fourteen, a hundred and forty-four weeks and six days. The man of about fifty-one against that north wall: seven hundred and forty-nine days, one hundred and seven weeks to the day, and it is the only figure in this entry that lands on a whole week. The ask: eight hundred and thirty-three days, a hundred and nineteen weeks to the day. The post at the corridor end: six hundred and ninety-one days, ninety-eight weeks and five days. The nine hand copies: seven hundred and nine days, a hundred and one weeks and two days, eight of the nine unfinished, the first row in which nobody has yet disagreed still not found, and not one of the nine laid against another on this or any other day this year. **The hold on nine crates in a yard in the second of the four towns: seven hundred and seventy-six days, a hundred and ten weeks and six days.** The fifteenth line: nine hundred and fifty-eight, a hundred and thirty-six weeks to the day. The separation: five hundred and twenty-three days, seventy-four weeks and five days, ended, walked, and printed on a Wednesday as it is on every other day this fortnight runs. Added to that board: nothing, and no twentieth nail. The Exchange: no sitting on this day, no count given in this city or any other, and this file does not say what a sitting does. The size of the load: not taken by anybody, and the only figure anybody keeps is in pencil on the end of a stirrer in a garage and is not printed here.

*Nine lines, eight struck, and a gate that has watched them go past for about four years.* **A woman of about forty-one at a bus depot set two figures side by side out loud in a yard — a figure of about nine hundred a year across nine rooms that she handed a man on a Thursday and has never had checked, against four crates under a bench in a shop in this city — and said that four hundred kilos and nine hundred a year had never before appeared in one sentence. Then she said the whole of it in about nine seconds: take the ninth off the door, because the day a crate comes back off that stop it becomes a line in a depot book, and a line in a depot book is a stop this place answers for, and a stop the depot answers for is a stop a person can be found at, and there has been a room at the end of that road that nobody in this city has been to for about four years. She said she did not care in the least what he did with his Thursdays. A man of about thirty-eight who keeps a warehouse laid his hand flat on that nearside door and said that in about four months this is a rota, and the rota has a name on it, and the name is the man who holds the van, and that he had called the answer a meeting four months ago in a different room, had been wrong then, and had never said so aloud because saying it aloud would make it a thing he did. Marek did not take the ninth off. He could have, in about nine seconds, and did not, and the file says why. Nobody agreed with anybody; about two of the four people in that yard were on her side and neither said so.**

*What the day did, and what it did not touch.* **A stop was asked to be taken off a van and was not taken off, and the reason it was not taken off is about him and not about the paper, and he said nothing at all in about four seconds, which was the correct thing to do and the most expensive silence anybody has let stand in a yard this year.** Nobody thanked anybody. Nobody was taught, shown, assessed or helped, and a man of about thirty-eight was offered an argument for free and was not thanked for the other side of it either. The register of correct acts with no consequence is closed at two, and refusing a woman who is right in about four seconds is not a third instance, because a stop is still on a door in a town four days of bus travel there and back and the fortnight is not going to stop, and neither of the two entries that has sat on that register since it was opened about four years ago has been touched by a Wednesday. From about half past six the seat by the door of the fourth of those four rooms was taken by a woman of about thirty and she was still in it at about nine, and no one in that building put anything to her in three and a half hours — no question, no sheet, nothing to carry, and no apology of any kind, and none is owed to her by anybody there, least of all by the man who made the room. Her page is the eighth of eight in a ring binder at the back of a shelf, and it has stayed closed on every one of the ten days this stretch of days runs. That drawer under the bench was not opened and holds the same four sheets, of which the fourth has never been looked at. A sheet handed back over a counter, a card standing on end in a rail, a form carrying one box that nothing has ever been written in, a docket with nine rooms pencilled along its reverse, a hand copy of a page front, and a bar of iron with four slots in it are six separate things and no sentence here lays one of them beside another. Sixty-two lines in the book and seventy-three in the tin, lid down all day, neither opened, and no count announced anywhere, because no sitting fell on this Wednesday and this file does not invent one.
