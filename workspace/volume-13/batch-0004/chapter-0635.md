# Chapter 635 — A Monday, A Man Of About Forty-One Named Ray Topping Who Has Driven The Same Van For The Same Firm For About Nine Years, Four Crates In The Back Of It, And A Load Nobody In This City Has Counted And That One Man Counts Every Friday In A Garage With The Door Open

**A Monday is a bench until two, a counter until about six, and four rooms off that service road after. This one is a garage with the door open at about nine, a name given out loud for the first time in about four months, and four crates that are not a number anybody has ever checked.**

---

Eleven jobs dated and eleven people over the counter on the Monday, the last of them at about ten past five, and the shutter down at ten and up at half past nine. Bench, counter, garage, counter, service road.

Four of those rooms are one thousand one hundred and forty-one days, a hundred and sixty-three weeks to the day, and the card in the rail is four days on from them, one thousand one hundred and forty-five. **The twentieth line on that board was never begun and has never been contemplated and there is no nail for it.**

---

**He got the name on the Monday morning at about nine, in a garage, from a woman of about sixty-three who runs the office end of it, and she gave it to him the way people in this city give names, which is as a correction to a thing he had already decided.**

"You've been calling him the van man."

"That's what I've been calling him."

"**His name is Ray Topping.**" She wrote it on a pad and turned the pad round, which nobody in this city has ever done to Marek and which he has thought about maybe eleven times since. **"And he's been with us nine years and he's the only one of our four who'd do the Tuesday run and I've never once had to ask him, and I'm telling you that because you're about to find out and I'd rather you found out from me.**"

---

**Ray Topping was in the back of the van with a cage in it and he came out of it the way a man comes out of a space that is too small, sideways, and he was about forty-one and he had a piece of foam pipe lagging on the inside of his left arm and about nine years of a van behind him and no particular expression at all.**

"You've asked the nine."

"I've asked eight."

"You've asked eight and one of the eight is a cold room in a town and the other seven are about a bus ride from where you're standing." He shut the back door. **"I've been counting that list since the Friday. Nine is not a route. Nine is a man with a van and nine stops, and you're on about it, and I'm the route, and I've been the route for about nine years and I don't want anything said about me at all.**"

---

**Nobody said anything about him. He asked for what he needed, which was a place to put about nine hundred returns a year for a fortnight, and Marek told him the shop, and Ray Topping looked at the shop for about four seconds and said the one sentence that made the whole of the next three weeks work.**

"You've got a back room."

"I've got a bench and a shelf and a cage with a man in it."

"That's not what I asked." Ray Topping put his thumb against the door. **"How deep is the back room and is there a floor that isn't concrete. Because if it's concrete I'll put the crates on boards and if it's not I'll put them straight down and either way they go under the bench and nobody sees them and nobody comes in and asks what they are.**"

---

**He gave Marek the floor. It is lino, laid about nine years ago, soft at one corner, and a man of about forty-one looked at it for about four seconds and said, "**That's about four weeks, that, if nobody stands on it,**" and Marek said, "Nobody's going to stand on it," and the man of about forty-one said, "**Everybody stands on it. That's how a room works.**"**

---

**The first four crates came in at about two on the Monday afternoon and went under the bench in about nine minutes, and the count of them is four, and the number of sheets in them is a number one man in this city has begun to keep.**

He keeps it on a tally on the end of a paint stirrer in the garage, in pencil, and he rubs the last entry out every Friday and starts again, and he is the only person in this city who has ever written a number of returns down, and he does it in a garage with the door open because the light in there is bad after four o'clock and he does not like doing it in the dark.

"I don't know what it's for," he said, at about half past three, to nobody, with the stirrer in his hand. **"You never said. You said a fortnight. I'm doing a fortnight. I've never once asked what a fortnight of this is for, because you didn't tell me and I didn't ask, and that's between us and it's how I get to sleep.**"

"Would you sleep if I had told you?"

"**No,**" said Ray Topping, "**and I'd have asked four more questions, and I wouldn't have got on the van on the Monday of the spring when the rear axle went and I did it myself with a bar and a jack from half past six in the morning until ten at night in a yard with nobody in it, and there was nobody to ask, and that's the best week I've had in nine years.**"

---

**What he said next is the reason this works and it is nine seconds long and he said it to a man of twenty-two who did not deserve it and both of them knew it.**

"You'll be the one everything comes to."

"That's the idea."

"**Then you'll be the one everything gets sent back from, and when that starts — and it starts about three weeks in, it always starts about three weeks in — somebody's going to stand in that shop and shout at you about a room they can't get into, and you won't be able to help them, and you'll be the man in the wrong city for the whole of it.**"

---

**And Marek said, "How would you know," and Ray Topping said, "**Because I have been the man in the wrong city for nine years, and it is not a complaint, and I would do it again, and I have got a van.**"**

**Nobody thanked him. He is not on a list, he is given control of nothing, he is not in the arrangement in any way that could be described as a role, and there is no form on which his name appears. He is a man in a garage with a tally on a paint stirrer and a piece of lagging on his arm.**

---

---

**The woman of about sixty-three kept the office end of that garage and had kept it for about nine years and had a drawer of carbon books in it going back further than anybody in the building, and Marek looked at the drawer and she closed it, and neither of them said one word about it, and it is the closest anybody came all day to giving him a count of anything.**

**Ray Topping came back in at about half past four to move a crate that was in the wrong place and found him standing in the middle of his own back room doing nothing at all.**

"You're stood on the soft bit."

"I'm stood on the soft bit."

"**You can't stand there for three weeks,**" he said. **"If you're stood here you're on it. Go on. There's a bit by the door that takes a boot.**"

---

**Marek stood by the door with a boot under him for about an hour and watched a man of about forty-one move four crates about in a back room and say nothing for fifty minutes, and at some point in the middle of it he said out loud, without any preamble at all, "Why are you doing this."**

Ray Topping did not look up.

"Because it's a Tuesday and I get paid for a Tuesday and there were four crates in a shop in Lattice Ward that I was not being paid for, and now there are four crates in a shop in Lattice Ward that I am." He moved a crate a half inch. **"That's the whole reason. There's not another reason. Don't go looking for one, because if there's one underneath this then it's a favour and a favour ends and a job doesn't, and I would rather it was a job.**"

---

**And then he stopped, and wiped his hands on a rag, and said the thing that is the only reason the next three weeks happen at all, and he said it to a spool of lagging on the wall and not to a person.**

"You'll want to be careful who you tell it's working."

"Why."

"**Because it works on about four people in this city who all know each other, and the second week somebody who isn't one of them finds out, and the third week it's a thing that happened, and by the fourth week a person can't get in and nobody can say why and they've all got a reason and every one of the reasons is a room somebody's already sent something to.**" He put the rag down. "I did a run like this once. Not this. About nine years ago. It went fine for five weeks and then it was a policy and I'd not been asked to make it one."

"What happened to it."

"**It's still going,**" said Ray Topping. **"That's what happened to it. That's the whole thing that happens to it.**"

---

**He left at about six and Marek shut the shop and put the bar across the bottom of the shutter, which he has done at about ten every working day since the spring and which is not a thing he decided to do and has never been a thing he could explain if anybody asked him.**

**He has been asked about it once, by a woman of about thirty-four, and what he told her was that a shutter that is only half down is a shutter that is thinking about it.**

---

---

**The woman of about thirty-four had been in that back room once, in the spring, with a meter, and she came in at about a quarter to five on the Monday because she had a thing she wanted him to hold and then she saw the crates and stopped in the doorway.**

"That's four."

"That's four."

**She looked at them for about four seconds and she did not go in, and what she said was not about the crates at all and is the only thing she said in this entry.**

"You've made a room."

"No."

"**You've made a room in a shop and there's a man whose job it is to fill it, and in about a month one person in this city will be able to get into it whenever they want, and that is the exact opposite of what you said you were doing, and you said it to me on a Sunday under a covered walkway.**"

---

**He did not answer her and she was right and she had worked it out in about four seconds standing in a doorway, and he has not been able to get round that since, and the reason it is on this page and not on a later one is that she said it to him at about a quarter to five on a Monday and then went back to the counter and served two people and did not raise it again for eleven days.**

**What he gave up in that doorway, in about four seconds and for nothing at all, was the sentence he had told himself at half past three in that back room, which is that this was a shelf and not a room. It was a room. It has been a room since the crates went under the bench at about two o'clock this afternoon and the lino is soft in one corner and everybody stands on it.**

---

---

**He counted the crates himself on the Monday night, at about seven, with the shutter down and the bar across the bottom of it, and it took him about four minutes, and there are four of them and the number of sheets in them is not four.**

Ray Topping's figure for the Monday, from the end of a paint stirrer, is on a scrap of paper in Marek's coat pocket, and it is a number with no units on it, and it is the only number anybody in this city is keeping about any of this, and it will be rubbed out on Friday.

**He had a pencil out to write it on the docket and then he did not, for the reason he gave the woman of about thirty-four at about a quarter to five and has not repeated since: that a number he is carrying is a number he will have to be able to give somebody in about a month, and the sentence he will have to use then is one a woman of about sixty-eight uses about herself about four times a year.**

He put the scrap back in the coat. He has still got it. It is in the fourth pocket with four pieces of paper that are not his, and the fourth pocket has never been a list of anything and is not one now.

---

*One thousand one hundred and forty-one is a whole number of weeks and one thousand one hundred and forty-five is four days past it, and those two numbers have been the same shape every day of this year and a man of about thirty-four in a kitchen is the only person in this city who has ever noticed that they have.*

*Nine minutes to put four crates under a bench, and a name given out loud once.* **A woman of about sixty-three in a garage office turned a pad round, which nobody in this city has ever done to him, and gave him a name: Ray Topping, about forty-one, the same van, the same firm, about nine years, the only one of four drivers who will take the Tuesday run, and she said all of that before he could find it out for himself and would not be thanked for it.** Ray Topping came out of the back of the van sideways, looked at a back room for about four seconds, asked how deep it was and whether the floor was concrete, was told lino laid about nine years ago and soft at one corner, said that was about four weeks if nobody stood on it, and was told nobody was going to stand on it, and said that everybody stands on it because that is how a room works. Four crates went under the bench at about two in about nine minutes. He said two more things, each in about nine seconds: that Marek would be the one everything comes to, and that about three weeks in somebody would stand in that shop and shout at him about a room they could not get into, and he would be the man in the wrong city for all of it. *What the day did, and what it did not touch.* **A man of about forty-one said his own name out loud once, in a garage, on a Monday morning, and did not have to and gained nothing by it, and a number about four hundred and fifty people in this city could each say one true thing about is now the only number about any of it that anybody is writing down, and he is doing it in pencil in a garage because of the light.** Nobody thanked anybody. Nobody was taught, shown, assessed or helped, and one man was refused a place under a bench for a week and left his crate by the door. The register of correct acts with no consequence is closed at two, and a man putting four crates under a bench in nine minutes is not a third instance, because everything in them is somewhere it was not, and what is on that register has not moved in about four years and both its instances belong to one woman of about fifty-two in the first of the four towns. She took the chair nearest the door at about half past six and was still in it at about nine. Nobody spoke to her, nobody put anything to her, and nobody in this city has ever said sorry to her for any part of that. Her page is the eighth of eight in a ring binder on a back shelf and it stayed shut today.
**Nothing was opened: not that binder, not a drawer under a bench with four sheets in it, of which the fourth is still unread by anybody, not a book on sixty-two lines, and not a tin on seventy-three. There was no sitting on this Monday, so this file has nothing to say about what one does.**
