# Chapter 632 — A Wednesday, A Street With Trees On One Side Of It And Nine Flats On The Other, A Second Room Asked In A Corridor And Said To In Four Seconds, A Plastic Sleeve In The Inside Pocket Of A Coat That Has Been There For About Four Years And Has Never Been On A Board Anywhere, And A Question Asked Of A Man Of About Thirty-Eight That Was Not On The List And Ought Not To Have Been Asked

**A Wednesday is a bench until two and a counter until about six. This one is a corridor in a building of about nine flats with trees on one side of the street, a man of about thirty-eight who keeps the rota for it, and nine seconds in which a sleeve came out of a coat.**

---

Eleven jobs dated and eleven people over the counter on the Wednesday, the last of them at about ten past five. Bench, counter, that street, service road, and the counter handed over at about four because there was a queue of five.

Those four rooms off that service road are one thousand one hundred and thirty-six days, a hundred and sixty-two weeks and two days, and warm. **The card in the rail is four days past them, one thousand one hundred and forty days, and the rail holds two and has held two for six weeks.**

The sixteenth line on the two nails is nine hundred and twenty-six days, a hundred and thirty-two weeks and two days, and the eighteenth is eight hundred and fifty-four days, a hundred and twenty-two weeks to the day. **Those two are not a day anything happened to the heating in this city. They are two numbers that a room has been subtracted from twice and this entry does not join either of them to anything.**

---

**He had four of the nine in his head and none of them on paper, because on Monday night he took the docket out of the fourth pocket and read the nine lines off it and then put it back, and that was the whole of what he did with it.**

He went to the flats on the Wednesday because the flats were on the way and because the water office had said *come back on Wednesday* on a Monday, and he would have gone past them anyway, and he has noticed twice this month that the reason he gives a person for doing a thing is about half of why he is doing it.

---

**There are nine flats and a stair and a board on the wall by the door with names on it and no numbers, and the man who keeps the rota for that building was on the half-landing with a folder under his arm when Marek came up, and he did not ask who was at the door.**

"You're the one with the nine," he said.

"**Which nine.**"

"Don't do that, it's rude." He shifted the folder. **"The nine with the ends on them. Somebody had a card up in a cellar and there's nine rooms on it and you're going round them."**

---

**He had told nobody. He told one man under a covered walkway on a Sunday about a fortnight ago, and that man wrote all nine on the back of his hand, and a man with a clipboard at a gate had already had them before that, and that is three routes and none of them is Marek and all three of them are true, and he has not spent a second working out how that happened because there is no second to spend.**

"How long have you known."

"Since the Friday." The man of about thirty-eight went up two steps without hurrying. **"A woman at a gate with a clipboard, and she read them out, and she said don't write these down, and I wrote them down."**

"I believe that."

"Do you." He said it flat and it was not a challenge. **"Right. What do you want."**

---

**The same question, the same words, and by the third room he had it down to about forty seconds and he still had to stop himself explaining the first half of it.**

"Would you put your returns through one man for a fortnight. One place, so an answer's in one room instead of nine. A fortnight, and then it stops whether anybody's happy or not."

"That's what I thought you were going to say."

"Yes or no. Nine seconds is plenty."

**The man of about thirty-eight said no. He said it in about four seconds and it was a clean no and there was nothing wrong with it.**

---

**No was the third one and it is the one Marek will still be thinking about at the end of this fortnight, and what the man of about thirty-eight gave him instead was a question.**

"Who else has said yes."

"I can't tell you that."

"You can if you want to. Not now. In a week, when it's a number."

"**It's a number in about a fortnight**," said the man of about thirty-eight, **"and in a fortnight I might be the one saying yes, and if I'm the one saying yes then knowing who else said yes in the first week is the only thing that would make me say it. So no, and you can't have it, and write me down as a no."**

---

**He wrote it down. He had it on the back of his hand by half past twelve and he did not put a name to it, because a name on a list of nine rooms is a room's business and a no without a name is a no.**

"**You'll want it in writing,**" the man said. "Whatever the answer is. Not from me — from the people who said yes. If nine rooms put a fortnight of paper through one man and there's nothing on paper anywhere saying they said it, then in three weeks one of the nine has had a bad morning and decided they never said any of it, and the man collecting it is the one person in this city who can't argue."

"That's four sentences of a reason."

"It's a reason and it's free and you didn't ask for it."

---

**And then the folder came up off the half-landing, and Marek asked a question that was not on the list, and he has been asked since why, and the answer is that he had spent about four months refusing to be the person rooms send things to, and there was a man in front of him who was owed an answer of a different kind, and about nine seconds was the whole of the difference between the two.**

"What are you doing with that."

"Rota."

"That's not an answer, it's a noun."

---

**The man of about thirty-eight took his hand out of the inside pocket of his coat and there was a plastic sleeve in it with a printed nine-line notice in it, and the notice had a signature box at the foot of it, and the signature box was empty, and the sleeve had nothing written on the outside of it, not a name, not a date, not a number.**

"That's from a Friday," he said. "**About four years back. I had an inspection and it was a query and I did it in a shop over the road from here in about nine seconds and a woman at a different counter told me my query wasn't on the list.**"

---

**And Marek asked what the query was, and the man of about thirty-eight said it in eleven words and did not say it as though it mattered.**

"Whether the stair light and the landing and the roof hatch were all in one person's charge. They weren't, they were in three, and nobody had ever written that down, and I wanted somebody to write it down."

"**And?**"

"And it wasn't on the list," he said, **"which means it never will be, and I've had it in this coat for about four years and it's never been on a board anywhere, and it goes in the bin on the twenty-eighth, and that's the end of it and I don't want to talk about it any more."**

---

**Nobody said anything for about four seconds. It is the longest four seconds in this entry and nothing whatsoever happens in them.**

**He did not offer to help. He did not say it was a good question. He did not say it was a shame. He has been offered help about four hundred and fifty times in this city in about four months and he has said no to every single one of them and this is the first time he has been in a room where the thing on offer was not help and the correct answer was still no.**

**What he said was: "That bin's the wrong bin."**

---

**And the man of about thirty-eight said: "**It isn't, actually.**" And he put the sleeve back in the inside pocket of his coat, and the putting back took about four seconds and was the only thing either of them did that was not words, and Marek watched him do it and did not say the second thing he had come to say.**

The second thing was this: that nine buildings in four districts and two towns had agreed, or were about to agree, to put a fortnight of their paper through one man who is not paid and is not on a rota anybody wrote, and that in about a fortnight there would be more paper moving through that one man in this city than has ever moved through him, and that the only piece of paper in this arrangement belonging to anybody was a form in a coat that was going in a bin on the twenty-eighth.

**He did not say it. He has not worked out yet why not, and the reason is not modesty, and the man of about thirty-eight has not asked him about it and will not.**

---

**What he did instead was ask a second question that was also not on the list, and it was the last one he asked anybody in this city for about a fortnight.**

"The building. Do you want to know whether the others said yes, when it's a number."

"**No,**" said the man of about thirty-eight. **"I've got nine flats and a stair light nobody owns. If four of them put their paper through a man in a shop and then one of the four gets sent something he can't read, he'll come to me and I'm the one with the sleeve."**

He went up. The folder went with him and the door shut and Marek stood in that stair for about a minute with nothing to do in it.

---

**There was a man at the counter at about a quarter to one who wanted to send a thing back and had brought it in a carrier bag and wanted to be told which counter it went to, and it had gone round four times in about six weeks and been posted back four times, and this is a thing that has happened in this city roughly nine hundred times a year and about four hundred and fifty people in it could each say one true thing about it.**

**He had the room written on it. It was a room and not a number, which is the whole of the rule, and the rule is that the person who can stand in that room is the person who can make it make sense, and he had been told it was received four times by four counters in four buildings, and he wanted it to stop.**

Marek told him where the room was. He did it in about nine seconds, and it is the one thing he is better at in this city than anything else, and it is why about nine hundred returns a year arrive at one room in a second district and are sorted into four trays by a woman who is not paid. **The man wrote it down without looking up and the man has never stood in that room and does not know whether anybody does.**

---

**And then the man said a second thing, in about four seconds, with no weight on it at all.**

"**It's the same room they keep saying it's in.**"

"Yes."

"Four times. The same room, four times."

"Yes."

"Then nobody's standing in it," said the man of about fifty-eight, and shouldered his bag, **"and whoever told you it's a room is telling you a thing that's about four years old."**

---

**He stood at that counter with nothing in his mouth for about nine seconds, and the woman of about thirty-four looked at him from four feet away and said nothing whatever, which was the correct thing for her to do and is the most expensive silence anybody has ever let stand in this shop.**

**What he gave up at a quarter to one was the answer to a question he has carried since the spring, which is whether a rule that has been right for about four years is right because it is right or right because nobody has gone and looked.** He cannot ask the woman who said it to him, because she has stopped. He has nobody else's version of it. The five that do not correspond to a count given in a room in Saltmarket have never been shown to anybody and are not going to be now.

He said, to the counter, to nobody in particular: **"Go on. I'll do that one on Thursday."**

**She was right, and he knew she was right, and knowing it is the part that has stopped him sleeping properly about four nights a month since the spring, and there is no form anywhere in this city on which a person writes down that a rule might be old.**

**He put the man's carrier bag down on the counter to weigh it, because there was nothing else to do with his hands, and the scale said four pounds and the man had said it was papers, and neither of those two numbers was the number that was on the thing.**

**The man of about fifty-eight was still in the doorway with the bag when he said the last thing, and he said it to the doorframe.**

"**If it isn't received, what is it.**"

**And the correct answer took Marek about nine seconds and it is the answer he has given to about four hundred people in this city since the spring, and he gave it, and the man went out, and the woman of about thirty-four wrote nothing down, because there is no column on a day sheet for an answer that is always the same.**

**At about four the woman of about thirty-four took that counter and Marek went to the flats again, at the end, because he had one more question for the man of about thirty-eight and he wanted to ask it in a corridor where it could not be overheard in a hall.**

He did not ask it. **The man of about thirty-eight was not in on the Wednesday night. The rota was on the fridge door in the hall in a plastic wallet and it said nothing about a twenty-eighth or a sleeve or a bin and Marek read all of it standing up in about forty seconds and put it back the way he found it, which is a thing he would not have done in the spring and has not thought about since.**

*Four seconds for a no, eleven words for a query, and two questions that were not on a list.* **Three of nine asked. A woman of about twenty-six in a supply room said yes with a condition. A room above a line in Saltmarket had not been reached and was not this day's work. And nine flats on a street with trees on one side, where a man of about thirty-eight keeps the rota and had a printed nine-line notice in a plastic sleeve in the inside pocket of a coat, refused in about four seconds and gave a reason that cost him nothing and half of what he wanted, which is that he might be the one saying yes in a fortnight and would want to know who had gone before him.** He would not be told that.
