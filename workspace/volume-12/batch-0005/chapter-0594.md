# Chapter 594 — A Friday With Eleven Jobs And Four Times The Paperwork In A Row Of Four Hundred Doors Where A Woman Of About Forty-Four Has Decided That A Card Is One Line And A Household Is Four Things And None Of The Four Can Be Checked From The Others, Said Out Loud To About Nine People By About Nine People Who Name What It Costs In Their Own Faces

**The Friday was a bench in Lattice Ward until about two, a counter until about six, and four rooms off a service road from about half past six, and its shape was about sixteen hundred pieces of paper going back down a road where about four hundred households live behind four hundred and a bit doors.**

---

Eleven jobs on the Friday, each one dated, eleven people at the counter, the last of them at about a quarter past five. The bench until two, that counter two until six, and the four rooms off the service road from about half past six. **The heat in that flat is on and has been on at that setting since the middle of last month, four names on that one lease are between them paying what it costs, and a fortnight a cook put out of her own wages is owed by nobody and is going to be owed by nobody when this month turns.** Those four rooms are one thousand and fifty-four days, one hundred and fifty weeks and four days, four of them and one behind, and they are warm.

The card in the rail along the front of that counter is one thousand and fifty-eight days, one hundred and fifty-one weeks and one day, four days past the room, and the rail takes two.

**The nineteenth line on the two nails above that bench is seven hundred and fifty days, one hundred and seven weeks and one day. The hold of the man of about thirty-three in another city is six hundred and eighty-seven days, ninety-eight weeks and one day, and the ask is seven hundred and forty-four days, one hundred and six weeks and two days, and the man of about fifty-one against that north wall is six hundred and sixty days, ninety-four weeks and two days. None of those four is a day anything happened to the heating in this city, and no figure of days off is carried for that heating anywhere in this entry.**

---

**The first of the four towns is a row of four hundred and a bit doors with flats above them and a pumping station at the end, about four hundred people, and a woman of about forty-four who keeps the winter list.**

She has kept it since the spring. About nine people in that town have asked her, at some point since the spring, to put their name on something, and she has, and one man of about thirty-six has signed a request to leave the arrangement entirely and it was honoured and the cost of it was said out loud by the people who were leaving.

**On the Friday of this week she told about nine people, in a first floor, at about seven in the evening, that the row is going to answer the same question four times over.**

The question is one line on a sheet from a firm four days of bus travel away. It asks for a single figure: how many households in your place will take a delivery in the spring.

**"The number we send is not the number we have got,"** she said. **"It is a card. And a card is one line with a name and a house and two boxes, and none of those four can be checked from any of the other three, and every one of them can be wrong on its own without anybody noticing for four years."**

She put four sheets of paper on the table. They were the same size as the card and they were printed the same way.

**"Name. House. Who is actually at that house now. The date somebody stood at that door and saw them. Four things. Four answers. Four returns."**

**"That is four times the paper,"** a man of about thirty-eight said.

**"It is about sixteen hundred pieces of paper going down a road for about four hundred households,"** she said, **"and it is four times what went last year, and I am telling you what it is before I ask anybody to touch it, because a thing that is four times the work should never be the same conversation as a thing that is the same work."**

Nobody in that room said anything for about four seconds.

**"The number comes back the same,"** she said. **"That is the part I want said out loud. It comes back the same and it will be right. Not better. Right. In four years the whole arrangement will work and it will work because four hundred people gave up two hours a week for it and not one of them got anything back."**

---

**About nine people said what it cost, in nine faces, in about ninety seconds, and she wrote all nine down on the back of one of the four sheets and read them out at the end and nobody asked her to.**

A woman of about twenty-six with two children said she can do a Monday and a Thursday and not a Wednesday, because of the school run, and that this has been the case since the summer.

A man of about thirty-eight said he can do two evenings and not four, and that he is already doing one every other week on the row and that the two are going to have to be the same evening.

A man of about forty-one said he is at a gate until about ten on a Tuesday and could not do any Tuesday at all.

A woman of about twenty-nine said she will do any evening and does not want to be named on a sheet.

A man of about thirty-three said he will do the four and wants it in writing that he is doing the four, because he is not doing the four for free and the shop is closed at nine.

A woman of about fifty-two said she can do any of them and that saying so out loud in this room is the last time she intends to say it.

**"Then the four evenings are four people and a half,"** the woman of about forty-four said, **"and the half is me, and the shop shuts at nine, and I will be doing the fourth from about nine until about eleven, on my own, at this table, in that shop, and nobody is to stay and help me and nobody is to feel they have to."**

About four of the nine said no to that last part and about five said nothing.

**"About nine hours a week,"** she said. **"Say it out loud, somebody, because the firm will see a number come back the same and it will not see this, and in about four years somebody will ask why the number was right and there will be a piece of paper in a file four days of travel from here with this evening on the back of it and nobody will have read it."**

A man of about twenty-nine said: **"About nine hours a week, of yours, and about two each of eight other people's, and about four hundred of them do not know any of this is happening."**

**"That is the sentence,"** she said, **"and I am going to write it down exactly like that."**

She wrote it down exactly like that.

**Nobody in that first floor thanked anybody. The nine went out into a street that was already dark. The man of about thirty-three has it in writing. The fourth evening belongs to one woman and she has told about four people not to come and about four of them came anyway on the first one and she did not say anything about it and they did not stay.**

---

**Marek heard none of it on the Friday, and the sheet that carried it would not reach this city until the Monday, and the four days between those two are the whole of what the work is.**

He was at a bench in Lattice Ward until two, and at a counter from two until six with eleven people through that door, and at about half past four a man of about thirty-eight with a meter under his arm asked him, not as a question, whether anybody had ever worked out what it cost to be found twice.

**"Found twice,"** Marek said.

**"Somebody hands in a card and somebody knocks on the door,"** the man said, **"and both of those are a person going to a place on purpose because a piece of paper said it should happen. It is the same act twice and neither one knows about the other. I have got a meter in the back of my van that has been read twice since the summer and I have not worked out which of the two readings was me."**

**"Which one were you,"** Marek said.

**"I have not decided yet,"** the man said, and went out into the rain with it.

Then four rooms off a service road with the heat on and about four people in them who were not asked anything.

**What he noticed on the Friday evening, and told nobody in those four rooms, is that the fourth of the four sheets is the one nobody will ever read.**

Three of them can be checked against the others. A name can be checked against a house. A house can be checked against a date. A date can be checked against a name. The fourth one, the one that says who is actually at that house now, can only be checked by knocking, and the knock is the thing being asked for, and so the fourth sheet is a piece of paper whose only evidence is a person standing on a step.

She is going to fill it in anyway. About four hundred times, by hand, by one woman, from about nine until about eleven, in a shop that has shut.

**He has wanted for a year for a place to put a thing that is true and that nobody can check from a distance, and there is one now, and it is a piece of paper in a shop in a town four days of bus travel away, and he will never see it, and he is not owed it, and the reason it exists is that a woman of about forty-four would not send three sheets and one lie-by-omission.**

**What he wanted, on the Friday night, with about four people asleep in four rooms, was to be the fourth pair of hands on a fourth evening between nine and eleven in a shop four days of bus travel away, and to have written the fourth sheet, and to have been one of the eight that a woman of about twenty-nine did not want named on anything.**

Nobody thanked anybody that day, at a counter in Lattice Ward or in a first floor over a row of shops, and the shutter came down at about ten, and a Friday is an ordinary weekday and there is no shop anywhere that opens on a Sunday.

Friday of week 218, at ten, and this entry is eleven dated jobs and eleven customers, about nine people told in a first floor over a row of four hundred doors that the row is going to answer one question four times over, about sixteen hundred sheets of paper where about four hundred households live, about nine hours a week of one woman between nine and eleven in a shop that shuts at nine, a man of about thirty-three told in writing that he is not doing the four evenings for free, nine costs named in nine faces and written on the back of one sheet, a man of about thirty-eight with a meter who said a thing about being found twice and would not say which reading he was, and nobody thanked at any point in it.*

The counter's standing contents, unchanged and in order: a bench, a cage with a man in it with his face turned to the wall, a roller shutter, a tool roll with one screwdriver in the fourth pocket, a wall list in its sleeve, a shelf of finished work, a rail of two. *Four returns for one card, as a record of ninety seconds and nine faces.* **Place: a first floor over a row of shops, at about seven in the evening, in the first of the four towns, with about nine people in the room and about four hundred and a bit doors outside the window. Who put it: a woman of about forty-four who keeps that town's winter list and is paid for it and is not owed anything by it. What was decided: that one card is one line and a household is four things — a name, a house, who is actually at that house, and the date a person last stood at that door — and that none of the four can be checked from another, and so all four go back, and about sixteen hundred sheets of paper go down a road where about four hundred households live. What she said before asking anybody to touch it: that it is four times the paper, and that a thing four times the work must not be discussed in the same conversation as a thing the same size of work. What she said about the result, in a face: that the number comes back the same and will be right, not better, and that in about four years it will work because about four hundred people gave up two hours a week for it and not one of them got anything back. What nine people named, in nine faces, in about ninety seconds: one Monday and a Thursday and never a Wednesday, because of a school run; two evenings and not four, and those two have to be the same evening as the other job; no Tuesdays at all because of a gate until ten; any evening but no name on a sheet; all four evenings, in writing, because he is not doing them free; any evening, said once in that room and not again; and about nine hours a week of hers alone from nine until eleven with the shop shut, having told about four people not to come. What she did: wrote all nine on the back of one sheet and read them out, and a man of about twenty-nine summarised it and she copied his sentence word for word onto the same sheet. Who thanked anybody: nobody in that room, then or afterwards. Owner: sixteen hundred sheets, about four hundred doors, nine evenings a week that are not anybody's gift, and one pair of hands after nine o'clock.**

*What the day did, and what it did not touch.* **A row of four hundred doors decided to answer one question four times over, and about nine people said out loud what that would cost them, and about four hundred households will be counted correctly in the spring without knowing that anybody did anything.** Nobody thanked anybody on the Friday: not the nine in that first floor, not the man of about thirty-three who has his four evenings in writing, not the woman of about forty-four who will be at that table alone from nine until eleven. The shutter went down at about ten and this day is not a Sunday and the hour does not vary. He taught nobody, demonstrated to nobody, assessed nobody and helped nobody, and none of the four was asked of him. **The register of correct acts that changed nothing, done by people owed nothing, remains at two, being a clipboard inside a cold-store door in the first of the four towns and four torn pieces of a list in a coat pocket at that town's gate, both the work of one woman of about fifty-two, and this day's fourth-evening woman is not a third instance of it, because the fourth evening is two hours and not nine seconds, and the four evenings she has bought change no household at all, and she is owed nothing by buying them.** The rail card is one thousand and fifty-eight days, in the rail all day, in a rail of two, and a return card carrying two boxes and a metal clip is a different object and did not appear in this entry. The rooms are one thousand and fifty-four days and warm. Nineteen lines hang on two nails and there is no twentieth. The drawer under that bench is shut with four sheets in it and the fourth has been in it since the Saturday of week 218. The man of about fifty-one by the north wall has still not been given a written thing by anybody. The page of the woman of about thirty was the eighth of eight and was not looked at, and no apology was offered to her and none was due.
