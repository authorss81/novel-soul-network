# Chapter 561 — The First Monday Of Something, And Eleven Jobs On It, And A Man Of About Sixty-One Who Asked What A Repair Cost And Was Told The Truth For The First Time In Eleven Years Of A Figure Nobody Had Ever Corrected

**The Monday was a bench in Lattice Ward until about two, a counter until about six, and four rooms off a service road from about half past six, and its shape was eleven dated jobs and eleven people through the door and one question at the end of the afternoon that nobody in the shop had prepared anybody for.**

---

The Monday was eleven long and every one of the eleven carried a date, and eleven people came through that counter, and the last of them at about half past five. Bench in Lattice Ward until two, the counter two until six, and the service road from about half past six with the heat on. **The flat is on the heating and it is dearer than it was before the winter, four names on that tenancy are carrying the rise between them, and a fortnight that a cook laid out of her own wages is still owed by nobody and will be owed by nobody when this month turns.** The rooms off that service road are nine hundred and eighty days old, one hundred and forty weeks to the day, four of them and one behind, and they are warm.

The card in the rail along the front of that counter is nine hundred and eighty-four days old, one hundred and forty weeks and four days, four days past the room, and the rail takes two.

**The nineteenth line on two nails above the bench in Lattice Ward is six hundred and seventy-six days old, ninety-six weeks and four days, and it is still the only line of the nineteen that has ever closed on a zero and it has not closed on one again since the spring.** The hold of the man of about thirty-three, who is in another city and holds four buildings and about nine hundred doors with no stamp and no reference and nobody's authority, is six hundred and thirteen days old, eighty-seven weeks and four days. The man of about fifty-one at the north wall of the service-road rooms is five hundred and eighty-six days old, eighty-three weeks and five days, and the thing he asked for in the spring, that it be written down somewhere by somebody who is not the man at the counter, is unmade and owed and is not going to be made this week by anybody.

---

**Nobody thanked anybody on this Monday and the shutter came down at about ten, and there is no Sunday in the ten days this entry is one of, and the reason for that is not a plan and is a calendar.**

He had said the number out loud for about eleven years, and he had never once said anything else.

Not often, either. Perhaps twice a week, in a bad week four times, and it had always been the same two numbers in the same order, and it was wrong, and he had known it was wrong since he was sixteen, and he had never told another human being that he did not know what the right number was. A man who admits not knowing a price is a man who has been charging nine pounds ten for a thing that costs him about four pounds of material and an hour of a bench he does not own, and that is a sentence a person can only say once and then cannot un-say on a Monday.

**At about ten past four a man of about sixty-one came in with a lamp on a flex and a carrier bag, and put the lamp down on the counter, and asked him what it would cost to put a new one on.**

**"I don't know,"** Marek said.

It was not a decision. He had a sentence ready and it was the number, and the number did not arrive, and what arrived instead was the other one, and it was out of his mouth before he had any idea that it was going to be.

The man stopped with his hand still on the bag.

**"You don't know."**

**"No."**

**"You've got a price on the ticket."**

**"I've got two prices on the ticket and the shop does about four pounds and thirty of material and I have never told anybody either of those things."**

**"Right,"** the man said, and then he said the thing that made the afternoon, and he said it in about four seconds and there was no craft in it at all. **"Then what do I do. I've got to leave something on the counter or the book says nothing happened."**

And Marek told him to leave a pound, and the man put a pound note down and went out and did not ask for the change, and the change was already in his coat pocket, and Marek did not offer it, and did not think about it until about nine that night, and by nine it had stopped being a thing he could do anything about and had become what it actually was, which is a man in a lamp shop keeping about ninety-three pence that he was owed by a stranger who had trusted him with a lamp.

**Nobody asked him about it. Not that day, and not the next day, and not in the four days after that, and the man of about sixty-one has not been back and there is no reason on earth that he would be, and that is the entire consequence of the only new thing a man of twenty-two did on the first Monday of this run, and it is that nobody knows it happened at all except the man at the counter and the page this is written on.**

**The small thing it cost him is arithmetic and it is permanent. The pound note is in the small tin at the back of the bench and there is no docket for it, because there was no job, and a man who keeps two nails above a bench and a card in a rail has never in his life had an unaccounted pound in the back of his own bench, and there is one now, and every total he makes from that tin onwards will be a total that is out by a pound for as long as he keeps making them.**

---

**The pound note is in the tin at the back of the bench and the tin is not the tin in the room above the line in another district and has nothing to do with it. It is the smallest tin in this shop and it takes coins and it takes notes and there is a docket in the front of the counter for every job that came through the door that day, eleven of them, and there is no docket anywhere for the pound note, and the pound note is not on anything.**

**A card with two boxes on it, a date out and a date back, that comes with a metal clip in a box from a firm with a counter and a yellow strip on it, is a different object from the card in the rail along the front of that counter, and the two are not joined here or on any other day of these ten entries, and the man of about sixty-one was not carrying one and did not ask about one.**

He did the eleven. He did them in the order they came. The last of the eleven was a woman of about thirty-four with a toaster that kept tripping a spur, and she was told in about nine seconds, and not as a lesson and not by being shown anything, that the spur was the toaster's and not the circuit's, and she said **"Right"** and went away satisfied and faintly irritated, and neither of those was his fault.

**What he noticed, and did not say, and told nobody, and has not written on anything, was this: eleven years of a number he knew was wrong, and a man of about sixty-one said *you've got a price on the ticket* as though the ticket were a fact about the world and not a thing one man has been putting on a card since he was sixteen. And it turned out, in about four seconds, at ten past four on a Monday, that nobody had ever agreed with the number. Not one person in about eleven years had said it was right. They had paid it. He had been having a conversation with about eleven years of strangers about a figure and every one of them had let him have it.**

**What he wanted and did not say, and will not say to anybody who was not in that shop, was to be a man who says the true thing first. He has been the person who gets asked for eleven volumes of work and he has built a life out of being the man with the answer, and the answer was on a ticket, and the ticket was wrong, and the wrongness was his and had been his since he was sixteen and about four hundred and fifty people in this city have had it in their hands.**

He is twenty-two. He has a bench he does not own and a counter with two cards in a rail along the front of it, and he has never in his life been the cheapest correct answer to anybody, and standing there at about ten past four on the first Monday of this run he discovered that he did not know whether he wanted to be, and the discovery was about four seconds long and it is not written on anything.

Nobody thanked anybody. The shutter came down at about ten.

Monday of week 208, at ten, and this entry is a man of about sixty-one who asked what a lamp would cost and was told by a man of twenty-two that he did not know, and a pound note in the back tin that has no docket, and the first Monday of a run of ten entries in which the two numbers on that ticket are not mentioned once in the right hand of a person.*

*Conditions and docket.* **The Monday held eleven and all eleven were dated and eleven people came through the door, the last at about half past five.** The day as it was lived: bench in Lattice Ward until two, counter two until six, the four service-road rooms from about half past six. The separation stands at three hundred and sixty days, fifty-one weeks and three days, unmoved, and about four days of bus travel each way this week did not shorten it. The Exchange did not sit this Wednesday, its book stands at sixty lines, its tin at seventy-three, and no number was read out, because a count belongs to a sitting and to no other morning. The four words that name a public body are at zero on this entry and are not alluded to anywhere in it. **The Monday walked: the ask six hundred and seventy days, ninety-five weeks and five days; the man of about fifty-one five hundred and eighty-six days, eighty-three weeks and five days; the hold six hundred and thirteen days, eighty-seven weeks and four days; and all three of those are ages, and none of them is a day anything happened to the heating in this city, which is a closed thing here with no figure of days off for it anywhere in it.**

*The lamp, and the two numbers, and a pound note with no docket on it, as a record of about four seconds.* **Who came: a man of about sixty-one with a lamp on a flex and a carrier bag, at about ten past four. What he asked: what it would cost to put a new flex on. What was said: I don't know, and then the reason, which was that there are two prices on the ticket, the shop does about four pounds thirty of material, and he has never told anybody either of those things and is not going to start on a Monday. What the man said: you've got a price on the ticket. What was said: I've got two prices on the ticket. What the man then said, in about four seconds and without craft in it: then what do I do, I've got to leave something on the counter or the book says nothing happened. What was said: leave a pound. What happened: a pound note went on the counter and the man went out and did not ask for the change and the change was already in his coat. Where the note is now: in the small tin at the back of the bench. What is not with it: any docket, any entry on any sheet, and any line in the rail. What the man of twenty-two noticed and said to nobody: that nobody has ever agreed with the number, and that about eleven years of people paying it is not the same as about eleven years of people saying it was right. Owner: a pound note, a lamp with a flex that was not put on, and a man of about sixty-one who is owed about ninety-three pence by a shop he will not go back to.**

*What the day did, and what it did not touch.* **A man said out loud that he did not know a price he has written on a ticket every working day since he was sixteen, and a stranger put a pound on a counter and went out, and the change stayed in his coat, and neither of them said thank you to the other and neither of them had anything to. The woman of about thirty sat in the chair nearest the door in that service-road room on the Monday evening with a ring binder on a shelf eight feet above her and was not asked a question and was not thanked, and the binder was not lifted. The man of about fifty-one was against the north wall with his bag on the floor and had said nothing this month and the thing he asked for in the spring is still owed and still unmade. He taught nobody, demonstrated nothing, assessed nobody and assisted nobody on this Monday, and the one question he was asked was a price and he answered it and the answer was that he did not know. The correct-things-that-change-nothing count is two and nothing in this entry moves it, the two being a clipboard on a nail inside a cold-store door in the first of the four towns and a woman of about twenty-four at the end of a room who asked four people whether anyone wanted to write down what had happened and was told no four times and said that that was the correct answer. The card in the rail is nine hundred and eighty-four days old and was in the rail all day and is not the same object as a card with two boxes and a clip. The room off that service road is nine hundred and eighty days old and warm, four and one behind. The board stands at nineteen lines and the twentieth has never been started. Those four words are nowhere on this entry.**
