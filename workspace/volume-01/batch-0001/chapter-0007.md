# Chapter 7 — A Line Is Not Proof

The clinic's treatment bay at half past nine on a Thursday night had the radiator turned up and a strip light with a flicker in it, and Talia Venn was standing just inside the door with her coat still on, and the first thing she said was that she had changed the terms.

"I offered you a scope on Tuesday," she said. "Two performances, on the frame, with a person, at working speed, use and remember, no derive, review on the date. That is what I offered you and I am not going to do it, and I am telling you on the step instead of afterwards."

"Why not?"

"Because the clinic does not own a rescue brace." She tipped her head at the shelf. "There are four-nineteens on that shelf for wrists and a box of thirty-odd other things and not one frame between them. If I improvise a brace out of a blanket and hand you a demonstration that is not the thing on the sheet, then the sheet is a lie — and if the sheet is a lie then every sheet I have ever written is a lie, and I am not going to do that to you tonight in order to be useful to you."

"Then write a different one."

"Yes." She went past him and hooked the door to. "Sit down. It takes four minutes and you are going to watch me do the whole of it, because the point of a scope is that it exists before the thing and not after."

She took a clean sheet off the clipboard and wrote while she talked, and she read it back before she turned it round.

"Write it out," she said. "Word for word, then I sign under it and you put your name on the other side of the line. If either of us wants to change a word, we stop and start again, and the first one is dead and does not get an amendment."

It was the purpose, the date, the two performances, use and remember, no derive, no retention by any third party, no comparison afterwards against anything Mr Senn already carried. It was a review date. It was the fact that the clinic owned no rescue brace and that what she was about to demonstrate was therefore a substitute rig and not a brace. And it was, written out in full, the fact that Mr Senn carried an echo of a patient's practice that he had not been authorised to retain, and that he was undertaking this to learn what an authorised one looks like.

She tore the sheet off, folded it once, gave him a copy, and put the other in her coat.

"Now you have read all of it before it happens," she said, "which is the whole difference between this and what I stopped you doing in a car park. And I want it noted that it took me two days to work that out, and I did not get it right first time."

Then she took down the shelf box and put a folded blanket on the table, two bricks in a canvas bag, and a length of the clinic's own webbing, because the clinic did not own a rescue brace and had never pretended to.

"Watch the hands," she said. "Not the plate. The hands. This is the whole of it and I am going to do it twice and you are going to see the same thing twice, and I am not going to explain anything while I do it, because if I explain it you will compare the explanation instead of the practice."

She got her left hand under the bag of bricks. The bricks were the load. Her right hand found the blanket, folded it twice more, and set it between the bag and the edge of the table — the equivalent of the plate's job, taking the force off the thing it was protecting.

The thing she was protecting was her own wrist, which was not injured, and Marek understood, a beat later than it should have taken him, that this was the point of the entire demonstration and that she had not said a word about it.

"First time through," she said, "I am doing it for a person I am not talking to. Watch the last two hands."

Her left hand came off the bag. Her right hand came off the blanket. Both came off and hung in the air for a moment with nothing in them, and then the left one went flat, palm down, on the tabletop, an inch away from the bag — not touching it, not holding it — and the right one went onto the bag's strap, and she said, in the ordinary voice you use to say a thing to a person in a room:

"This is a block of foam on a table. I have taken the weight. Nothing else has happened. You can move your own arm whenever you want, and if you move it I will let go and it will fall, and that is the correct result."

The right hand came off the strap. The left hand stayed on the table, flat, saying nothing.

Then she took the weight back and stood up and said, "That's one. It's not better than the version you already know. It's a different thing. Do you want the second one now or in ten minutes."

"Ten minutes."

She used the ten minutes to wash her hands and to let a man with a hi-vis jacket have the chair.

At five to ten she did it again, and the second time she slowed down in one place, at the left hand, and put it down a half-inch further from the bag than the first time, so that the space between her hand and the load was visible from across the room.

"You noticed that," she said.

"I noticed you changed it."

"I changed it because you are watching my hands, and a person watching a demonstration deserves the same distance twice." She wiped the table. "That is not a technique. That is manners. I would like it on the record that the manners and the technique have nothing to do with each other."

Marek stood by the sink with his notebook out and had written four pages in nine minutes and had to cross out three of them.

*1. She lifts her hands off. That is the difference. The kit never lets go, because there is no point at which the hands are required to be empty.*
*2. She says a sentence to a person. Out loud. At a point where the sentence can still stop the thing happening.*
*3. (crossed out) The third beat is the same and only the fourth is different.*
*4. (crossed out) The difference is the sentence.*
*5. (crossed out) It is the fourth beat, obviously.*

He had written it five times and had not once described an action a person could fail at, which was, he understood, exactly the mistake a person makes when they are writing down a shape they are carrying in their chest instead of a thing that happened in a room.

He tried once more.

*6. Both times: hands off the load, then one hand on the table, not touching, and a sentence said to a person, and then the hands are free and the person decides. The kit has no point where the hands are required to be empty and no sentence, and the fourth beat is a word from the front of the room.*
*7. I do not yet know why mine has a person in it. I know only that it has one. Two performances of one thing is one thing.*

He had the shape. He had it as clearly as he had ever had anything.

He also had a piece of knowledge he did not want, which was that a nurse who had trained in the same family of courses he had — same department, same first-year grounding, same set of laminated cards with the four columns — was not a second opinion about a kit. She was a colleague. She was the good version of the same argument.

Two performances of one thing is one thing. And one thing that agrees with another thing he already believed was not evidence. It was company.

He got as far as thinking the word *agreement* and then stopped, because the word had a shape of its own, a position in the air, like a wire with two people on it —

* * *

> Two practices agree. One boundary is not performed.

Nothing followed. He stood at the sink in a treatment bay in a box on wheels and waited for the rest of it, the way a man waits for the second half of a sentence that somebody has clearly decided to write, and nothing came, and there was no shape to hold, and there was no next step offered, and no hint at all about whether the sentence he had just been given was an observation or an opinion.

Two practices agreed. One of them was a printed card in a drawer and the other of them was a woman who had learned her trade from the same twelve people he had, and agreement between those two things was the sort of thing that happened at conferences.

He put the notebook down and did the only honest thing available, which was to write, in the margin, in the smallest writing he owned:

*Two sources, one of them trained by my own teachers. I wanted this to be proof. It is not proof. It is what I already think, with a witness.*

Then, underneath, in the same small writing, because he was the sort of man who wrote rules down the moment he was frightened of breaking them:

*Do not let wanting press two things together until they look like evidence. If I ever catch myself doing that, the wanting is the answer, not the thing.*

Then he put his hands in his pockets and said, "Can I ask you something that is going to sound like a small question."

"Yes."

"Could you be wrong?"

Talia Venn wiped her hands. "About the fourth beat?"

"About anything. I want to know what your evidence is." He heard how it sounded and pushed on anyway, because it was half past ten on a Thursday in a clinic car park and he was going to say it or never say it. "I have two performances of your brace and a card with a hole in it. That's it. That's the whole of my case. And every single part of it comes out of a building I go to every day, and two thirds of it was taught to me by men who get paid to be right about this, and you were taught by the same men."

The radiator ticked. The flicker in the strip light went on going.

"I taught myself that brace the summer I turned nineteen," Talia said, "in a county clinic, on a table like that one, with a book and a great deal of guessing, and I have been fitting them every working day since. I stopped counting at nine thousand. I learned the fourth beat from a woman called Iris Vandermeer, who was taught it in a hospital corridor by a man who was taught it by somebody in the public health service who is, as it happens, still alive and still teaches in Lattice. So it is not independent. Nothing in this city is independent. That is what a *lineage* is, it is a set of parents, and you do not get to be rigorous by pretending your parents had different opinions."

"Then how do I show a board?"

"You don't. Not with me, and not with that." She nodded at the shelf, at the box of four-nineteens, at the plate with Threadline on the shoulder of its strap. "I have been fitting that thing since September. I can tell you my hand does not like it. That is a real observation and it is mine, and it is worth precisely nothing to anybody outside this building, because I am a nurse on a route who has fitted twenty-six of them and has a bad feeling about the fourth beat, and that is a *column entry*, not a finding." She put the clipboard on the counter. "What a board cannot do is take my word and your word and a hole in a card. What a board would have to be shown is that a technique can be completed in more than one way, by two people who were never in the same room and never taught by the same twelve people, and neither of them using anybody's voice. And I am not that. I am the Thursday you wrote down."

Marek wrote it down exactly. Then he wrote underneath it: *I need three I have never met.*

"Why three?"

"Because two is a coincidence with a witness." He turned the notebook around. "It's a rule I made up ten seconds ago. Tell me it's wrong."

"It is wrong, it is extremely wrong, and it is the right instinct." She almost smiled. "Two is what a room of people will give you for free. Three is what you have to go out and get. The rule you just invented will get you nowhere in four weeks and it is the only thing in this conversation that I would put on a wall."

The Institute's kit store was on the ground floor at the back, behind the porters' cage, and Marek had a brass key for it on a loop of string that Davan Rusk had handed to third-years in the first week of term, when a different department had needed a different key and nobody had thought to ask for the old one back.

He let himself in at eleven forty. He turned the light on and did not turn it on all the way, because the tube over the door is the one Sera Quill can see from the street.

There were nine crates, all Threadline, all the same, stacked two high. The delivery tags were zip-tied to the handles with cable ties, printed in the depot's own house font.

He crouched in front of the nearest crate and worked the catch, and the smell came out at him — mineral fiber, machine oil, the faintly sweet chemical smell of new webbing — and the inside of the crate was packed in a waxed paper with a card on top, and under the card, the plates, standing on edge in their foam slots, twenty-four of them, each one in a printed sleeve.

He took out the card and turned the light onto it with his phone, which was also the camera, which was also the only piece of equipment he had ever owned that cost money.

**PRACTICER KIT — RESCUE BRACE — REV 4-19**
**ASSEMBLED BY: THREADLINE SERVICE DEPOT, LATTICE**
**ASSEMBLY LOT: TL-4-19-0311**
**UPSTREAM TECHNIQUE BUNDLE:** ————————————
**UPSTREAM LICENSOR:** ————————————

Two blank lines with a rule drawn under each, and the rules the same length, and the printing clear enough to see that somebody had drawn them at the same moment with the same rule and then had left both of them empty.

He took four photographs. He took them at an angle so the light did not flare, and he checked each one, and one of them showed a plate sleeve in the corner with **4-19** stamped into the housing, and the second showed the whole label including both empty lines, and he looked at the two empty lines for a long time.

An empty box is not a wrong answer. A blank is a person declining to fill something in, and the reason a person declines is a thousand possible things, and only one of them is somebody with a reason to hide.

He wrote on his forearm, in pencil, because he had no hands free and no light: *two blanks, same rule, same pen. Take the pic. The pic is not the answer.*

Then he lifted the whole stack of twenty-four plates out and set it on the floor, and counted them out loud in a whisper, and put them back in the same slots in the same order, because a crate that has been disturbed is a crate that somebody will notice, and he was not going to walk into the department tomorrow and have the only useful thing he had be a story about a disturbed crate.

Under the plates there was a printed booklet in a plastic sleeve, and the booklet was the reason somebody in that building had built a whole department around a thing with a hole in it.

**PRACTICER KIT — INSTRUCTOR NOTES — REV 4-19**

Eleven pages. Four of them were the drill scripts, word for word, including the thing that had been in Rusk's mouth at eleven-oh-four on the Thursday of week one, set out in his own instructor's hand and made of iron.

*Beat four, scope. The learner cannot perform this beat alone and is not expected to. The instructor confirms scope aloud on the beat — "scope" — and the learner releases on the instructor's confirmation. Do not allow the learner to proceed on a self-initiated release. Where the learner releases without confirmation, re-run from the top.*

Nine lines, and then a note underneath them in the same print:

*This is a support beat. It is not a step in the technique.*

Marek sat down on the floor of the kit store with his back against a crate and read that again, and then he took the 3-08 manual out of his jacket, because he believed in carrying the manual, and held it under his phone light with his left thumb.

On the same page, in the same place, the 3-08 manual said: *Beat four, scope. The practitioner confirms aloud, at this point, what the brace has been permitted to touch, and returns the next decision to the subject of the brace.*

Same beat. Same page. Same four columns. One of them had *the practitioner*, and one of them had *the instructor*, and between the two of them a person had walked out of the sentence, and the sentence still scanned, and it still scanned beautifully, and there were three hundred and forty people in the department who had been handed it this term and taught it out loud.

He had no idea why. That was the honest position and he wrote it down as the honest position: it could be a bad print run, or a card printed against the wrong revision, or a supplier who assembled a bundle out of somebody else's book and never asked what was in it, or somebody who sat down and chose it. Four possibilities and eleven pages, and not one page in the booklet carried a name, a date, or a set of initials anywhere in it — because that is what an instruction sheet does not carry, and Marek had spent two years reading parts lists, and a parts list does not tell you what a person was thinking when they left a component off.

He turned to the header of page four, where the emergency note sat in a box.

*Where a local node does not answer, the last approved civic sequence is used as the provisional response.*

The same sentence he had read on a laminated card in week one without registering it, and the same sentence that was in a fourteen-year-old district relay log in a cabinet at Lattice Ward, in a careful clerk's hand, in a row marked 04:20. Two sources, and they agreed, and one of them was the source, and neither of them named a body.

And that was what made his hands cold, standing up with a dead forearm and a numb face. Not his competence. Not his teacher. Not his school. Not a man against a wall with a good voice and twenty-two years. A product, in a waxed-paper crate, with two empty lines on the label, put in a corridor, signed for, and carried out of a depot by somebody who had never asked a single question and could not have told him what the fourth beat was for if he had.

And the thing he could not get past, standing there with the wax seal under his thumb, was that the four possibilities were not equal and that he had no way at all to tell which one it was. He had a hole and he had a box of plates and he had two blank lines, and the blank lines said *nobody wrote a name down*, and a person who had chosen to leave a beat out would have wanted exactly that, and a person who had simply never been asked would have wanted it by accident. The two looked the same from inside a store room at midnight, and the difference between them was the entire case.

People had signed for it. He was one of them. In September he had stood in a corridor at the practical building and put his own name and his own student card against a delivery note for nine crates, in a queue, without reading it, because the queue was moving and it was Tuesday and he had somewhere to be.

He put the plates back in their slots. He put the booklet in its sleeve. He folded the waxed paper over the way it had been. He closed the catch and pulled the waxed seal across it and pressed the flat of his thumb on the wax the way it had been pressed, and he stood in the dark with the light off and the door shut behind him and the brass key warm from his hand.

Then he got the notebook out in the corridor outside the porters' cage and wrote the count out longhand, in the margin, with the date, because he did not trust a photograph to still be readable in a week, and because a number you have written is a number you can say out loud in a room.

*24 plates. 24 printed cards. 1 instructor booklet, rev 4-19, 11 pages. 2 empty lines on the label. Lot TL-4-19-0311. No name, date or initials anywhere in the booklet. Card 3-08 in my jacket, beat four: the practitioner confirms aloud. Card 4-19, beat four: the instructor confirms aloud. Page 4 header: last approved civic sequence. Same line in a Marrow Falls log, day 2, row 04:20.*

He read it twice. He did not write down what it meant. He had a rule now, and the rule was that the day he wrote down what it meant would be the day he started being the man who knew, and the day he started being the man who knew was the day somebody else got to decide what he was talking about.

His phone was in his pocket and it had a message on it from the department's group thread, four hours old, which he had scrolled past while he was looking at a woman's hands.

**WORK-SETTING RELEASE CHECKS — WEEK 6. Third years, in pairs, 18:30.**
**Tuesday: PELL, R. — market cross, Lattice. ARRICK, T. — courier dock, Saltmarket waterfront, west berth.**

He went over the message until the words stopped meaning anything. He read the line twice, specifically, in case the second reading would say something else.

*Rafi* was at the market cross with a partner and a market crew. *Tam* was at a west berth loading dock at half past six on a Tuesday evening, alone with a member of a courier crew, doing a routine brace on a live load on wet concrete, because the department had decided in a fortnight that a work-setting check was how you found out whether a person could release when the place was loud.

He had eaten lunch with him on Sunday. Tam had said, *It's fine, it's a check, it's forty minutes, they'll sign it off and I've got my kitchen hours, it's fine*, in the exact voice that Marek had now heard twice from a man who was frightened.

He looked at the time. Five past one on the Friday morning. Five days.

He stood in the corridor outside the porters' cage and found that he had a schedule, which was the thing he had wanted, and that a schedule was worse than a suspicion, because a suspicion is a thing you can be wrong about and a schedule is a thing that arrives whether you are right or not.

Five days. He had a shape he could not put in anybody, a woman who had told him in plain words that he was not allowed to give it to anybody, a card that said the word was not a step in the technique, and a boy who had eaten lunch with him on Sunday and said it was fine. And there were two things he could do with five days and he could name both of them, and the first one was to go to Tam Arrick before Tuesday and say the thing out loud.

He stood in the dark and worked out that the first thing was the same thing he had done in week one.

He walked to the tram with the brass key still warm in his hand, wrote it in the load book at twenty past two, and then sat on the end of his own bed and did not sleep, because the shape of the fourth beat — the one he had crossed out three times and finally written down in a clinic bay three hours earlier — had gone from a feeling to an appointment.
