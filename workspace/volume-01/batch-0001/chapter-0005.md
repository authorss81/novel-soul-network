# Chapter 5 — The Seal Run

Three weeks is not long enough to learn anything and just long enough to find out what you have.

Marek went to the Spine eleven nights in that period. He repaired a resonance lamp, a stair relay, a door buzzer, two plates, and a set of bathroom heaters in a building nobody could name. He took a courier job on the Wednesday of the second week, forty minutes of levering a jammed gate in Nine Bridges, the wrong arm again, and it put him over into the Sunday and cost him the Sunday, which was rent, and it did nothing at all for the shoulder.

He tried the fourth beat alone on the bench on six separate nights. He got nowhere. He was, by the end, quite certain that his memory of it was a memory of a feeling and not of a shape, the way you can remember the moment you were happy and not one single thing that happened in it.

He saw Rusk in the corridor twice more and both times it was nothing. Once Rusk asked him how the shoulder was. Once Rusk said, offhand, that the department had put new return-path markings on the plates in room two, and that it had taken the technician a fortnight, and that the technician had complained about the color, and that it was a very ugly green.

On the Wednesday of the third week he could not remember whether his mother was on nights. He had to look it up. He had to look up his mother's shift pattern, in his own phone, in his own mother's flat, three streets from his own front door, and he stood in the kitchen with the paper in his hand for a while, and then he put it in the drawer with the takeout menus, and then he did not mention it to her, and she did not notice, and neither of them said anything about it at dinner.

That was the three weeks. Eleven nights at the Spine, six attempts at the fourth beat on a bench, two conversations with Rusk that were not conversations, and one evening in his mother's kitchen with a piece of paper in his hand that he put in a drawer with the takeout menus. Nothing happened in them, and nothing was supposed to, and that was the arrangement he had made with the Institute and with himself: he would be ordinary for three weeks and then he would find out what he was.

---

The exam hall was on the ground floor of the practical building, in a room that had been a gymnasium once and still had the high windows and the two basketball hoops with the nets taken down and one hoop's backboard cracked in a line that somebody had painted over.

Nine candidates. Three examiners at a table: two from the Institute and one sent by the term's licensing board, a thin woman named on the board notice only as **BOARD OBSERVER**, who had brought her own chair and her own pen and did not look up during the briefing.

The frame stood at the center of the floor on a low platform. It was the full rescue brace: mineral-fiber plate, copper return contact, webbing strap with a steel cam buckle, mounted on a load rig that could be set to eighty or a hundred and ten kilos and could be raised to a working height. A manikin hung off it, dead weight, the kind they use for load testing.

There was a bench of nine. Marek was fourth.

Rusk stood at the front in a clean shirt, with the laminated script in his hand and not clipped to anything, and he read the room the way he always did, in a voice that made twenty-two years sound like a reason.

"You will each take the frame alone. The examiners are here to score. They are not here to help you, and I am not here to help you, and if you need help that is the end of your run." He said that last part without any relish at all, which was somehow much worse than relish. "The sequence is four beats. Anchor, load, close, scope. The card is on the bench in front of you. The card is revision 4-19 and it is what you have been drilling all term. If the card and your body disagree, the card is what you will be scored against today, and you should raise it afterwards."

Somebody laughed. It was not a funny line.

"The Solo Seal certifies that you can perform the core rescue technique alone," Rusk said. "It does not license you to work with partners. It does not license you to work with equipment. It certifies one thing, which is that you can do this by yourself, on a person, with nobody in the room but you and the person. Every one of you will do that hundreds of times before you are thirty. This is the piece of paper that lets you do it for money."

He turned the script over.

"Rules for the run, from the board, and they are the same every term. First: a candidate may ask the board observer to repeat a beat. Second: the instructor may speak a beat, because the instructor is present under the scoring conditions, and the board records who supplied it. Third — " and he read this one off the card and not off his own memory, which Marek noticed and did not understand at the time " — where a candidate's sequence does not complete, the deficiency is recorded against the candidate, and remains recorded until the candidate demonstrates otherwise to the satisfaction of the department. Fourth: the run is scored on completion of the sequence, not on safety of the person, because the manikin cannot be hurt and the two are not the same test."

Nobody said anything. Nine people and a great deal of white floor.

The first candidate was a girl Marek did not know, second-year, tall, extremely prepared. She did it beautifully. That was the fact of it — she was better than him, and better than Tam, and her hands knew the strap and her feet knew the anchor and she took the load at a hundred and ten kilos without her breath changing.

And at the fourth beat she stopped.

She stopped the way Tam had stopped, and the way everybody in that room who had drilled this kit since September must have stopped, and she turned her head, very slightly, toward the front.

"Scope," Rusk said.

She let go. The plate took the weight, the frame settled, the manikin came to rest, and a small honest sound went out of the room, and the board observer wrote something, and the department examiner said, "Complete. Four-four. Thank you."

Nine minutes. The whole run.

Marek watched the second and third candidates do the same thing, and understood at last what he was looking at, because the thing he was looking at was a machine, and the machine worked. It had worked all term. It had worked for him — once, on the Thursday in week one, and it had cost a boy the use of his own release forever, and the department had written *favorable note* on it.

The board observer's pen made a small sound on the fourth candidate's form.

Then Marek's name was called.

He walked out onto the floor. Two doors, one at the main and one at the service, both reachable, both open, both eight steps from where he was standing. The lamp above the frame buzzed.

He set the plate. He seated the return contact. He put his heel down and took the anchor, and the room narrowed to eight inches of mineral fiber, and everything that had happened in three weeks narrowed with it, including the notebook, including the tray at the Spine, including a woman with a clipboard he had never answered, including his mother's shift pattern in a drawer with the takeout menus.

He took the load. He felt the copper take it and pass it up. He walked the strap in three alternating passes and the frame came alive under his hands the way it had in week one, when eleven people had been surprised by him and he had been surprised by himself.

Close.

And then the place.

He was aware, with the clarity that comes with practice, of exactly what everyone in the room was waiting for. He was aware of the board observer's pen. He was aware that Rusk was standing eight feet away with a script in his hand and a mouth already forming the shape of a word he had said ten thousand times. He was aware of the second examiner's hand hovering over the box marked *incomplete*, which was a physical box, an actual small wooden tray on the table, and he had not known that until that moment and would think about it for a long time afterward.

Rusk began to speak.

"Sc—"

Marek heard it happen. He heard the consonant leave the man's mouth, out of habit, on the beat, the way a person hands someone a thing they have handed them a thousand times. And he thought, in the whole of the fraction of a second that was available to him, about a fourteen-year-old log page in a metal cabinet, and about a boy's name in a square box, and about the fact that if he took the word then this run would be complete, and complete was the only thing the tray in front of him was not measuring.

"No," he said.

It came out quiet, and loud, and in the flat register he used for reading serial numbers.

The room stopped.

"Senn," Rusk said.

"Don't give it to me." Marek's hands were on the frame. "I'll do it. I just need a second."

Nobody gave him a second. The board observer wrote something and did not look up. The second examiner's hand stayed where it was, over the wooden tray, without closing on it.

Marek stood on the examination floor with ninety-one kilos of dead manikin hanging off a plate in his hands and tried to do the thing he had felt in a gap two seconds wide.

It was not there.

He reached for it honestly. He went down the sequence the way he had gone down it in his head a hundred times in three weeks, looking for the moment where the question gets asked, and he put his hand flat on the plate where the load was going through, and he tried to say the thing out loud to a manikin: *this is what I am allowed to have touched, and here is where you decide what happens next.*

The words were there. He could feel the shape of the sentence. But the sentence wanted a person on the other end of it, and there was nobody, because the whole point of the Solo Seal is that there is nobody, and he had spent three weeks being certain that a person was optional, and it turned out that the fourth beat was not optional at all.

It was the beat where somebody else answered.

His hand stayed on the plate. Eleven seconds. Fifteen. Somewhere in the middle of it he understood, entirely, that he could not do this, and that he was going to fail, and that he was going to fail because he had been correct in public, and there was no version of the next thirty seconds in which those were two different things.

He took his hand off the plate and stepped back.

"Run ended," he said, because somebody had to say it and it was going to have to be him. "Fourth beat not performed. I did not use the substituted cue."

The board observer looked up for the first time. She was perhaps fifty-five. She had a very still, unbothered way of sitting, and she looked at him the way you look at a document that has arrived with a form missing.

"Candidate," she said. "Did you have a substitute available?"

"Yes, ma'am."

"Did you use it?"

"No, ma'am."

"Was it supplied by the instructor?"

"It was begun by the instructor. I declined it."

The board observer wrote three lines and turned the form so the department examiner could read it upside down.

"You understand that the record will state that your sequence was not completed," she said, "and that the deficiency is recorded against you, and that the department may require you to demonstrate otherwise before the entry is amended."

"Yes, ma'am."

"Do you have anything to say that you would like on the form?"

Marek stood on the floor of the old gymnasium with the high windows and the painted-over crack in the backboard, and he had a speech. He had had it in the taxi. He had had it about the fourth beat and the missing performed check and the fourteen-year-old log, and he had been holding it in his mouth for three weeks the way a man holds a stone he means to throw.

He looked at the manikin, hanging there, ninety-one kilos, a practice.

"I'm not able to say it on this form," he said, "because on this form it would sound like a student explaining a mark, and in two weeks it will be a mark. I'll say it in a room with the equipment turned off, and then somebody can write it down properly, and if it's still just a student, it can still be a student."

The board observer looked at him for a moment and then wrote something and put her pen down.

"Candidate four," she said. "Not completed. Fourth beat not performed. Substitute cue declined by the candidate, not supplied by the candidate, and not used. Deficiency recorded pending department review. Thank you, Senn."

She said *Senn* the way you say a word you have just read off a form, and then she turned to the next name, and it was over.

---

He did not cry and he did not shout. He walked out through the service door, into the light, and stood on the step in the way you stand when you are waiting for your legs to finish a sentence.

Then he laughed, once, out loud, in the middle of an empty walkway.

Because of what Rusk had said. He had said *she will do what the load-holder says or she will do nothing* — to Marek, in a plastic chair, exactly three weeks before, in a room with a stuck blind. And then today Marek had been the load-holder, and had not said anything, and Odile's successor had done nothing, and the man in the plastic chair had been right about the mechanism and wrong about the cause, and had also been right to be frightened, and Marek had been right too, and the room had still ended with a wooden tray and a form.

It was the first time in his life he had chosen a rule over a reward. It did not feel like pride. It felt like stepping off a curb expecting the ground to be there and finding it there exactly where it was, which is a small thing, and which he understood — sitting on the step, with his hands hanging between his knees — he would be spending a long time thinking about, because he had chosen it in front of three people whose job it was to make him regret it, and there was no version of this afternoon in which he was a man with a plan.

The department's administrative officer found him at the water fountain, which meant she had been sent, and she had a folder.

"Mr. Senn. I'm Darla Vint, practical department office. Take this with you and read it at home. Do not sign it here."

The folder held three sheets. He read the first one standing up.

It was a letter of offer.

*Mr. Senn is offered a place as technician apprentice, City maintenance track, subject to satisfactory completion of the current term.*

He read it three times because it took him a while to see the shape of the thing, and the shape was: a job. A real one, with a wage, in a trade he was already good at, on a track that did not require a licence, offered to him by the practical department of a school he was failing, on the day he failed it.

The second sheet was the transcript. His record, printed, with the two earlier practical failures and today's line, and today's line read:

*Solo Seal, practical sequence: not completed. Fourth beat absent. Performance dependent on external cue.*

The second examiner had written it in eleven minutes, from memory, without asking the board observer a single question, and it was now the official line, and it was going to be photocopied, and it was going to be true in every way that mattered to anybody who ever read it, and Marek had spent three weeks knowing this and had still hoped — which he noted, and which he was going to have to look at later — that being right would turn out to be a defense.

The third sheet was the condition.

*Provisional witnessed assessment: Thursday of week 7, 09:00, examination hall, board convened.*
*Requirement: a scored release completed alone and unprompted, with no substituted cue used, followed, or accepted within the scored window.*
*Scholarship: suspended pending the outcome of the above. If the assessment is not attempted or not passed, the scholarship review will open in the ordinary way at the end of term.*
*Note: a technician apprenticeship may be held concurrently with the scholarship review. The department does not consider the two to be exclusive.*

Marek read it twice, and then he looked at the doors of the building behind her, and counted them, because it was the only thing in his life he could still do correctly.

"She said 'unprompted,'" he said.

"The board's word. It has been used before."

"Does the instructor still get to speak?"

"The instructor is present. The board's position, and I am quoting it, is that a practice which can only be completed in the presence of a particular person is not a solo practice, and if the board is going to certify it, somebody is going to have to prove it without the person in the room."

He folded the three sheets into his jacket, in the inside pocket, in the order they had been given to him, and he noticed that he had done that in the correct order without thinking about it, and it was the only small professional thing that had gone right all day.

"Can I ask you something," he said.

"You can."

"The assessment needs me to show that the fourth beat is missing."

Darla Vint looked at him for a second longer than the transaction required.

"No, Mr. Senn. The assessment needs you to show that you can do it."

He said thank you and walked away, and he was two steps past the water fountain before the rest of it arrived and stopped him in the middle of the walkway, the way a word in your own mouth can stop you.

She had not said *the fourth beat is missing.* She had not read the folder. She had not been in the room, and no one in that building had yet said, in any document, in any form, in any meeting, in any voice he had heard the sentence *the fourth beat is missing.*

Somebody was going to have to say it out loud, in a room with the equipment turned off, before it could be written down, and the only person he could think of who might say it was a man who was not a student and not a teacher and not on any board, at a repair bench in Lattice Ward, and the man had already refused him once and would refuse him again.

He got out into the tram trench and walked all the way to the tram, and the wind from the south came up out of the water and smelled like salt and diesel and the fish market, and Marek Senn stood on a platform in Nacre with an offer of a job in his left pocket and a lie in his right, and the lie said *dependent on external cue*, and the truth said *he asked for it and nobody gave it to him*, and the second one had nowhere at all to be written.
