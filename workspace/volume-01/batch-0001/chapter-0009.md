# Chapter 9 — What the Record Says

The noticeboard outside the practical department office had a correction on it, printed on a single sheet, dated the previous evening, in the department's house font.

**PRACTICAL DRILL RECORD — GROUP C, WEEK 1 — CORRECTED ENTRY**
*Sequence not completed as scripted. Student intervention at the point of release. Equipment sound. No further action.*

No further action. Four words that had cost a boy a fortnight and would, if left alone, cost a great many more people nothing at all, because nothing at all is what the record was for.

Marek stood and read it twice. Then he went in and asked for the sheet.

"The record is a department matter," Darla Vint said, without any unkindness whatsoever. She was the same as she had been on the Thursday, which was to say she was doing her job in a building that had made the job unpleasant. "You can have a copy of your own transcript at any time. You can't have the drill sheet, because the drill sheet is a teaching record and it names the whole of Group C."

"Eleven students didn't intervene."

"No," she agreed. "Which is why it says *student intervention* and not your name. That is not a courtesy, Mr. Senn. That is a data-protection decision and I made it in about ninety seconds, and if I had put your name on that sheet it would have gone to a board and a board would have asked whether a third-year was capable of ending a sequence without a cue, and once that is asked about you it is asked about all of you, and then we are a school that is frightened of its own students."

Marek took the sheet off the pin. It was a light pin. It came away in his hand.

"Sit down," Darla Vint said. "Anneke Roos wants twenty minutes and she is going to offer you something, and I would like you to have read the third paper before you decide anything at all."

Deputy Roos was fifty-four and had been in the building for nineteen years and had the specific stillness of a person who has learned that the cheapest thing in a meeting is a silence.

There were three papers on the desk. The third was two sides and it was not a form.

"Technician apprenticeship," Marek said, reading it standing up.

"City maintenance track," said Deputy Roos. "Starts the week after next. Forty-one hours, the Spine's own rota, not ours. You would be inside a year, on the ladder by the second, and Ms. Quill has been asking me since September whether I had anybody to put on the ladder, which I took at the time as a complaint about somebody and now I understand was a vacancy."

"Sera asked for me."

"Sera asked for a pair of hands. You are the pair of hands who is standing in front of me with a theory."

He did not sit. He read the whole second page.

"Here is the problem with the deal and I am going to give it to you myself, because the department is not permitted to offer it in a corridor." Deputy Roos turned her chair a quarter turn, away from the desk, as though the conversation were about to become something less official and she wanted to be clear about its status. "You cannot have the apprenticeship and the review. Not at the same time, not this term, and not in any way that leaves a room saying one thing and a file saying another. If we investigate your theory, you are a student with a conflict. If we take you, you are a technician who had an idea at work."

"And if the theory is right?"

"Then you have cost yourself a term and a great deal of money, and you will be right, and you will be a technician." She said it without any relish. "And if the theory is wrong, you will have cost yourself the same term and the same money, and you will be a technician, and the thing you were worried about will have been a gap in a card."

"How would the department characterize it?"

"Hardware." She said the word carefully, laying it on the table. "We would say the return-contact change in 4-19 was made in a way that was not adequately explained in the accompanying material, that a trainee in our own drill identified a variance, and that the department corrected its documentation and reported a vendor matter under the ordinary route. That is true, and every word of it can be defended in front of a board, and none of it requires anybody to have lied."

"And my transcript?"

"Your seal line stays as it is written, because it is accurate, and because a corrected transcript is a thing you cannot give a student twice." She let that sit. "But the technician track does not read the seal line the way the department does. It reads whether you are safe with your hands and whether anybody trusts you near a plate. And you would have a reference from me, and I do not give them to people I have not taught, and I have taught you for three years and you are the only one in your year who has never once asked me for an extension."

Marek put the second page down on the desk, squared to the edge, and put the third page beside it.

"You'd be offering me this if I thought the fourth beat was in the kit."

"I would be offering you this if I thought the *department* was in the way," Deputy Roos said. "Those are different sentences and you will notice that I said the second one."

"And if I go to a board with the kit?"

"Then I will defend the kit with everything I have, because I have three hundred and forty students inside a building that runs that technique twice a week and I have signed the order, and if it is unsafe, Mr. Senn, then I am the one who is going to have to say so, and I have a family and a job and eleven weeks until the board sits and I have not the least idea what I would do with the answer." She put her hands flat on the desk. "You are asking me to bet a building on a shape you saw in a room and cannot say out loud to anybody who was not already on your side."

He stood in that office with a job offer and a reputation in his hands, and he thought about his own handwriting in a clinic bay six days earlier, in the smallest writing he owned: *do not let wanting press two things together until they look like evidence. If I ever catch myself doing that, the wanting is the answer, not the thing.* He thought about a fourteen-year-old log page in a metal cabinet, and about a hand flat on a wet crate.

"How long would the vendor matter take?" he said.

"Six months. Nine if it goes to the district office. And in six months the plates will have been reissued twice and the thing you counted will be sitting in a shipping crate in a corridor with a new lot number on the label."

"Two lines on the label are blank."

Deputy Roos looked at him very steadily. "What lines?"

"The upstream technique bundle. And the upstream licensor. Both ruled, both empty, drawn the same day with the same rule." He heard himself and kept going anyway, because it was the only ground in the room. "That isn't a company keeping a secret, ma'am. A company that has something to hide writes a name. An empty box means somebody in a depot asked a question once and nobody answered it, and then everybody downstream of them stopped asking, because it is on the form and the form is fine."

Anneke Roos was quiet for a long moment.

"You have looked at a bundle label," she said.

"Thursday night. There's a brass key on a loop that nobody asked for back."

"Then the department is going to want that key," she said, "and the department is going to want the crate locked, and I am going to have to do that, and I am sorry, because I can see exactly what you did and I cannot let it stay open in a store room behind the porters' cage." She took a breath. "But I am not going to have it reissued."

She said the last part looking straight at him, and she said it as an offer, and it was the first thing anybody in that building had offered him that was not made of a record.

Then she squared the three papers on her desk and put her pen on top of them, and waited, because she was a person who understood that a room with nothing on the table is a room where a person takes whatever is nearest.

"Before you go," she said. "Say it out loud. Not to me — to the door, or to the paper, or to God if you are the sort. Because you are going to be asked this again, by somebody junior, and you are going to have to answer it again, and I would rather you had a sentence that was yours than a sentence you assembled each time under pressure."

Marek looked at the three papers. A job. A line on a transcript he could not fix. A building full of three hundred and forty people.

"I'm going to say the thing and you are going to tell me it's stupid."

"I am not going to tell you it's stupid. I have got a department of forty people and a bad feeling about a supplier, and I have been stupid about institutions for nineteen years, so go on."

"I'm not going to take the apprenticeship."

Deputy Roos did not move.

"Not because it's a bad offer. It isn't. It's the best thing anybody in this building has put in front of me in three years, and you knew that when you made it, and you made it anyway, and I have thought about nothing else since the Thursday you handed it to me." He put two fingers on the top sheet without moving it. "Because you have put a price on the sentence, and the price is that I have to stop asking. And the second I have signed, the day I stop asking is the day Tam Arrick has a scarred forearm and a transcript that says the equipment was sound, and nobody will ever know that a decision was made in an office, because a man in a good job who is not asking is not a story. That's the whole product. That's what you just described to me, and you described it honestly, and I want to say that you described it honestly."

"Then what do you propose," Anneke Roos said. "Because there is a form on the desk and the form is not going to fill itself."

"I'm not proposing anything. I have nothing. I have four photographs of a blank box and a boy in a bed." He took his hands off the paper. "I don't know how to make it cost you something you can't afford, and I know that if I file a complaint this month you reissue the plates in a new lot with a clean label and there is nothing left in the city to count, and I know that if I wait, the term ends and my scholarship review opens on a Tuesday. So I am going to have to do this the bad way, which is slowly, out of the building, with three strangers, and I already know that is not going to be good enough and I am doing it anyway."

"And if I offer you the apprenticeship on Thursday instead, and the review on Friday, and no conditions at all?"

"Then I would take it," Marek said, "and in eight months somebody would ask me what I was too busy with, and I would not have an answer, and I would be a man with a good job and a habit." He stopped. "That's the honest reason. The other reason is that I said no to Rafi Pell in week one, in my own front room, and the only reason that cost nothing was that he asked me properly first and I had to answer out loud. I am not going to be a person who answers out loud once."

Anneke Roos picked up her pen and put it in her pocket.

"Then I am going to record that you were offered the City maintenance track on its own terms, in this room, with a witness, and that you declined it, and that you did so before you had anywhere else to go." She looked at him for a second longer than the transaction required. "You are aware that I have to write down *why*."

"Write down that I would have taken it if it had cost me nothing to be wrong. That's true, and it's the only version of it I can stand having written down."

She wrote three lines, and turned the form so that he could see it, which she had not done with any of the other papers, and which he noticed, and which he decided not to think about for a long time.

"Get out of my office," Anneke Roos said. "And Senn — do not thank me. A thank-you in this corridor before the end of term will cost me a fortnight I have not got."

Davan Rusk was in the corridor outside room two with a box of return-path markers and a paint pen.

Marek had rehearsed six versions of this on the tram and every single one of them had been terrible.

"Can I ask you about the manual?"

Rusk did not stop unpacking. "Which manual."

"The 3-08. The last reprint." Marek stood too close and knew he was standing too close. "I saw the order. It's in the stores requisition book on the shelf by the copier, and the date is the eleventh of March, and it's signed with your initials."

Rusk put a marker down on the sill.

"Yes," he said.

"Four months before the 4-19 landed."

"I ordered four-oh-eight in March because four-oh-eight is what was on the shelf, and I ordered it because the bookshop had stopped doing the free reissue and it was going to be eight credits a copy, and I put it in myself and I paid out of the department's teaching budget because the department's teaching budget has eight credits in it." He said all of it to the window. "I did not know the 4-19 was coming. It came in a delivery in the summer with a lot number and a form and I signed for it, because I sign for things, Mr. Senn, that is my job, and in September I gave a hundred and eleven students a card off the shelf and a manual out of a box and I did not open the box."

He turned round. There was nothing performative in his face at all. It was the face of a man who had gone through a door and found a room he did not like.

"You want to know if I know," Rusk said. "I know that a fourth beat is a support beat. I have said those words out loud to a classroom of children perhaps ten thousand times, and I said them this term, and I said them to you, and if you have come to tell me I knew, then I have come to the wrong building, because I have been standing in the wrong building for four months and I did not know there was a room."

"You knew the fourth beat wasn't the learner's."

"I knew the fourth beat wasn't the learner's *on the frame*." He said it very carefully, and Marek felt something cold go down his back. "The word is there so that eleven people don't hurt themselves in a room in my absence. If the frame is live and somebody goes down, the person closest to the load says the scope, and the person in the strap lets go, and everybody goes home. That is the design. I have never in twenty-two years had a stop where the word was not available, and I am not going to sit in this corridor and be told by a third-year that I have been the missing part of a practice for twenty-two years when I have been the emergency."

He put the box down.

"Get your evidence out of my building," he said, not unkindly. "And when you have got it, do not bring it to me first. Bring it to somebody who can be harmed by it and can't lose their job. There is a difference and you are not too stupid to have heard it."

The Continuity Office sent a form.

It came by hand at eleven forty, in an envelope with a reference number on it, and a woman in a grey coat delivered it to the front desk and would not come up. It was two pages and it was the plainest piece of paper in the whole week.

**NETWORK PRACTICE INCIDENT NOTIFICATION — CONTINUITY OFFICE**
*Section A. Location. Section B. Practice and revision. Section C. Nature of variance.*
*Section C offers three categories: (i) equipment; (ii) technique variance attributable to the trainee; (iii) technique variance not attributable to the trainee.*

Category (ii) had a tick already in it, in a different ink, in a different hand.

Marek stood in the corridor holding it and looked at that tick for a long time, and then he wrote one line in the load book in his own hand, which was the sentence that turned the next month of his life around:

*They do not want to know. They need it to already be in the box.*

---

The kit store was locked when he got there, at one o'clock, and the crates were not in it.

They were in a steel cage against the far wall with a chain and a padlock. All nine, stacked two high, the wax seals still on them, and the pads on top inside their printed sleeves where he had counted them a week before. There was a laminated card wired to the cage: **TRAINING STOCK — 4-19 — LOCKED PENDING SERVICE. Access: department only. Equipment handling only.**

Locked, not quarantined. A locked crate is a thing that is inconvenient. A quarantine is a thing that is stopped, and stopping things required a form, and a form required somebody to be responsible, and Deputy Roos had a building to run.

There was a man in there with a service case and a folding rule.

"Student?" the man said. "You from the department?"

"Third year. I'm not — I'm not authorized. I was just looking."

"No, mate, nobody's authorized. They ring me, I come, I do the plates, I go." He put the case on the table and took out a card, and gave it to Marek to hold, which nobody had asked him to do. It said: **P. Vance, Field Service, Threadline, Lattice Depot. Service ref SB-4-19-03.**

"Depot's got three of these in your building," Vance said, "and the other two sites, and the clinic on the Saltmarket, so if you've got a bad one I want to hear about it before the next round. When did this batch arrive in here?"

"September. The first week."

"Right, and the label on the crate — was that still on the wax paper when it came in, or had somebody pulled it off? The wax paper."

Marek said, "Why?"

"Because the return contact changed in 4-19 and we put a service notice out on it, and the notice tells people not to file it as a hardware fault, because it's a design change and it isn't covered by the standard warranty and if we get three hardware claims off one building we get a review," Vance said, and he said it fast and comfortably, the way a man explains a thing to somebody he assumes is on the same side. "It's nothing wrong, it's just not a fault. So the label's got to stay on and the notice's got to go in the folder. Was it on?"

"I don't remember."

"Right." Vance stood up and moved toward the cage and got the chain off the padlock, which was unlocked, which meant the sign on the cage was doing the work of a lock. He got the cage door back and put his hand in toward a plate on the top shelf.

"No," Marek said.

Vance stopped.

"Any of them?" Vance said. "Because I've got to service all of them, that's the—"

"I'm not saying it's any of them. I'm asking what's happening first." Marek heard his own voice go flat and did not like it and kept going. "Before anybody touches that, I want everybody in this room to say, out loud, what the purpose is, how long it's for, and what stays behind afterwards. Equipment in, equipment out, nothing taken. And nobody's hands on a plate unless everybody in the room heard them say it."

Vance put his hand back down. He looked at Marek for a second, and then at the door, and then he said, "Are you a union rep?"

"No."

"Then that's not how we do this." But he said it without heat, and he sat down on the bench, and Darla Vint, who had come in off the corridor to ask him whether a room was free after four and was still standing there with her hand on the back of the chair, said:

"I'll write it down." She got a pen out. "Purpose, duration, and what's retained. That's all, is it."

"There's a form for the store room," Vance said. "It's pre-signed, it's for equipment handling, it's in the folder, nobody reads it, everybody signs it."

"Right," Darla Vint said. "I'll put *equipment handling only* on it, and I'll add *no individual practice, no share, no observation* underneath, and the third-year can countersign that as the person who asked, which is a thing I am allowed to do and which means if anybody comes back in a year there's a line with his name on it." She looked up. "Do you want your name on this, Mr. Senn? You don't have to. If you don't, I write *staff present*."

He thought about it, and it took him longer than it should have, because it was the first time in a month he had thought about a piece of paper instead of a person.

"My name," he said.

She wrote it. Vance watched her do it, and then he said, to Marek, not unkindly, "You're a strange one for a third-year," and Marek said, "I'm a third-year who's been in this room twice this week," and Vance said, "Yeah. Well. The wax paper was on, was it."

And Marek said, "I don't remember," and wrote on his forearm, in pencil, with Darla Vint watching and not minding: *asked him. He answered. He wasn't hiding the notice. The notice is why the design change isn't a fault. Get the notice.*

He got it at four o'clock, off the department's own purchasing file, in the corridor outside the exam office, with the assistant in the copy room who was owed forty-one cents and did not ask him anything: the vendor matter docket, with the depot address, the assembly lot, the two blank lines, and at the bottom, in the space where the department records who authorized the spend, a printed name.

**O. VEY, OPERATIONS — THREADLINE.**

And stapled to it, the service notice itself, one page, ref **SB-4-19-03**: *Return contact, revised for district distribution. Not a fault. Do not claim. Prior practice continues unchanged.*

He read that last sentence until it stopped sounding like an instruction and started sounding like an answer, in a copy room with fluorescent light on it and a woman's radio going somewhere in another room.

*Prior practice continues unchanged.*

He photographed it, and then he copied it by hand, and then he went and copied five more things in the space of an hour and forty minutes, sitting on a bench in the copy room with his jacket over the pile so nobody would see what was under it: his own transcript, both pages; the department's stores requisition for the 3-08 manual, the eleventh of March, Rusk's initials; the kit crate count, twenty-four plates, two sets of cards, one booklet, longhand, in his own writing; the bundle label, both empty lines, the lot number; the eleven pages of the instructor notes, of which he copied pages three and four and nine because those were the ones with words on them; and the four hours of day two of a district relay log from fourteen years ago with a typed block at the foot of it, which he had copied off the wall at Spine 7 on the Thursday of week one, in the one hour between putting a notebook down and being told to put it away, and had been carrying around in the book like a man carrying somebody else's letter.

He did not copy the drill record. He did not need to. He had photographed it, and it said *equipment sound*, and that sentence was going to be true for years.

Then he went down to the waterfront, because he had said he would.

Gus Haldane was in the door of a fish office on the west berth with a cup of tea and a manila envelope and a face that had not slept.

Marek put the envelope on the counter.

"That's a copy of the crate label, the card that comes with the plates, my instructor's booklet pages, and a correction of a school record that says the equipment was sound," he said. "Your crew uses those plates. You've got a right to read them."

Gus did not touch the envelope for a while.

"Senn," he said. "I was on that dock. I heard you say a word."

"I know what I said."

"I'd like to have somebody tell me what the word was."

"I can't. Not in a way that'd mean anything. I can tell you the card's got a beat in it where a person's supposed to take their hands off and say what the thing is allowed to have touched, and that the word replaces it, and that the word was the only reason Tam's hands came off, and that he was right to come off and I was wrong to say it, and I don't have a way to make it different yet." Marek put his hand flat on the counter. "Take the envelope. Don't sign anything for it. If anybody from that building comes and asks you to hand it back, don't."

Gus Haldane took the envelope and put it inside his coat.

"There's a room," he said. "At the Saltmarket Exchange. You can leave a thing there and nobody has to sign for it, and there's a woman there who cooks who'll tell you to your face that half of what people bring in there is nonsense." He drank his tea. "She's right, by the way. And she's also the only person I've ever met who can tell the difference."

Marek walked back up the ramp with his hands in his pockets and the copy in one of them and the official record in his head — twenty-four plates, a sealed crate, a corrected sheet, a service notice that said *prior practice continues unchanged*, and a department that would rather have a hardware fault than an answer.

He did not file anything. Not yet. He had understood that afternoon, sitting on a bench in a copy room, that a complaint was a request to somebody else to fix it, and a vendor response to a complaint was a new set of plates in a new lot with a new number and a clean label, and that in nine months there would be nothing left in this city to count.

What he needed instead was not a form. He needed people. He needed three observations of a fourth beat from people who had never been inside the practical building, never been drilled on a frame, and never in their lives been told that the word was the answer — and he had exactly none, and he had eight days, and somewhere in Nacre at that moment there were a great many people doing difficult things with their bodies for money.

He got the tram. He wrote the sum in the load book and then he sat in the front seat with the notebook closed on his knees for forty minutes without opening it, which was the first decision he made in six weeks that was not an emergency.
