# Chapter 4 — Aftertaste

He got home at four in the afternoon on the Thursday of the drill and lay on his bed with his boots on and his forearm over his eyes, and the first thing he found out was that he could not name a screwdriver.

Not refused to. Could not. He reached for the word and there was a place where a word should be, the way there is a place in a step where a stair should be, and he lay there and waited for it the way you wait for a page to turn, and it came after ninety seconds in the wrong shape, as *the green handled thing with the flat on it*, which was not a screwdriver at all, which was a chisel, and which was also not the word.

Rafi came in at half past five with a bag of parts and put it on the bench.

"You've been lying there four hours."

"Ruf. Shut up a minute."

Rafi, who had the single greatest gift of anyone Marek had ever met — the ability to be told to shut up and shut up completely, without sulking, and then to be there — went and sat on the floor with his back against the radiator and waited.

Marek got the notebook.

It was a cheap spiral thing from the Institute's bookshop with a picture of a crane on the front that had come off in the four years he had owned it. He got a pen out of his jacket, and he uncapped it, and he wrote at the top of a blank page, *fourth beat*, and underneath it, on three lines:

*Ask what it is allowed to have touched.*
*Ask who decides what happens next.*
*Say it out loud, where the person doing it can hear.*

That was the shape. That was what had been in the gap for four seconds, when two of him had existed and the shape of the hole had a shape. A question the hands ask out loud, at a point in the sequence where the answer can still change what happens. Not a thought. Not a feeling. Something the body did, in front of another body, at a moment when the other body could still refuse.

He read it back and thought: that is the fourth beat.

Then he thought: that is not the fourth beat.

Because when he wrote it a second time, the line came out *name the limit before you take the weight*, and the third time — he was careful, he ruled a line under the second one, he started a fresh one — it came out *say what you are not touching out loud*, and none of the three was the shape, and the three were not even versions of one shape, they were three different things a person might do at the fourth place, and the third was arguably better technique and had nothing whatsoever to do with what he had felt in that room.

He sat on the edge of the bed and looked at a page of his own handwriting for a long time.

*Eleven-oh-four, drill, room two.* He wrote the date and the place, because that was the sort of thing that might matter later. *Line came for about four seconds. Rafi's half was a load on the back of the heels and a shoulder going in and then a check — he checks with his own hand, he has always done it, nobody taught him. Shape of the hole: a question, asked out loud, at the fourth beat, so the person being held can hear it. Then I said the word. I said it in Rusk's rhythm.*

And then he stopped, because the next line wanted to be about Tam, and he did not have words for Tam that he was willing to put on paper in a room where Tam might come in and pick up a pen.

*Head: gap, two seconds, could not name the floor color. Did it come back? Yes. Took about a minute.*
*Taste: battery. Got four of those, one long, three short, in maybe three weeks of work.*
*Borrowed: the arithmetic. Still going at 4 p.m. Four or five seconds after the fall, still running. Not mine. Do not write down what it was. Do not write down what it was.*

He underlined the last line twice, which was a thing he did with things he was frightened of.

Rafi said, from the floor, "Is it the word?"

"What word?"

"The scope word." Rafi said it carefully, like a man handling something he had been told not to touch. "The thing that stops the fall. Can you give it to me? I want it. I've been thinking about it since Thursday and I can't stop thinking about it, and I ran a lap of the ring road on Saturday and I did the shoulder thing four times and it wasn't the same, and I know it wasn't the same, and I could feel the difference and I want to know what the difference *is*."

"Ruf."

"I'm not being daft. I know how it sounds. Listen to me." Rafi got up onto his knees. "That thing on Thursday. When it happened. That wasn't you. That was — I was holding the strap and there was two of us holding the strap and I could feel what you were about to do before you did it, like a bicycle coming round the corner. Do you know how good that is? Do you know how many hours I've spent on that ring road alone trying to get the balance right? I ran it twice since Thursday and I did the shoulder thing four times and it wasn't the same, and I know it wasn't the same, and I could feel the difference and I want to know what the difference *is*. That's not a trick, that's a *skill*, and I want to learn it, and I'm not going to stop asking you about it, and you can just tell me no."

And there it was, standing in the middle of his own front room in a courier shell, being completely sincere: the first person Marek had ever known who would have said yes without needing to know what the question was.

The word he had been telling himself for three days came up his throat and it fitted. *If I can just become useful enough.* He could hear it in his own voice. He could even hear the warmth in it.

"No," Marek said.

Rafi nodded, immediately, without any of the things a face usually does. "Fine. Noted. When you know, tell me." And that was that, and it was a kindness, and it was also the reason it had to be no today.

"I don't have it," Marek said. "That's the honest answer. I had it for four seconds and I did not do anything."

"You said the word."

"I said *his* word."

Rafi went quiet. Then he said, "Oh," in a completely different voice, and went and sat back down against the radiator, and Marek thought: there. That is the whole of the danger and it took nine seconds.

He had a clinic text he had not answered.

It had come to the transport stream's group on Sunday, from the mobile clinic on the Saltmarket route, about free Thursday practice slots, and then on Monday, because he had not replied to the group, a private one from a woman called Talia Venn who had come to the Institute's induction week in September and stood at the end of his row in room two and asked whether third-year transport students could give twenty minutes on a Thursday. She had a clipboard. She had a laminated card of her own. She had asked him twice, out loud, in front of other people, and both times he had said some version of *this week is impossible*, and the second time she had looked at him for slightly too long and said, "All right," and written something on her clipboard, and gone.

The group message on Sunday had asked who could take a slot. On Monday she had written to him directly: *No pressure from me, and no answer needed if the answer is no. It is genuinely no pressure. If you ever want a Thursday, the Saltmarket route clinic has a room with a working sink and a door that shuts.*

He had gone over the message so often it had worn a groove. He had not typed a reply, and it had gone to the place where messages go when you read them and do not answer, which is not the same place as a refusal, and which everybody who has ever waited for a reply knows about.

He understood — he could see it, he could almost write it down — exactly why he had not answered. It was not that he was busy. It was that she asked people to name things, and he could not think of a single arrangement in his own life that he would have been willing to have named. Not the four hours. Not the eleven credits. Not the way he had eaten a bun in a corridor. Not Evan's card in the tool roll. If a woman with a clipboard asked him to say what he was willing to share, he would have had to say all of it, and then she would have written it on the clipboard, and then it would have been in a file.

So he put the phone face down on the floor and went to get his work gloves, because at half past eight that night he had a shift at the Spine, and the shift was the only place in the city where nobody had ever asked him to name a single thing.

---

Civic Spine 7 at ten past nine, on a Thursday, with four units on the list and one of them a lamp.

He did the lamp first because it was Mrs. Oyelaran's and she was seventy-nine and had already complained once, in writing, and the Spine does not get a second complaint from the same address.

Then he sat down at the bench with the notebook and a cold flat of the department's terrible coffee and tried to do the only thing he had wanted to do for three days, which was to see if the shape would come back.

He could not get it. He tried twice with Rafi's half in mind — the load on the back of the heels, the shoulder going in, the check with his own hand — and the best he got was a fairly good description of a courier changing lanes in rain.

Sera Quill came in at half past nine the way she came in every night, which was without hurry and straight to the wall of notes, where she read the day's entries standing up the way other people read a newspaper.

"You've been on the plates," she said.

"Four units. Lamp's done. Nothing on the plate rack."

"I saw the forms." She took the clip off the top of the pile and read his handwriting on the back of the fault report, both sides, twice. "Third one this month."

"Yes."

"You put it in the file."

"No. I put it through the tray."

"That is not the file." She put the clip back on. "The file is what the department reads. The tray is what I read. Do you know the difference?"

"No."

"The difference is that the tray is a person." She said it without weight, the way she said most things. "Somebody has to be the person, Senn, or it is just a number going into a wall."

She went to the sink and washed her hands, which she did every hour whether or not she had touched anything.

"Can I ask you something technical," he said.

"You can ask."

"I had a line on Thursday. A real one, about four seconds, with a person in it who hadn't agreed to anything except with his hands." He turned the notebook around so she could see the page if she wanted to. "I want to see it again under conditions I control. I'm not touching anyone's practice. I just want to put a plate on the jig and a strap in the frame and have a go at the fourth beat, and I want to know if I can do it at all without a voice. I know I'd be performing it on a thing. I'm not asking for permission to share with a person. I'm asking for a bench."

Sera dried her hands and came and stood next to him and looked at the jig, and the room got about two degrees smaller.

"No," she said.

"It isn't a person."

"No, it isn't." She put one hand flat on the bench, on the wood, right next to his notebook. "That is why it is worse. Senn. Do you know what a return path is?"

"Information about the technique?"

"No." She looked at him with an expression of real, unsentimental patience. "It is a piece of engineering. It is a cut somebody made in a circuit on purpose so that a person could find it in the dark with one hand and know that it was a thing a person had made and not something that grew. A return path is a physical mark in the metal, cut by a maintenance crew, inspected on a schedule, and it has a *location*. It is not an idea. It is not a promise. It is a groove."

She tapped the bench.

"When you do a thing like that on a person, it is not a groove. It is a hope. And a hope in a person is the hardest object in this building to maintain, because you cannot get at it with a screwdriver, and it does not come with a schedule, and when it fails there is nothing to look at afterward." She straightened up. "And you want to find out whether you can do the fourth beat on your own, on a plate, in a room with a door. And if you cannot do it on your own, on a plate, in a room with a door, then you cannot do it at four in the morning on a person who is frightened, and you will have spent your whole life learning a thing that only works when somebody is watching."

"That is not what I'm—"

"And the other thing." She said it more quietly. "You are not doing this on your own plate. Everything in this room goes out to the public network. Every plate on that shelf that gets opened under a live line, a nurse in Crown Terrace sees a record of who was in the room and what the room was doing. If I let you put a four-second line on the jig, I am writing a line into the public record that says Civic Spine 7 ran a private experiment on a crew's equipment, at night, without a partner, with a student who is already on a transcript with two failures on it." She looked at him. "I will not have the public network used as somebody's laboratory. Not while I am the one who signs the shift."

He said, after a while, "That's fair."

"It is fair," Sera agreed. "It is also the only reason you still work here."

She went back to the wall of notes.

He did not work for another hour. He sat at the bench with the coffee going cold and read the wall, because the wall was the one thing at the Spine that had never once told him to hurry, and because his head was still full of a shape he could not get to.

The wall was a hundred and forty clips: printed repair tickets, a shopping list, a child's drawing of a relay, four different handwritings of the same fault on three different crews, a photocopy of a safety notice from nine years ago, and a page that was older and browner than the rest and had been folded twice and pinned flat.

It was a log page. Municipal, from the look of it: a grid of hours down the left, a column for node, a column for what was done. The header said **MARROW FALLS — DISTRICT RELAY LOG — DAY 2** and the ink at the top said the date, which was fourteen years ago, when Marek had been eight years old and had not yet been in a room with a stuck blind.

The entry at the row for 04:20 read, in a careful clerk's hand:

*Node 9-Barrow no answer. Carried last approved civic sequence as provisional response. Bridge held. Field crew did not report for second hour; no fault found at node.*

He read it until the words went flat, and on the third time through he read it the way you read something when you have heard it before today and did not know where.

*Last approved civic sequence.*

At eleven o'clock this morning, in a room with a stuck blind, he had read that exact phrase in a header on a laminated card and had gone past it, because it was a header, because headers are where the rules go that are not about today.

He got up. He went and got the notebook and wrote the sentence down exactly, and then went to the bottom of the log page, where the day's entries were signed the way municipal days are signed, in a confident slanted hand with a lot of letters joined up at the top.

*I. Sorn.*

And below that, typed into a printed block near the foot of the page, small and even and official:

*Continuity design office — resident architect, Iona Sorn. Emergency design liaison to Ashfields response.*

Marek did not know the name. He had never seen it. He wrote it down anyway in the notebook, both forms, and then he stood there for a while with the pen resting on the paper and the very specific feeling of a person who has picked up a thread and does not yet know what it is attached to.

He became aware that Sera Quill was standing four feet away with her back to the wall of notes and her arms folded, looking at the page, and had been for some time.

"Where did that come from?" she said.

"The log. It's older than the casework." He nodded at the cabinet. "It's in there, in the day book for Marrow Falls, day two."

"Give it back to me."

"Why?"

"Because it is not yours and because I do not want it read." She said it flatly and without any threat in it at all, which was the worst way she could say a thing. "Give it back to me and do not ask me about it."

Marek did not give it back to her. He heard himself decide not to, and he heard how quickly he decided, and he put the notebook away faster than he needed to.

"Row four-twenty," he said. "The same phrase is on the drill script. I saw it this morning. I don't know what it means."

Sera Quill looked at him for a long moment, and her face did something complicated and shut itself back down.

"You're tired," she said. "You've had a week. Put the notebook away, Senn."

He put the notebook away.

At one in the morning he signed the day book, and then he turned the notebook back to the last written page and ruled a new one and titled it, because a column needed a name and because he was the sort of person who needs his columns named:

*LOAD — week 1*

*Thurs 11:04, drill room 2. Line 4 sec, one partner, hands only, no words, no licence. Spoke Rusk's word into a falling classmate. He let go because I said it. Cost: headache, gap 2 sec (floor colour), metallic x4, borrowed arithmetic 5+ min, not mine.*
*Thurs 16:10, back at the room. Could not name a screwdriver for ninety seconds. Slept none of the afternoon. Yellow line visible = about a 4-day mark for me. Ask somebody what the four-day mark is.*
*Thurs 21:10–01:00, Spine. Four units, lamp done, plates done. Asked for the jig. Refused. Sera was right about all of it, including the part I did not want.*

Then, at the bottom of the page, in the space that was left, three lines, and under them a heading he gave a number and a question of his own:

*Three weeks to Thursday of week four.*
*No license, no share, nothing to put in the log that anybody reads.*
*Get the fourth beat. It is the last thing on the card.*

*Do not know:*
*Whether the word I said out loud is the word the drill card means, and if it is not, which of the two made the room work.*

He read that last sentence over. It was a good sentence. It was the sentence of a man who had a job.

He had to walk a long way to the tram with it, and by the time he got to Pell Street the borrowed arithmetic had finally gone quiet, and the head had gone quiet with it, and he lay in the dark on his back with his gloves under the pillow and understood, with total clarity and no comfort whatsoever, that he had done everything right on Thursday. That four people had watched him hesitate and one of them had sat down in a plastic chair to tell him so kindly. That he had held a classmate's fear in his own chest for five minutes. That he had said a word he did not own into a boy who let go because of it.

And that if it had been his mother on that ladder, he would have done exactly the same, and it would have worked, and it would have been the best thing he had ever done.
