# Chapter 10 — A Different Shoulder

The Saltmarket Exchange had been a ferry waiting room for sixty years and it still smelled like one, under the smoke and the vinegar and the fish. Long trestle tables, a parts bin along one wall, a noticeboard with a February on it, and four cookers at the back in a room that had been a kiosk. Any Nacre person could walk in off the street. That was the entire point of the place, and on a Thursday evening at six it was the loudest room Marek had been inside since term started.

He came in at ten past six with Gus Haldane's envelope in a satchel, because he had not thought of anywhere else to take it, and because a man had told him on a wet dock that he could leave a thing here and nobody would have to sign for it.

He got as far as the second table.

"Senn." The woman at the trestle with the tea urn was perhaps twenty-seven, in a cook's jacket with the sleeves pushed up, and she had been looking at him since he came through the door. "You're the fourth-year — you're third-year. You've got that thing where somebody's told you to be careful with a doorframe."

"Sorry?"

"You came in and you looked at the two exits. Everybody who does that is either a professional or in trouble." She put down a cloth. "Are you in trouble?"

"I'm looking for somebody who can do a boundary release."

At the table nearest them, four men who had been eating stopped chewing. The room got a half-second quieter, which for that room was a considerable achievement.

"Say the rest of it out loud," the woman said, "because those four are going to want to sell you something."

"Dessa," said one of the men. "Don't."

"I am not being clever, I am being practical." Dessa Kwan came around the table and put her hands on her hips and looked at Marek, who was twenty-two, sleep-deprived, in a work shirt with a student card on a lanyard, holding a satchel. "That is my name as well. I run the kitchen and I teach nobody for money. Say the rest of it out loud."

"My classmate had his arm broken on a dock on Tuesday. There's a card in that crate he was taught from, and the fourth beat on the card isn't a step in the technique, it's a word somebody else says. Nobody in the Institute will say it out loud because if they do they're saying a product is unsafe. I've got a week. I need three people who were never taught that card to show me a thing they do with their own hands, so that I can put three of them in a row in front of a board and say *look, it can be finished more than one way.*"

Dessa Kwan looked at him for about four seconds.

"Right," she said. "Sit down. Don't put your bag on the table, it's got somebody else's fish money in it, put it on the floor." She turned around. "Nobody's doing anything for you. First: this room is not a stage. Second: there is nothing happening this evening that anybody has agreed to watch, and if I see anybody performing a thing so that a student can write it down, I will put the kettle on the head of it." She said that last part cheerfully. "Third: you have not told me one thing about him. What's his name."

"Tam Arrick."

"What was he doing on the dock?"

"Taking a load. He works nights at a kitchen and the department sent him to do a check with a courier crew."

"Is his arm any good?"

"They don't know till tomorrow. They said two tendons."

Dessa's face changed. Not much. It was more that something left it.

"Then you'll not be asking him tonight," she said. "You'll be asking him tomorrow, with him in front of you, and you'll shut up for the first two minutes. Now — you watch, and you don't ask anybody for anything, and if a thing goes past in here tonight that you want to ask about, you can ask me about it tomorrow, and I will tell you whether I want to be asked. Deal?"

"Deal."

---

He watched for two hours and he wrote four lines.

The first was at ten past seven, when Dessa took over the hot pass and started running a Thursday service on four burners with two other people, and Marek stood eleven feet away and could not follow it and could not stop it.

It went: a check, a call, a lift, a check. Every plate that came off was checked by a person and named aloud to a second person, and every time Dessa changed hands with a pan she said what the pan was allowed to have done, out loud, in the middle of a service, at working speed, and then the other person had to answer before they took the weight.

At eight-fifteen she put a spoon down on the pass and turned around.

"That's it," she said, to nobody. "I am out of that pass. Gwilym, it's yours, I've said what it can touch."

"Gwilym, it's yours, you've said what it can touch," said Gwilym.

And Dessa Kwan walked away from a hot pass in the middle of a Thursday service and went and had a cigarette, and Marek stood there and understood that he had just watched the fourth beat, performed nine times, by a person whose name was not on any card in the practical building.

He wrote: *Kitchen. Hands off. Say what it touched, out loud, mid-service. The other person answers. Then the decision is theirs and she goes and has a cigarette.* And then, underneath, in brackets: *(They are not asking each other whether they may. They are telling each other what already happened. Different from a share. Different from anything I can put on a form.)*

The second was at a quarter to nine and it was nothing at all, and it was the most useful thing he saw all night.

A fish porter of about sixty came through with a crate on his shoulder and put his other hand out flat against the edge of a trestle table, and the table went over on its bad leg, and the crate came down, and the man's hip went out from under him, and he took all of it on his left side and the crate came to rest on his chest.

Marek was six feet away and did not move, because he could not move, because the man did not need him.

He got his hand off the table frame. He said, "That's the floor, it's cold, it hurts, I can hear the ice." And he lay there and let the man's knee come up. And then, after a moment, he said the second thing: "It's off you. It's on the crate. It's not on you anymore. Get it off yourself or leave it, it's your call, I've got you."

And the man said, "Leave it," and laughed, and lay there for another half minute, and then got himself up with his own two hands and a table leg, and picked the crate up off his own chest and put it down, and went back to work, and nobody in that room said a word about it and two of them had clearly seen it.

Marek wrote: *Fish market, 20:45. Man of about sixty, bad hip, took a full crate on his chest. Did not ask a single person for anything. Said two things out loud: what had touched him, and what was off him now. Then he decided. Nobody taught him that. Nobody is going to teach him that. He has done it for forty years and it is not a technique, it is a man who works with his hands.*

And under it, because he had learned one thing in a month: *(I do not know his name. I do not know if he has ever held a brace. I do not know if he would do it again on request. Asking him is a whole conversation and I do not get to have it standing in a doorway.)*

---

He found Rafi at ten past nine, at the ramp, doing what Rafi did on Thursdays, which was help a market crew get forty crates of produce off a truck before the wholesalers started shouting.

Rafi saw him coming and put the crate down and wiped his hands on his trousers, and Marek had a whole speech ready and it was gone in the first three seconds, because Rafi said:

"Yes, obviously, whatever it is, yes."

"Ruf—"

"You've been white for three weeks and you've been sleeping on my floor with your boots on, and you had a thing on your face on Tuesday like somebody had put a hand inside your head." Rafi pulled his hood down. "I'm saying yes. I'm not even waiting for it. Ask me after."

Marek stopped.

That was all. He stopped, and it took him about two seconds to do it, and he had spent three weeks learning that he could do it and here it was again.

"Answer me properly," he said. "Because I am not going to be the thing that decides what you meant."

"Ow," said Rafi cheerfully. "Fine. Go on then."

"Right. In full." He had it written on a card in his jacket, which he had written on the tram, and he did not read it, because reading it would have made it a form. "Purpose: I want to see a real thing that you do with your body and find out whether the fourth part of it is in your body or whether it comes from somebody's mouth. Duration: about ten minutes, and if you want to stop at any point, you stop, and I stop, and we don't talk about it afterwards and you don't owe me a story about why. What stays: nothing. What goes: everything, tonight, by morning, including the shape, including — " he heard himself and made himself finish it " — including the four seconds from the drill. You did not agree to those. You agreed with your hands in the middle of a falling boy and I have never once asked you about it and I am not going to use it."

Rafi Pell stood on a loading ramp in a courier's shell with a crate in each hand and looked at Marek for a while.

"Use only," he said.

"Use only."

"Right." Rafi put the crates down. "That's a yes. I'll do the step."

---

He did the step on the wet ramp with the truck behind them and the wholesalers shouting from the far end, because that was where he did it.

It was a long slow turn off a kerb onto a ramp with a load in both hands, and it was genuinely good — better than good, better than anything the Institute had shown a third-year, two years of a working man's body solving a problem four hundred times a night.

Marek watched the whole of it and wrote the timings and could not write the last part, because the last part was not there.

Anchor, the heel, the road. Load, the weight coming up over the hip, the shoulders going down and back. Close, the hands finding the two points where the load is allowed to sit and putting it there.

And then the ramp.

And then a man on a wet ramp with two crates in his hands, at the top of a load, with the whole of the trouble still in front of him, and nowhere at all to put anything.

"He's going to put his shoulder on the wall," Marek said.

Rafi glanced at him. "How do you know that."

"Because you've got a mark on your left shoulder blade the size of a dinner plate and you've had it for two years and you told me once it was a chair." Marek pointed. "There. Every time. You go to the wall."

Rafi looked at the wall. He was quiet for a second.

"Aye," he said. "Two year now. Near enough." He put his hands out and took an imaginary load and walked up the ramp in the air, and at the top of it he turned his left shoulder into the wall, and the whole thing settled, and he came out of it easy as a man coming out of a doorway. "That's the step, mate. That's how I do it. It works. It's good, actually. Ask anybody on the ring."

"Ruf. There's no beat in that."

"There's a wall."

"There's a wall and there's you, and every single time you do it, somebody in that scheme gets to decide where the wall is." Marek stopped, because he heard his own voice, and it was his mother's voice, and he made himself finish more slowly. "Sorry. Sorry. That's — I don't mean it like that. I mean: it's the only place in that step where something gets decided, and you decided it with a wall and a shoulder, and you've decided it the same way two thousand times, and you call it technique, and it *is* a technique, and it's the only one anybody's taught you, and I'm telling you it's shaped exactly like the thing on that card, and I don't know what to do about that yet and that's not your fault."

Rafi put the invisible load down.

"Huh," he said, and then, with the good grace that had always been the most aggravating thing about him: "Well, that's the first useful thing anybody's said about it in two year. Do you want me to do it without the wall?"

"No. No, definitely not. I don't want to see you do it without the wall on a ramp, and I don't know what happens, and I'm not going to be the man who finds out on a Tuesday night." Marek pulled his own card out and looked at it and put it away again. "I didn't come here to fix your step, Ruf. I came to see if you had a fourth beat, and you don't, and I'm not allowed to give you one, and the only reason I can say that out loud is because a woman made me give something back last week."

"Yeah?" said Rafi, immediately, with both ears up. "Which woman?"

"The one with the clipboard."

"Course it is," said Rafi, hugely pleased, and Marek laughed out loud on a loading ramp, and it was the first time in three weeks that anything had been funny.

---

The ward was eleven beds and it was twenty to midnight on the Thursday, the lights turned down to a strip, and a boy of twenty sat up in bed four with his right forearm in a cast from the elbow to the knuckles, white, and enormous, and eleven centimetres of it.

Two tendons. He would not have the operation explained to him twice, which the registrar on duty found charming and would later describe as *not entirely oriented* in a note.

Marek sat down on the plastic chair. He had four sentences and had brought none of them.

"Third tendon's intact," said Tam. "Can't move the middle finger. Everything else goes. Six weeks cast, three months before I'll hold anything heavier than a kettle, and my hands are my whole job, so." He looked at the cast. "They reckon I'll get it back. They were careful to say *we reckon*, which is the way they say a thing they don't want to be sued over."

"Tam—"

"I'm not finished." He shifted, and worked out where to put his good arm. "I want to write it. The recovery. Not a plan the department gives me — a plan I write, and I hand it to them, and they have to do it my way or they have to say in writing why not."

Marek put his hand flat on the edge of the bed, on his own knee.

"That's — " he started, and then heard what he was about to do. He was about to say *that's dangerous*, and *that's a lot of pressure*, and *you should get a physio*, and every one of those was true and every one of them was a way of taking the thing off a person who had just been told that his own arm was going to come back.

"Okay," he said. "Go on."

"I need an observer." Tam said it to the ceiling. "I need somebody who watches me do it, once a day, at the start, and writes down what actually happened. Not a doctor. Not you."

Marek said, "No."

"Not yet," said Tam. "Hear the whole thing. The department's physio is going to give me four exercises and I'm going to do them four times a day for a year and I will not know whether I'm getting better or whether I'm just getting more careful. If I'm getting more careful, I've lost it, and I want to know that early, and a doctor can't tell me because a doctor is not allowed to make me fail." He turned his head. "I want somebody who does the thing properly, in front of me, on purpose, at working speed, who was never taught that card and has never heard of it, and who will say *no, that wasn't a release* if I fake one."

"Who?"

"I don't know," said Tam. "I haven't got anybody. That's why I'm telling you on a Thursday night in a hospital with a cast on, because you are the only person who has any idea what I'm talking about, and because you'll go and find one, and you won't find one for me, because that's not your job, and I want you to be the one who has to try."

Marek sat in the plastic chair and did not say anything for a while.

"Can I put it like this," he said. "I can't promise you I'll find somebody who never had the card. There's no such person that I know of. But I can promise you that if I find somebody, they don't get asked by me to be nice to you, and they don't get paid by me, and they don't get told what they're watching, and they get told the truth, which is that you may never finish without a voice and that that's not a moral failure and that you will be able to do almost everything you did in June."

"You can't promise that."

"No."

"Right," said Tam, and lay back. "Then you go and ask somebody tomorrow, and it's a Thursday, and there's a room down at the market with a woman who runs a kitchen and cooks for a living."

Marek's head came up.

"How do you know about the room?"

"Because I washed up in that kitchen for two years," said Tam, "and everybody from the market goes in there, and if I want somebody in that room who was never taught anything, the fish market's the place, and there's an old man called Farrow who can't get his hip over a kerb anymore and takes every crate in the place like it weighs nothing, and I've watched him do the thing a hundred times and I've never once heard him ask anybody for anything." He shut his eyes. "Go and ask Farrow."

---

He did not go and ask Farrow. He wrote it in the load book on the tram and he wrote what he did not know at the bottom of the page, which was a thing he had started doing in week three and had never told anybody about, and which by then was the only part of his life that was in order.

*Week 6, Thursday.*
*3 lines visible in one evening, none of them mine:*
*Kitchen (Dessa K). Says what the pan touched, mid-service. Other person answers. She puts it down and leaves. Ran 9 times. Not a share. Not a form.*
*Market porter, 20:45, unknown name, 60s, bad hip. Took a full crate on his chest. Told himself what had happened and what was off him, out loud, then decided. 40 yrs, no teacher, no idea it's a technique.*
*Ruf. Anchor, load, close, wall. No fourth beat. Compensating two years with L shoulder against a wall and calling it the step. First victim. Not my opponent.*
*Directly compared: 1.*

*Do not know:*
*Farrow's name is Farrow. I have never spoken to him. I do not know if he will do it twice. I do not know if he would do it once for me. I do not know whether asking him is a gift or a theft and I will not know until I stand in front of him.*
*Do not know whether any of the three are independent. Kitchen, market, courier: three trades, three rooms, three sets of hands. But every working technique in this city comes from somebody, and I have no way yet of telling a thing that grew out of a practice from a thing that was installed into one. I need a fourth party and a way of asking that isn't my own wanting.*

*Thursday of week 7 is in seven days.*
*The plate is locked. Locked is not stopped. 4-19 is in two satellite sites and on a shelf in a box on wheels at Saltmarket, and a nurse has fitted 26 of them since September.*
*And there is nobody, anywhere inside that building, who can finish a release without somebody's voice coming out of it.*

*Which means on Thursday I have to walk into the exam hall and do the one thing I know for certain I cannot do, and I have a week, and I do not have a technique, and I have never had a technique, and what I have is three strangers and a boy in a cast.*

He got off the tram at Pell Street at twenty to two in the morning, and Rafi was asleep on his back with the courier bag on the floor, and Marek lay down in the dark with his work gloves under the pillow and did not sleep at all, because for the first time in six weeks the problem in front of him was bigger than the thing in his own chest, and there was no version of being useful that solved it, and somewhere in Nacre at that moment there were about four hundred thousand people who had spent their whole lives getting good at things without ever once being taught that they were techniques.

He got up at six and wrote a list of five names and one of them was Farrow, and then he did something he had not done in three years, which was get on a tram at ten past six in the morning with nowhere to be and no work, because the Spine's day shift did not start until eleven and he was going to be early everywhere for a day.
