# Chapter 2 — The Practicum

Room two had been a store room in a previous life and still had the store room's ambitions. The floor was poured grey resin, scuffed to a matte in the traffic lanes, with tape lines laid down in four colors to mark where a person was supposed to stand at each stage of something. Along the left wall, a rack of standardized training cards in plastic sleeves, each one printed with a sequence in four columns and a number in the corner. Along the right, four rescue-brace kits on a shelf, and above them, in a rack that had been bought with someone else's grant, a set of practice plates.

The blind on the tall window was stuck a third of the way down, so the room had a stripe of hard white light across it at head height that made everyone look like they were standing in a fluorescent river.

Marek got there at nine-oh-five. He put his bag on the floor by the wall, not under the bench, because under the bench was where the previous class left things and the previous class had stolen a plate stirrup.

Nine-twelve. Group C was twelve, and by nine-eighteen there were eleven of them in the room with Rusk's own name written on the sheet below them, which is how the department had always run a third-year drill. The door opened and Odile Fenn came in with her hair wet and a paper cup, said "he is going to make us do it twice, he always makes us do it twice," and went to stand in the yellow tape. Then Tam Arrick came in already in his work boots, which was against the rules and which nobody would mention, because Tam was a third-year who pulled double shifts at a kitchen on the Saltmarket and slept through the mornings, and the department had long since decided that a boy who can stand up is a boy who can be taught.

Tam's face had the particular gray of a person who has been awake since two. He said, "You saw the roster?"

"I saw the roster."

"He sent a note at midnight." Tam dropped his bag. "Group C, room two, full kit, and if you're not here at nine-fifteen he counts the roll twice. I have never in my life wanted to be in a room more."

"He's counting the roll twice because last term somebody logged attendance wrong."

"He told me that. He told me that at eleven at night, in writing, with a number attached."

Rafi arrived at nine-sixteen, out of breath, in a courier's shell with the reflective stripe still on it, carrying a ratchet strap he did not own. He had been assigned to the ground team by the inter-faculty roster because transport students are the only ones at the Institute strong enough to hold a load and used to being told to. He took one look at the room and one look at Marek and said, "How bad."

"Full kit," Marek said.

"Full kit," Rafi said, with the flatness of a man who had heard a price before a service.

At nine-eighteen, Davan Rusk came in.

He was forty-seven and had been in the practical department for twenty-two years, which meant he had taught in this building when the floor was green and the window was a different shape, and it showed in the way he moved — he never adjusted for the room, he simply assumed it was the room he remembered and it turned out to be true. He carried no bag. He carried a clipboard, and clipped to the clipboard was a laminated card the length of a ruler, and he set it on the front desk at an angle so the whole class could see it, which Marek appreciated more than he could have said.

"Good," Rusk said. "All of you. Group C, this is the first scored-sequence rehearsal of the term, and in three and a half weeks some of you are going to stand in the exam hall and do a version of this on your own with a board watching, and the board does not care how you feel about any of it."

Somebody at the back laughed and stopped.

"Rescue brace," Rusk said. "Group C, mounted load. Tam, you're on the ladder, and you come down on the count, and the group takes you. Do not put your weight on the dummy's neck, I have seen it happen. Beating, assembly, load, and release, in that order, and the order is not a suggestion."

He pointed at the third-year on the ladder, who was Tam, because Tam was tall and had the only clean hands in the room. Tam went up four rungs and clipped his fall line to the overhead rail. The dummy stood on a rig at the foot of the ladder, two and a half meters up, a heavy training torso with sand in the pelvis, and its whole job was to be the thing a falling person's weight was transferred into. The brace was laid out on the floor in the order it was supposed to be picked up, so that a group learning it learned the order with its hands before anybody said the word order out loud.

Marek knelt.

The plate came off the shelf and into his hands and his whole life narrowed to about eight inches of mineral fiber.

It was the same plate he had pulled a scorched contact out of two nights ago. Not the same unit — a different one, from the shelf, out of the crate — but the same make, the same batch, the same slight grain in the fiber. The housing seated true. The copper return contact came up dull and clean. The strap was webbing with a steel cam buckle, eleven centimeters wide, and the threadline printed on the shoulder of it in a font that had never once had to apologize for anything.

Beating. Anchor, heel pressure, floor. Load, the two-handed lift, the brace taking the weight through the return contact. Close, the strap in three passes, alternating, so the tension walked around the frame instead of pulling one corner crooked. And then the fourth.

"Scope," Rusk said from the front.

"Scope," said the room.

And Marek waited.

Nothing answered. There was no fifth motion to make, no contact to touch, no place in the sequence where the body had to do anything at all. Three beats closed the brace, and the fourth was a word.

"From the front," Rusk said. "Marek. Go."

Marek did it correctly.

That was the part he could not explain afterward. He put his heel down and took the load in two hands and set the return contact where it belonged, and he walked the strap in three alternating passes, and he felt the frame come alive under his fingers the way a good door closes — no rattle, no guess, one honest movement. He felt the practice in it close too. The weight went into the plate and the plate took it and the copper took it and there was a moment — not longer than a heartbeat — when the whole assembly answered him back, present and complete, the way a held note answers.

He had never felt that before. Not once in three years of passing practicals. Not once at six in the morning at Spine 7 with a plate in a cradle.

He let go and stood up.

Nobody said anything, which was how he knew. Odile was looking at her hands. Two of them were at the back with their mouths slightly open. Rafi, on the ground side of the yellow tape, had stopped shifting his weight.

"Well," Rusk said, and there was nothing in his voice at all, which from Rusk was a considerable event. "That is the shape. That is what it looks like. Somebody tell the third-years what happens when it does not."

He did not say *good*. Later, alone in the corridor, Marek would understand that this was a gift, and would also understand that a gift from Rusk meant Rusk had not seen it before either, and would not follow either.

"Again," Rusk said. "Full sequence, group, from the top."

They ran it four more times. On the third, Tam's foot rang the ladder and Rusk stopped the whole room to explain, patiently, for two full minutes, what a foot is for. On the fourth, Rusk worked the room one person at a time and got it out of Tam properly, in two goes, with a hand under his elbow that made it look easy. Marek, on the ground, took the load and the beat and the close, and at the fourth beat he waited every single time, and every single time nothing came, and every single time Rusk said "Scope" and the room said "Scope" and the drill simply went on to the reset.

He filed it. He was tired and his shoulder was a hot wire under the collarbone and there was nothing in the world more useful to him than an explanation, so he filed it under *things I will look at when I have forty minutes*, and that was a lie he told himself at eight-forty in the morning and believed until eleven.

The script card sat on the front desk the whole time, clipped to its board, angled toward the class, its header line clear as anything from the middle of the room.

*In local drills, where a node does not answer, the last approved civic sequence is used as the provisional response.*

Nobody looked at it. It was a header. Headers are where the rules go that are not about today.

At eleven-oh-four Rusk said, "Swap. Tam, you're on the ladder, Odile, you're on the plate, Marek, you're the load and you're calling it. Real order, real timing, nobody rushes, and if the load says stop you stop."

Rusk stepped back to the wall. Rafi took the ground side of the line with his ratchet strap and stood on the tape like a man who had been told where to stand and was going to be irritated about it later.

Marek took the load. Eighty kilos of training dummy on the rig above him, an unknown quantity of boy coming down a ladder, and a shoulder that had been wrong since a job on Wednesday.

"Slow," he said. "One rung. Tam, one rung at a time and say it out loud, because if you say it I can hear you and if I can hear you I know where you are."

"Okay." Tam climbed. One rung, said, one rung, said. Odile came up off the tape and began the beat.

At the second rung Tam's boot went off the rail.

It was not fast. That was the thing nobody tells you. The ladder did not shoot away from him. It leaned, four or five degrees, and kept leaning, and there was a full second and a half in which four people in the room could have done something ordinary and had instead begun to count, because the count is in the body and it starts before anybody decides to start it.

Marek's own count arrived in the middle of it, out of three years of practice, in Rusk's cadence, from nowhere he had summoned.

Anchor. Load. Close.

And then his mind went to the fourth place, the way a foot goes to a stair that isn't there, and waited for the word, and the word did not come from anywhere, because the word was supposed to come from a man against the far wall who had not opened his mouth yet.

Two seconds wide. That was the size of it. He could feel the width of it exactly, the way you can feel the width of a gap in the dark.

The rig came off its stop with a clang. Odile's hands came off the plate. Tam went out and down and out.

Marek's tongue filled with the taste of a battery, and for a second and a half the room had no name and no color.
