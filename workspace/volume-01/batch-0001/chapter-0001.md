# Chapter 1 — Night Shift Ledger

The night list at Civic Spine 7 was a clipboard on a nail, and Marek read it the way other people read weather.

Two exits. One at the corridor's east end, steel, good. One behind the parts cage, aluminium, sticks in wet weather unless you knew to lift before you pulled. He lifted. He had been lifting for two years and nobody had ever asked him to.

Four units on the list. One flagged urgent, a clinic room on the Ashfields line with a sticking relay plate that made the techs nervous, because nervous techs called the Spine instead of doing the job themselves. That one was twenty minutes of work if he did it now and an hour and a half of arguing if he left it until six.

The other three were ordinary. Two practice plates and a door buzzer on the third floor of a walk-up in Nine Bridges.

He took the plates first, because plates were the ones that got reused.

The bench lamp threw a circle about the size of a dinner plate onto the scarred wood. Under it the unit sat in a cradle, opened, its cover face down on a folded rag. Mineral fiber, pale grey, thin as a coaster. Copper return contact at the base, dulled to the color of an old coin. Everything about it said manufactured, said Tuesday, said a price tag in a department store.

He hooked the fine driver into the contact block and eased it up a quarter turn. The copper gave.

There was a way to describe what he was looking at that other repairers used. Arcing. A scorched contact. A unit that had been run hot or shipped wet or installed by somebody in a hurry. Every term of it was true and none of them helped him, because what he could see was not the burn. What he could see was the shape the damage had made in the practice the plate carried — a place where the sequence went out and did not come back, like a sentence with the last word cut off.

Looking at it put a faint sour taste on his tongue and made the lamp's circle swim sideways for a moment before settling.

He had never told anybody about that. There was no good way to say it in a maintenance log.

"Two minutes," he said to nobody. "It's a contact, not a fault."

The third one this month. That was the part that stuck in his throat. Spine 7's practice stock came out of the same crate the Institute's practical department used for drills, because six years ago somebody had signed an equipment-sharing annex and nobody had ever read it since. Whatever the Institute taught three hundred students a week, the Spine's crews were holding the same plates in their hands at two in the morning.

He cleaned the contact, dressed it with a copper dressing strip, tightened the block to torque, and ran the plate through a nine-cycle check with a dummy strap on the jig. It went green on the seventh. It went green on the eighth. It stayed green on the ninth, which it had not done in two weeks, and the copper warmed under his thumb at a temperature he could live with.

He wrote the fault in his own hand on the back of the form.

That was the whole trick, and it was a small one. Fault report, front: equipment returned to stock, no fault, no vendor, nothing. Then he turned the form over and wrote what he had actually seen, in the space behind the endorsement block where the office kept its routing notes because there was never room on the front. He wrote the date, the unit serial, the batch stamp, the arcing pattern, the corrected action. He wrote *contact block scorched on arrival from crate; third unit this month; not a random fault.* He signed it and dated it and pushed it through the tray in the cage door.

That was the first time in the volume that he chose a limit over a gain, and it cost him about nine minutes he did not have.

The second plate was a clean job. The buzzer needed a washer and thirty seconds.

Then the ashfields clinic job, because he was tired and because tired people make the good decision more often than proud ones do. Twenty-two minutes. Green on the first try, which was humiliating.

At 03:10 he washed his hands in the sink, dried them on the roll, and put his work gloves back on before he took them off, which he knew was stupid, because that meant he had to take them off again. His shoulder had been wrong since a courier job he'd taken at six the previous evening, a jammed gate on a service ramp in Nine Bridges, forty minutes of levering with the wrong arm. It had gone from a dull ache to a bright small pain under the collarbone when he lifted anything above his chest, and it had been getting better all night, and now it was not.

He had not eaten since a bun at two in the afternoon. He looked at the vending machine in the corridor, decided against it, and then bought a bun from it anyway, and ate it standing up, and it did nothing.

On the bench under the lamp, where he had set it to keep it out of the way, there was a transit sequence card.

It was older than he was. Grey card stock gone the color of a nickel, the corners soft, a shallow scratch through the middle of the printed beats where somebody had gone over it with a file. Four beats printed along the top. A fifth line ruled and left blank, and the blank was not a printing error, because the ruled line is shorter than the others on purpose — somebody had gone over the top of it with a fine file so it would not print.

His father's card. Marek had never asked where it came from. It had been in the tool roll in the hall closet of his mother's flat in Nine Bridges for as long as he could remember, and it had been in his own tool roll since he was nineteen, and he used it every week for what it is: a shim. It is the right thickness, the right flexibility, the right nothing. You slide it under a mineral fiber plate to keep the frame square while you torque the block, and the plate does not creep, and the copper does not twist.

Four beats and a blank fifth, and the file mark that says somebody decided the blank should stay blank.

He had tried once, at fifteen, to ask Lena about it. She had been folding a cloth bag, and her hands had kept moving, and she had said, *Your father kept the things he needed,* and then she had asked whether his rent was due. He had let the subject go, and so had she, and the silence between them had gotten a little thicker in that place and stayed there.

He slid the card under the last unit, squared the frame, and finished the job.

At 03:20 he signed the day book, which was the ledger the Spine kept, and the word *ledger* was on the sign over the door along with the hours and the name of the person to call if the network went down after midnight. He wrote the four units in, and the total, and the two faults, and the note about the crate, and the number of the forms he'd pushed through the cage.

He wrote the count by hand and initialed it. Then, because his shoulder was singing now, he decided to go home and sleep.

He had planned to stay. There was a fifth job on the informal sheet behind the door — a woman in the Lower Wards with a resonance lamp that dropped out every time she ran it above half, and she paid in soup, and the soup was very good, and it would have been forty minutes and fifteen credits and the kind of useful feeling he had been chasing since he was fourteen.

He looked at the sheet. He put his jacket on.

He walked because the night bus cost two-fifty and he had eleven credits in the world. Lattice to Nine Bridges on foot took fifty minutes along the tram trench, and the trench was loud, and he used the whole of it to not think.

At 04:15 his phone buzzed. Nell.

*can you send 40 this week. its for the deposit on the sports thing*

He read it twice on the dark platform. The batteries in her team's kit had a charge controller that cost more than the cleats. He had known that since last summer. He typed *no* and deleted it and typed *running short* and deleted that and typed *ill try* and looked at it for a long time on the platform with the wind coming up out of the trench.

He sent *ill try* and put the phone away, and it was the least true thing he had said all night, and he had said *it's a contact, not a fault* to an empty room.

Lena was on nights at the long-stay clinic on Kestrel Row, eleven to seven, and she would be home at eight in the morning to sleep until two. He had a key. He did not use it.

He got into the flat above the laundromat on Pell Street at 04:50, where Rafi was asleep on his back with one arm over the covers and a courier bag on the floor containing, Marek had established over eighteen months, three tools Rafi did not own and one fork.

Marek took four hours. He set his alarm for eight forty, lay on his back in the dark with his work gloves under his pillow like a man expecting a war, and slept the whole way through with no dreams he could remember, which was the best thing that had happened to him in a month.

He woke at eight forty to Rafi singing something about a bridge.

The 09:00 practical review was the third item on a list of five, and the list was a document in his phone called THINGS, all lowercase, no punctuation, the private bureaucracy of a man who could not keep his life in his head. Item one was *refill script*. Item two was *lena saturday*. Item three was *review*. Item four was *ask about the deposit* and item five was a line he had not touched since the term started, which said *figure out the thing with your hands*, and he knew what that meant and it frightened him.

He got the bus because he had ten minutes and the walk was thirty.

The Institute of Applied Practice put its practical building on the east side of the Lattice campus, four floors of a building that was old municipal and had been converted by people who believed in vinyl. He counted two doors out of habit while he was still twenty meters away, one main and one service, and the service one had a bicycle locked to it, which meant the lock worked, which meant the door worked, which meant if he ever needed it he could get it.

The review took eleven minutes. It was about a wall-mount bracket he had built for a second-year, and the second-year had not shown up, and the reviewing instructor looked at the bracket and said the work was clean and asked who it was for, and Marek said the second-year's name, and the instructor wrote it down, and that was the whole event. Item three went off the list.

He came out into the flat white morning with the Thursday practicum roster already open on his phone, because his name was on it, and he had been not looking at it since Monday.

**THU 09:20 — PRACTICAL ROOM 2 — TEAM RESCUE DRILL (3RD YR GROUP C)**
**INSTRUCTOR: RUSK**
**EQUIPMENT: PRACTICUM STOCK**

That was all. The roster never said which stock.

He stood on the step of the practical building and went back into his memory of two hours ago — the crate under the bench at Spine 7, the one they had opened in the first week of term because the drawer had been stiff, and the delivery tag zip-tied to the handle with a cable tie, and the tag's printed line.

**PRACTICER KIT — RESCUE BRACE — REV 4-19**
**ASSEMBLED: THREADLINE SERVICE DEPOT, LATTICE**
**UPSTREAM BUNDLE: —**

And under it, on the third plate of the night, stamped into the fiber where the housing met the base, in a small clean font that he had read ten times without reading it: **4-19**.

The same revision. Not the older stock. Not the manual.

He had been carrying the Institute's printed manual in his jacket for three weeks, because he believed in carrying the manual, and the manual was a **3-08**, and 3-08 was what every technique card in his head had been calibrated against, and somewhere in the last four months the whole school had quietly changed the metal without changing the book.

Thursday was tomorrow. The drill would be run on 4-19, in room two, at 09:20, with Davan Rusk standing at the front of it, and there would be eleven other third-years on that list and no manual older than four months and no one to ask.

He put his hand flat against the cold of the service door frame, to feel something solid.

The whole city, it turned out, was one delivery. Somebody had sent it, and everybody had signed for it, and the signature line on the back of his own fault report was the only one in the building tonight that had anything at all in it.
