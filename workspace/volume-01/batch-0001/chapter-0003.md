# Chapter 3 — Four Beats

The gray came and went. That was the part he would keep afterward, when people asked him what it felt like: not the fear. The gray.

He had his palm flat on the wall — the west wall, the one with the training-card rack, his fingers in a sleeve of laminated sequence he could not read — and his tongue tasted of a battery, and the room had no name for about a second and a half. In the middle of that gap the ladder finished leaning and Tam's shoulder arrived at the height of the brace's top rail with a sound like a dropped tray.

Odile said something. Rafi said something louder.

Marek's count was still running, and it was running in Rusk's cadence because that was the only cadence he had, and it was three beats long and it arrived, on its own, at the place where the fourth one should be.

In that place there was nothing.

Two seconds, he knew that. He knew the width of the gap the way you know the width of a stair in the dark. And behind the gap there was a person falling, and the falling needed the fourth beat, and the fourth beat was a word that was supposed to come out of a man against the far wall who had not opened his mouth yet.

Marek's hand came off the wall.

"Rafi." It came out flat and too fast. "Rafi, I need your hands on the strap. Now — give me what you're doing with the load, four seconds, use only, you can take it back whenever you want, just do it, do it now."

He did not have time to be asked properly and he did not take it. He put that in the file later, in the place where he kept the things he would not be able to look at directly.

Rafi said, "Yeah — yeah, doing it," and put both hands into the webbing at the yellow tape, and Marek felt the practice of the room tilt.

* * *

> Two practices agree. One beat is not answered.
> Do you offer the next step?

Nothing followed it. That was all. No shape, no answer, no name for the hole, just the fact of it sitting there in the middle of two honest people like a missing tooth.

And then the hole had a *shape*, because there were two of him now.

Rafi was not performing anything. Rafi was a courier on a wet ramp at seven in the evening, and what he had in him right then was a body taking a load it did not want, weight on the back of the heels, the shoulder going in — and that practice had a shape the Institute had never once taught him, and it had a place in the middle of it where the person checked whether the thing they were holding was still the thing they had agreed to hold.

Three beats. And then a place where a person decides.

Marek had four seconds. He could feel the line as a line: not a picture, not a voice, a *position*, two bodies agreeing about where a thing was, thin as a wire, and already fraying at both ends because he was the only one holding it and a man holding a line alone is just a man holding a rope.

Tam came down another foot. The rig hit the ladder's back rail with a flat sound.

Rusk said, "Scope."

One word, on the beat, in a teaching voice that had been on the beat of that word for twenty-two years.

And Marek did the only thing there was to do. He put his weight over the plate, felt the return contact take the force and pass it up the frame into the strap, walked the webbing one final quarter turn with his left hand because his right shoulder had stopped taking orders, and let the fourth beat be the word that was already in the room.

"Scope," he said, in Rusk's cadence, from nine feet closer to the fall than Rusk was.

Tam's whole body let go.

That was the release. That was the thing the fourth beat was for, and it happened — the frame came off the ribs, the arm came down, the boy stopped holding his own weight, and the plate took the rest of it, and Tam Arrick came to rest hanging in a strap with his arm out and his breathing coming back in a ragged way that Odile later said sounded like the sea at the far end of the pier.

The room made a noise.

Then Marek's own anchor went out of him.

It did not drift away. It simply was not there any more, the way a floor is not there, and the line went with it — not snapped, just gone, the way a word is gone when you finally hear it. Rafi's hands came out of the webbing on their own and Rafi said, "Whoa. Whoa. What was—" and sat down in the yellow tape on the back of his heels with his hands hanging between his knees.

Marek got about four steps and put his hand on Odile's shoulder and stayed there, and the light stripe across everybody's faces kept moving because the blind was still stuck and the sun was moving and time had not stopped, apparently, on account of anything.

His head split open behind the left eye. Not a headache — a *gap*, exactly two seconds wide and in the same place as the other one, and in it he could not remember what color the floor was. He knew the tape lines. He could see four of them. The floor was not a color he could reach.

And underneath the pain, in the place where his own fear usually sat, there was somebody else's.

It came up through the base of his skull and it was not his. It was thin, cold, enormous, and it was shaped like a boy at nineteen inches from a rail calculating, with total clarity, how long he had. It had no words in it. It was a body doing arithmetic about its own fall, and it was still running, four or five seconds later, ticking along inside Marek's chest as if the fall were still happening, as if it were still to happen, as if it were a thing that would happen again in a moment if he let his attention go.

Somebody put him in a chair. Somebody else's hands, small, brisk, a strap of webbing across his upper arm while a voice he liked said, "Senn. Two fingers. Look at my fingers."

He looked at two fingers. He said, "Scope," into the air, to nobody, because that was what his mouth was doing.

Rafi said, from the floor, in a small voice, "That's what it was? That was the missing one? Just the word?"

The Institute's ambulance came at eleven nineteen, which was fast for a Tuesday, and took Tam because Tam wanted to go, which he was allowed to do, and which nobody argued with, and which Marek understood the next day, when he had more room in his head, was Tam choosing to leave the building before anyone could ask him questions in it.

The dummy went back on its hook. The brace came apart. Odile cleaned the plate with her sleeve and put it back in the rack, and the plate went into the rack with the copper contact still warm, and it looked like any plate, and it was warm.

Rusk came and sat down on the plastic chair beside Marek, which nobody had seen him do in three years, and put his forearms on his knees.

"You hesitated," he said.

"I know."

"Four seconds. Maybe five." Rusk said it kindly. He was not a cruel man; that was the difficulty. "Odile had her hands on the plate and she was waiting for you to call the load, and she couldn't, because you hadn't called it, and she is third-year and she will do what the load-holder says or she will do nothing and today she nearly did nothing." He turned the clipboard over. "I gave you the scope. You gave it to him. He let go. That's the sequence working the way it's designed to work, and I want to be very clear with you, Senn, because I think you are about to decide that I didn't."

Marek said, "You were going to say it."

"I was going to say it on the beat." Rusk nodded. "And you got there four seconds before me and got there with nothing in your hands. You don't have a license for that. I want you to hear me say that as a man who has watched two of you nearly kill each other in this room and as a man who has to write to the board about both of those. Today went well. Today could have gone the other way and I would have been the one holding the pen."

He stood up and took the clipboard and went to the desk and unclipped the laminated script, and Marek understood, sitting there with a stranger's arithmetic ticking away in his sternum, that the conversation had gone the way conversations go in that department: it had been a debrief, and the fact that Rusk had sat down on the plastic chair first had been a kindness, and neither of those things made what had happened to him smaller.

Marek had seen four people hesitate in that room. Rusk, at the wall, four seconds late, with a reason. Odile, on the plate, with her hands open. Rafi, on the floor, saying *whoa*. And himself, at the center of it, hearing the shape of a hole in a sequence and reaching for the only thing anybody in the room had ever used to fill one.

Marek sat in a plastic chair by the door with his right arm across his knees and his head on the wall and did the arithmetic he could not stop doing.

Tam's shoulder was a bruise and a bad week. In a fortnight, in a month, with heat and a strip of mineral fiber and no new bad nights, that boy would take a load again and take it better. The arm would be the arm. The plate was sound; he had checked it. The return contact was clean; he had dressed the same batch's contact at two in the morning on Tuesday, fifty-seven hours ago, with his own hands, and it had gone green nine times out of nine.

Nothing was broken. Nothing needed a brace, and the brace had worked, and they were all going to write it up as a near miss with a favorable note for the department.

He had done it with one word out of his own mouth, in somebody else's rhythm, four feet closer to the fall than the man it belonged to.

He put his face against his knees and stayed like that for a while, and what he felt was not the pain in his head, which was fading, and not the borrowed arithmetic in his chest, which was also fading, slowly, like a sound from the next street.

What he felt was relief. That was the shameful part, and it was the part he would not be able to explain to anyone for six weeks.

For four seconds, in a room where he had failed twice, he had not been a problem. Somebody had been falling and he had been the one who knew, and it had been *enough*, and no part of him had needed anything from anybody.

He sat with that until it began to turn over, and what it turned into was the thing that would keep him up most of the night and most of the nights after: the arithmetic was still running. Not the fall. The fall had stopped.

The boy in his chest was waiting for the next time. The release had happened, and it had happened on a stranger's word, and the word had come from inside Marek, and somewhere in the middle of Tam Arrick's body there was now a place where that was allowed.

Odile came back in at half past twelve with the drill's incident sheet, because the department wanted it before the end of term and she was the one who always did this.

"You sign it," she said, and put it on the arm of the chair, and Marek read it with his good hand because his head still would not take a whole page.

It was a single side. Four numbered lines, one for each beat, each with a small box for a time and a longer box for a name, and a box at the bottom for the narrative. Line one read *anchor*. Line two read *load*. Line three read *close*.

Line four read *scope*.

The time box for line four had a number in it, 11:04, in Tam's handwriting, because Tam had filled it out in the ambulance bay while they waited for the trolley, and he had not asked anyone to help him, and he had written down the exact second at which the fourth beat of the sequence had happened to a boy of twenty.

In the name box on line four, in the same square capitals, he had written one word.

*Senn.*

Marek read it until the letters stopped meaning anything. They did not move.

Below it, in the narrative box, Tam had written thirteen words in a flat careful hand, the way you write a note on somebody's parcel:

*He said it. I let go because he said it. Both are true.*

Nobody had asked him to say it like that. Nobody had asked him anything at all.
