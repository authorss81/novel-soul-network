# Chapter 6 — Not a Keepsake

The mobile clinic on the Saltmarket route was a white box on wheels with a fold-down step, parked in the lay-by outside the wholesale kitchens, and it ran on a diesel auxiliary unit that smelled like a bus. There was a hand-lettered sign on the door: **THURSDAY PRACTICE SLOTS — ASK FOR A SLOT** with a smaller line under it that said *no obligation, no record without asking*.

He had been past it four hundred times. He had never gone in.

He went in on the Tuesday of week five, at ten past six in the evening, because on Thursday he had been offered a job and a date for a humiliating examination and because he had discovered that he could not write a reply to a text message and could apparently walk into a vehicle.

She was standing in the doorway with a clipboard, and she recognized him before he had got his hand up, which was humiliating in a way he had not budgeted for.

"Senn," Talia Venn said. "You never signed the form."

"I know."

"I know that you know. I keep a set." She turned the clipboard around. "Come in. Boots off at the step, we have a rule about the floor, and I am not joking about the floor."

The treatment bay was eight feet by eleven, a counter along one side, a sink with warm water, a fold-down table, a chair for visitors, and a shelf of stock. Four people in it. A woman with a child's arm. A man in a hi-vis jacket asleep sitting up. A boy of about nine being persuaded by his mother to hold still. And on the table, a man of about fifty with a ring finger that was the color of a plum and a great deal of swelling.

"Two minutes," Talia said to nobody in particular, and turned to Marek, and said, "Out here. Not inside. Out here, please, and I am going to say a thing to you in the open air where there are no patients, and then I am going to come back in and finish Bern's hand."

Marek went out. She came out after him with the clipboard under her arm and shut the door behind her, and there was rain starting on the roof of the box, thin enough to hear.

"What did you do to a person called Tam Arrick?"

"How do you know his—"

"I have his chart. I have had his chart since he was sixteen. I have a note on it that says *routine brace practice, night kitchen work, recurrent shoulder, no injury on file.*" She stopped. "Something happened in week one. Something enough that a boy I have known for two years wrote thirteen words on an incident sheet and gave them to the department and then came to me on the Monday and asked whether the department keeping a record meant the record was somebody's. Do you know what that means?"

"It means he's scared."

"It means a twenty-year-old had to work out whether the school was a place that reports people to a third party." She took a breath, and Marek understood that she was about to be extremely fair, and that this was worse. "I am going to ask you a question and I want you to answer it in the words you would use in a room with the equipment off. What did you do to a person called Tam Arrick?"

"I took a share with him."

"That is a technical sentence. Try the other one."

"I asked him for four seconds of his balance without asking him properly, and I used what he gave me to stop a plate from taking his shoulder off, and it worked, and I said a word he had been taught to need in somebody else's voice, and he let go because of it." Marek's mouth had gone dry. "And I've been carrying the shape of it since. I didn't ask for it and I can't put it down and I don't know how to get rid of it and I have been trying to get rid of it for four weeks."

Talia Venn did not soften. That was the thing he kept coming back to afterward. She did not soften at all.

"You have his finish," she said. "His practiced release. The four seconds, and everything that came with them, and you have had it for four weeks, and you did not ask him, and he has never in his life given anybody a keepsake."

"It saved his—"

"I know what it saved. I know because I was the one who splinted him on the Friday, and I know what his shoulder looks like now, and I know it would have gone the other way." She said it in exactly the same voice. "And I am not going to have that in the room with him. I am going to have to stand next to a man on a table on Thursday and explain to him why the nurse is not using the man who was here on Tuesday as a reference."

Marek's face did something he had not authorized.

"You don't get to do that," he said. "You don't get to be right about me and then make it my problem that Bern Colley's hand is on the line—"

"No. I don't. That is exactly right and thank you." She turned the clipboard over. "So here is what happens. I am going back in there and I am going to do Bern's hand, and while I do it I am going to say the sentence, out loud, in plain words, in front of him, that there is a man in the car park who is carrying a piece of another patient's practice that the patient never agreed to, and I am going to say that I stopped it, and that is the whole of what I did, and then Bern Colley can have an opinion about it and I will write down whatever he says. And you will stand there and let me say it, because you were going to walk in here and use the best thing in your life and I have stopped you."

"You could have just asked me to put it down."

"Yes."

"And if I had said no?"

"Then I would have written that you had a shape you had taken from a man who did not give it to you, on a Thursday, in a treatment bay, and I would have written it in the space where I write the things that worry me." She said it gently, which was obscene. "I have a column, Mr. Senn. *Interference noted.* Most of it is about men with clipboards. Some of it is about you now."

Behind the door, the boy of nine said, loudly, *I'm bored*, and his mother said something reasonable, and the diesel unit ran, and the rain got slightly heavier, and Marek stood in the mud beside a white box on wheels and understood that he had walked into a building to get help and had instead been told the truth, in public, by someone who was not going to be thanked for it.

He said, "There are three permissions."

"Use, remember, derive." She said it immediately, the way other people say their own street. "Use means during the named event. Remember means I keep a faint echo afterward, and I can be asked to forget it and mostly I can. Derive means somebody later compares the trace and builds something new, which is the one that actually matters and the one nobody ever asks about. What did you have?"

"Use."

"You had *keep*. That is not a permission. That is not even a mistake somebody makes, because there is no word on any form for it, which is why it is the one everybody does." She tucked the clipboard under her arm. "And on Thursday of September, when I came to the end of your row in room two and asked you for twenty minutes, and again the week after — you said no twice, in front of other people, and both times you said a version of *this week is impossible*, and both times it was true, and neither time you told me a reason. And then you turned up here on a Tuesday without ever answering the text, which means you did not come because of my clinic. You came because you wanted something off me."

"Yes."

"Good. At least it is honest." She looked at the rain. "One more thing, and then I am going back to my patient. There is a form for the Thursday slots. It is the Institute's facilities and equipment access form. It is pre-signed by the department, in ink, once a term, for everybody."

"She said it was pre-signed. I never signed it."

"You never signed it. I have a set, as I told you." She went into the box and came back with a clipboard and a single sheet of paper and gave it to him. "Read the whole of it, at the bottom, in the box."

He read the bottom, in the box:

*Access to premises, to equipment, and to the handling of training stock under the supervision of the host institution. This form confers no permission in respect of any individual practice, share, record, or observation. Any such permission must be given in the manner and on the scope provided by the host institution, naming its purpose and its limits.*

He read it twice. Somewhere in the middle of the second reading he began to hear, very clearly, his own voice from three weeks ago in a corridor: *I didn't sign it.*

"This form confers no permission," Talia said, taking the sheet back. "A pre-signed piece of paper, a signature by a department that is not here, a person standing near you — none of that is a yes from the person on the table. That is not me being clever. That is the only sentence in the document and it is there because somebody got hurt once and had to write it down."

"Senn," called a voice from inside, patiently.

"I'm coming," Talia called back, and to Marek: "In there. You will stand by the sink, out of the way, and you will be quiet, and Bern Colley is going to ask you a question and you are going to answer it. Not me. Him. And if you have anything at all in your head right now that sounds like a reason, you keep it, because he has a bad hand and he has a train to catch."

---

Bern Colley said: "Is he the reason you were in my chart?"

"He is a person I have a question about," Talia said, "and he is going to answer it, and then I am going to ask you a question, and the two of you are not going to be mixed up. Mr. Colley, is it all right if I finish your finger first and then we do the strange part?"

"Strange part." Bern Colley looked at Marek. "You want to watch me get a hand fixed?"

"Yes, sir."

"Why?"

"Because I have a piece of somebody else's practice in my head and I have to find out whether it's real. And you are the only person in this building who can tell me that."

---

He went to Tam at half past seven that night, to the room over the chip shop on Sennet Row, and stood in a doorway while a boy of twenty ate noodles out of a bowl with a fork, and it took him four attempts to say the thing.

"Tam. I need to ask you about Thursday of week one. Not about the plate. About me."

Tam put the fork down. "Say it."

"I asked Rafi for a share without asking him. I did that part badly and I've stopped doing it. But the part I need to ask you about is different. When the line came, I got more than four seconds." Marek heard his own voice and it was very steady, which was humiliating. "I've still got it. Four weeks later. I can feel the shape of your release, your actual one, the one you do at the kitchen, the one you never said out loud to anybody. I didn't know I was keeping it. I found out this afternoon when somebody I don't even know told me to my face."

Tam's face did not change for a long moment.

"Is it mine?" he said.

"What?"

"Is it mine, or is it the kit's."

Marek opened his mouth, and the first thing that came up was the easy answer, and he took it out of his mouth and put it on the floor where they could both see it.

"I don't know," he said. "Honest answer. The last four seconds of it are the room, and the room was the kit, and the kit is the same in every pair of hands. But the shape of where you checked—"

"I checked by listening for the person on the bottom of the ladder," Tam said. "Every time. I never once checked for the plate. I checked for the *person*. That's not in the card. I don't know where I got it. I do it every time and I could not tell you."

"And I have that."

"Yeah. You have that." Tam picked the fork up and put it down again. "Right. So what do you actually want."

Marek had prepared for this for four hours. He had a whole shape.

"I want to ask whether you're willing to have it remembered," he said. "That's it. Not used — I'm not using it anywhere, I'm not asking you for anything again, and I wouldn't even if you'd said yes, because the asking is the part I got wrong last time. Remembered. If you say no, I take it to the woman at the clinic who has the column and she tells me how to put it down, and whatever it costs, it costs me and not you. And if you say yes, I still have to write down that I asked, so that it isn't me remembering on your behalf."

Tam Arrick looked at him for long enough to be uncomfortable.

"You've already had it four weeks," he said.

"Yes."

"So what would saying yes actually do?"

"Nothing. That's the point. It would mean I stop lying to myself about it."

The boy laughed — one short bark, entirely without humor — and got up, and crossed the room in two steps, and hit Marek in the shoulder with the flat of his fist, hard, on the bad side, and Marek's whole body came apart in the pain for a second.

"That," said Tam, "is for four seconds. And don't ever ask me that question again in a doorway. Come to the front of the room like a person."

He sat back down on the bed.

"Use," he said. "The rescue. That's it. That happened, I let go, you caught me, and you can keep that because it happened and you can't take it off me either, and I'd like somebody to have been the one holding it when I fell down four feet. That's yours. I don't care. I don't want it repeated, I don't want it improved, and I don't want my kitchen hands in your notebook, and if you ever go near that again without asking me I'll put your head through the wall of your mother's flat."

"Never again," Marek said.

"Never," said Tam, and then, after a second, with the first flicker of something almost warm in it, "and the word. You used Rusk's word. Not your own. He taught it to me in room two with his actual mouth and you said it in a room with a stuck blind and my shoulder still went to you instead of him. Do you understand that's the worst thing that's ever happened to me? Not the shoulder. The other thing. There's two voices in it now. There's two voices and one of them is a person I've met four times."

---

He got back to the clinic at nine-forty because he did not know where else to go, and Talia was on the step with her coat on and a bag over her shoulder, closing up.

He told her first, out of order, in the rain: Tam had said use, rescue only, no retention, never again, and had put a fist into his bad shoulder, and was the only person Marek had ever met who had ever said *come to the front of the room like a person.*

"Use for the rescue," she said. "That's generous and it's the correct answer and I am going to record it exactly like that, including the fist, which is part of the record." She wrote it on the clipboard standing up, quickly, and tore the sheet off and folded it into her coat. "Now. Bern Colley asked whether a brace like the one on his finger was the same as the one they drill with at the Institute."

Marek said, "No."

"He said that was a very fast answer for someone who has been in my bay for an hour." She looked at him. "Tell me why it's not."

Marek took the stock down off the shelf — a mineral-fiber wrist plate, printed with Threadline on the shoulder of the strap, revision 4-19 — and put it on the counter next to the clipboard, and set out Bern's printed card beside it.

"The card on this says *scope: force only, no joint, no sensation.*" He said it carefully, because he had thought about this in a clinic car park and it was the only thing he had that was still worth saying. "It's printed. There's no beat on the card between *close* and *scope*. Between the third beat and the word, there's nothing in the technique. Nothing in the fiber, nothing in the copper, nothing in the strap. It's a label, and it's a good label, and it's honest as far as it goes, and there's no action under it anywhere in the object."

"And in yours?"

"In mine there's a fourth beat that the person has to do with their hands, at a point where the answer can still change what happens. It asks out loud what the thing is allowed to have touched and it hands the next decision back to the person it was done for." He put his hand flat on the plate. "I've never been able to do it on my own. That's not a confession, that's a measurement."

Talia Venn picked up the printed card and read the back of it, and then the front, and then she put it down and did something with her face that he had not seen her do.

"Four nineteen," she said. "We have been fitting four-nineteens since September. Twenty-six of them, on the shelf, in that box, and two in the treatment bay and one on that man's finger." She nodded at the door. "I have been asking the supplier about the return-contact change since week two and they have sent me a very polite letter about a scheduled maintenance upgrade."

"What does the manual say?"

"The manual says three-oh-eight." She said it without any expression. "Nobody updated the manual, and nobody asked, and I have been the one putting a plate on a man's finger for five weeks with a card in my head from a version of the technique that no longer exists in the building."

---

They stood in the road a while. The diesel unit shut off and the rain got loud.

"Here is what I can offer," Talia said. "And I am offering it because you need a comparison, not because you asked nicely. I will perform my brace twice in front of you. Both times, at the table, on the frame, with a person, at working speed, and you may watch. Use and remember, no derive. Nothing is kept for later comparison by anyone. I will write a scope sheet: the purpose, the two performances, the date, the review, and the fact that Mr. Senn carries an echo he has not been authorized to retain and is undertaking this to learn what an authorized one looks like. I keep a copy and you keep a copy. If you cannot describe what I did differently by tomorrow, you have lost nothing but the evening."

"And if I do describe it?"

"Then you will have one observation, and it will not be evidence of anything, because you are one tired student with a bad record and a thing in his head he should not have, and no board in the world is going to change a transcript on the strength of it." She shouldered her bag. "Which is why the thing you actually have to do is not with me. It is with people the Institute has never taught, and it is going to take longer than a term, and there is no version of the next four weeks in which you walk into that examination hall in seven days and pass."

"I know."

"You do not know. You have known for four hours." She said it without any cruelty. "You will know on Wednesday. Thursday we can talk about it."

She got on her bicycle. She got off it again.

"Marek."

He looked up. It was the first time she had used his name.

"The thing you said at the end. In the car park. That you are still carrying it." She stood with one foot on the pedal. "Four weeks is not very long. You will not get rid of it, and I would rather you did not spend the next year trying to get rid of it by pretending it is not there. It is a boy who checked for a person at the bottom of a ladder. That is not a horror. That is the best thing in your head, and it belongs to him, and now it is in you, and the honest position is to say so out loud to one person and keep saying it to one person until there is a person who can tell you what to do about it."

And then she said, "I am the person you can say it to, and I am going to be there on Thursday, and I am not going to tell anybody anything. Say it to somebody anyway. Not me. Somebody who is not me."

She rode off up the lay-by with her bag banging the wheel, and Marek Senn stood in the rain on the Saltmarket route with a wet form in his hand that had granted him absolutely nothing whatsoever, and the worst of it, the part that was going to take him apart over the following week, was that she had been right about all of it and had still given him the Tuesday.

He walked back to Nine Bridges with his boots full of water and the shape of Tam's four seconds in his chest, and by the time he got to Pell Street he had understood the shape of the next five days: he had one authorized observation coming, and it was not going to be enough, and the only way to get enough was to stop looking at the Institute.

He wrote it in the load book at midnight, and then he read back the entry from week one, the one where he had written *get the fourth beat, it is the last thing on the card*, and he put a line through it, and wrote underneath:

*It is not the last thing on the card. It is the last thing anybody in that building can show me, and that is a different problem, and I have been treating it as one for a month.*
