# Chapter 29 — The Bell on the Wrist

The back room of the Saltmarket Exchange on the Monday morning of week 12 was a storage space with the shelving taken out and eleven trestle tables borrowed from the front, and by ten past ten there were about forty people in it and Oren Vey had a demonstration.

He had brought it himself, in a case, and he set it out on a trestle table in ninety seconds with the ease of a man who has done this four hundred times.

"Right," he said. "Short. What this is, what it is not, and then it goes on a person and you all watch it and then you all say no at me and I'll take it, because that is the only way this is worth anything."

He put a hand down flat on the table.

"What it is: a supported completion route. Revision SC-4. It is a wrist cuff, a contact, a single tone, and a trained completion assistant standing inside the room. The learner does anchor, load, close, and the assistant presses the contact, the tone sounds, and the learner releases. Every time. The same tone, the same pitch, the same length, the same place in the beat. It is not a person having a good day. It is a procedure."

"And the assistant?" someone said.

"Certified. Two hundred hours. Assessed annually. There is a complaints route and there is a name on the certificate." Oren Vey did not oversell it and he did not apologise for it either. "And I am going to say the unarguable version of this before anybody asks: it is more reliable than a human voice, and a human voice is what it replaced, and there is a signed record in a file somewhere saying the human voice was put there because a scope was applied early with the subject still in the load, twice in six months, one of them a fourth-year. This does not argue with that record. It agrees with it."

"Whose product is the cuff?"

"Mine," said Oren Vey. "Designed in Lattice, made in the district, and I am not going to pretend that makes me neutral, and I have not got a second version of this speech ready for a room that thinks it does."

He did the demonstration twice on the assistant, a man of about thirty in a Threadline polo shirt who had done it two hundred times and could have done it in his sleep, and then he said, "Right, and now I'd like to do it on somebody who has actually had one of these, and I've asked two people and one of them hasn't come and I'm not going to say who, and the other one has written a consent on the Wednesday and I've got it here with his name on it and he is in the room."

The room turned round.

Tam Arrick was by the door with his right arm out of the splint for the first time in public, and a folded sheet in his left hand, and Marek Senn was against the wall nine feet away with his hands in his pockets and nothing whatsoever on his face, having been asked by Oren Vey at half past eight that morning to be outside the room for the asking, and having agreed to it in a corridor in about two seconds without needing to think about it.

Tam held the sheet up.

"I'm going to read this out because you lot have heard about me and I want you to hear the actual thing," he said, "and then I'm going to do it or I'm not, and nobody's got to be polite."

He read it out. Purpose: one demonstration, one time, of a supported completion route on a right forearm with no weight, so that a room of people who do the work can see whether it works on a hand that cannot close. Not for a board. Not for the department. Not for a document. Stop-word: his word, out loud, no reason needed. After: nothing goes in a file with his name on it unless he puts it there. Signed and dated the Wednesday, and the witness was Mal Farrow.

Forty people listened to a boy of twenty read out his own consent in a market.

Oren Vey said, "Thank you. That's the right way to do it. Do you want to read the second one?"

"There's not a second one."

"That's fine. Tam—" and he stopped, and changed what he was going to say, visibly, in front of forty people. "T. Arrick. Would you rather I ask somebody else, and you can watch?"

"Yeah," said Tam Arrick. "Stop."

The room made a noise. Not a big one. A small one, the noise a room makes.

Oren Vey did not move. "Say it again for me, and say it to the room and not to me, so that I have not taken it personally."

"I said stop."

"Stopped," said Oren Vey, to the room, and put the cuff back in the case and shut the case, and that was the whole of it, and he did not ask, and nobody asked, and Mal Farrow said, from the back, in a flat voice: "He's stopped it and the paper says nobody may ask him why, and I am the witness on the paper, and I am not asking, and I'd like everybody in this room to notice that nobody has asked, because that is the entire of what this afternoon is for."

Nobody had asked.

---

"You'll want to know why," said Oren Vey, to the room, at about ten past eleven. "And I am not going to ask him, and I am not going to try, and I would like it recorded that I had the demonstration on my own table and a signed consent in my hand and a witness and I put it away inside four seconds, because I said on the Friday that I would not sit on a thing for two days, and neither am I going to sit on this one."

Nobody said anything.

"Right." He put his hands flat on the table. "So let me at least find out whether the cuff is the problem, because if it is then I have wasted everybody's morning and I will go away and build a better one."

---

"The kitchen first," said someone.

It was Dessa Kwan, and she came to the front, and she did not look at Oren Vey, and she looked at the cuff still sitting in its open case.

"I'm not doing this in a kitchen," she said. "That's not modesty, that's the answer, and it's the whole answer, and I don't care if you write it down."

"Why."

"Because that thing tells the cook the load has settled." She held her hands up, apart, at chest height, the way a cook does. "It goes *ding*, and I hear it, and I look at the plate, and the plate is beautiful, and the load is *on the plate*. And the thing I need to know is not whether the plate is done. The plate is always done. I need to know whether the man at table four has gone grey and put his fork down and is not breathing properly, and that is not information in the load, that is information in the room, and it is the only thing in this business that has ever mattered and no machine in this city has ever been able to hear it."

"So you'd keep doing the pass the way you do it."

"I do the pass the way I do it because I say what the pan touched and I say who it is and then I put it down and walk away, and it is the only reason nobody has ever dropped a pan in that kitchen, and it has never once involved a bell." She put her hands down. "And I'll give you the other one, because you'll ask. It is not free. That is a cuff, and it is a tone, and it is a person trained for two hundred hours, and the two hundred hours is a wage, and the wage is money, and the day the money goes somewhere else there is no bell, and then the load settles and nobody hears it and a man in a kitchen does not know. And I have run a kitchen on a Saturday with a broken extractor fan and a gas cut-off and no second pair of hands, and I have done it, and I would do it again, and I would not be doing *this* on my own in front of a customer and calling it the same job."

"That's two."

"That's two," she said, "and I know it's a good answer and I know it's a kitchen answer and I don't care, it's the one I've got."

---

The market next, and it was the boy of twenty-two from the crates, off a run, in a market jumper, and he came to the front and put his hands in his pockets and said:

"Does it need a phone."

"It needs a tone," said Oren Vey. "Somebody presses a contact and there's a tone. There's no phone in it."

"Right, because the thing I was going to say was a phone." The boy did not take his hands out. "I've been on the Saturday rate six weeks. I do four to six trips an hour and I do about four hundred and ten crates in a week, and there are eleven of us on the Saturday, and I could tell you which six of them are over fifty without looking, and if those six are off there's five of us and I am one of the five and I am the youngest."

"I see."

"So the route has to be there every trip," said the boy, "because a crate's heavy once and light the other time and you only know which when it's on you. And a route that needs two hundred hours of somebody means two hundred hours of somebody, and if that's a body of my own doing it, then it's me, and I've already got a job, and if it's a wage out of that then it's money, and if it's a company coming round in a van then it's a company coming round in a van, and the day there's no van I'm back on the Saturday rate with no route and nobody's told me, because nobody tells me."

He looked at the cuff in the case.

"I've got a mate on a Thursday who's broken his wrist twice," he said. "I'd be frightened for him. I'd want one. I'm not saying I wouldn't. I'm saying I can't have it be a thing I can only have if somebody keeps paying for it, because then it's not safety, it's a service, and I've been sold a service my whole life by people who don't have to be in the yard at six in the morning."

Oren Vey said, "What's your name."

"Reuben."

"Reuben, that's fair, and it's a better objection than most of the things I get." He looked up. "And I have to say that the version where the van comes round is the version we sell and the version that isn't that is a building with a room in it, and you should be careful of me, because I am offering you the van and the van is the reason the objection is correct."

---

Marek Senn, against a wall, nine feet away, with his hands in his pockets, heard a name he had chosen not to buy on the Tuesday of week 10 in a fish office yard, said out loud in a room in his own hearing by somebody who had turned up for fish.

He did not look at the floor. He did not look at a wall. He had four seconds in which to do the only thing every other person in this room would have done, which was turn round and look at the spot where he had been standing on the Tuesday, and he looked at Oren Vey's demonstration table instead, and the man in the polo shirt, and the cuff, and he kept his face entirely still, and that was the single most expensive thing he did in week 12.

Mal Farrow got up next.

He got up slowly, the way he does, with a hand on a chair, and he did not come to the front.

"You've been up there twenty minutes telling us about a procedure," he said, "and it's a good one, and I'll not argue with it, and I'd use it on a Tuesday. So I'm not going to stand here and tell you it doesn't work. It works. Everybody in this room knows it works."

"Then what's your objection."

"I'm the floor." Farrow said it flatly, and there was no drama in it anywhere. "March 2011, on the ice, in that building. Three ribs out, two broken and one cracked, and I was fifty, and a doctor told me I'd be lucky to see fifty. And eleven years later there's a plate in a hip unit that a nurse put on with her own hands, and it takes a load and there's nobody to do the last bit, and I can't get a full breath, and it goes for two and a half minutes, and there is no bell and no route and no assistant, because I am on the floor of a fish market and there is nobody in the room but the nurse and a man with a key to the container."

He stopped and took a breath, which everybody in the room noticed, and which is the sort of thing that is only visible when a man does it.

"And I did it myself," he said. "On the floor. Out loud, to nobody, for about two and a half minutes, and what I said was *get it off yourself or leave it, it's your call, I've got you*, and then I said *leave it*, and then I got up on my own two hands and a table leg and I went back to work. And that is the only reason I'm sat in this room, and I'd not have a thing to say in this room if that hadn't happened, and it happened because there was no bell and no route and no assistant, and I was the route."

Nobody said anything for a while.

"I'm not being brave," said Mal Farrow. "I'm just the only one of you that's been the floor."

---

Ines Sarr came to the front and did not take the table; she stood in front of it with her arms folded and looked at the cuff.

"In the eighth week," she said, "on a Friday lunchtime, in a corridor in Nine Bridges, over a cup of tea, I worked out on my own that I have been the word in my own room."

"By which you mean—"

"I have been saying *scope, off*, out loud, in front of people, at the fourth place, for four months, and eleven of them have come to a Thursday class and eleven of them release when I open my mouth, and I worked out on a Friday over a tea that I am a key-holder in a corridor of my own, and I did not know it, and I could not stop it, and the only reason I found out is that a boy in a sling kept being frightened in a room in front of me." She unfolded her arms. "So I am going to tell you the truth, which is the least interesting-sounding thing anybody has said in this room this morning, and then I am going to go and sit down."

"Go on."

"If you put that on eleven people," said Ines Sarr, "then in about eleven months I will not be the worst of it. I will be the best of it. That is what a room is. You put a thing in a room and the person who owns the room owns it, and I have just spent four months finding out that I own one without knowing, and I have thought about very little else since." She looked at him. "And you want to know how I know I would use it. I would use it. I would use it on the first hard afternoon and I would use it against a nineteen-year-old I have known for four months and I would not feel anything about it, because that is what an owned room feels like from the inside. I do not want to be that again in a worse coat."

"Ma'am—"

"You may call it a route. It is a route," said Ines Sarr. "I have never said anything else about a route in my life. It is a route and it goes through a person and I would rather it went through a person I can be angry at than through a thing I can blame, and I know that is an argument about my own comfort and I have had it out with myself on the bus."

---

Rafi Pell was the last, and he did not come to the front.

He stood up at the side of the room where he had been standing, and he did not come forward, and he said, "Can I do this where I am."

"Please do."

"Right." He held up two fingers, to the room, and said *one*, and then said *two*, slowly, going nowhere, and forty people in a market back room watched a courier count to two before he was allowed to say no.

"Two years," he said. "I've got a wall. Down the side of a ramp, off the front corner, every run, four hundred times a day, and it started about eighteen months after I started, and I've known exactly what it is for a month because a man in a notebook asked me and then wrote it down wrongly. The wall's not a wall. It's a place where somebody has to be. I made it out of a doorway and a man shouting *keep it up the rail* when I was nineteen, and it has cost me a shoulder and it hasn't put me in a hospital and it's the only technique I've got, and I have never once asked anybody to take it off me."

He put his hands down.

"So that's what you're offering, isn't it. A beep. And I'll put my shoulder against a beep. That's not better. That's the same wall with a battery in it and a warranty, and I'd still have to ask the beep." He stopped. "And that's not the bit. Here's the bit. It's *you*."

"He isn't the assistant," said Oren Vey.

"He isn't the assistant and it isn't a man in a polo shirt either," said Rafi Pell, "because whoever it is, it's somebody, and somebody in this company, and in a year I'll be asking that somebody to let me go, because that's what I do now, on a wall, twice a day, and I don't want to be doing it to a person who has a job and a certificate and a name on a card." He looked at Oren Vey. "You haven't changed anything. You've put the man in a chair with a lanyard and given him a procedure."

The room was very quiet.

"That's it," said Rafi Pell. "That's the whole of it. No."

---

Oren Vey stood behind his trestle table for about four seconds and then he did the thing that Marek Senn was not expecting and would think about for a long time afterwards.

He put both hands flat on the table and he said:

"Right. And I want to say the last bit myself, because I have earned it today, and because five people have just refused me and not one of them was rude, and because a boy of twenty read out a consent in front of forty people and then stopped, and I want both of those things said out loud by me and not by him."

He looked around the room.

"You all know what the one thing a panel can act on is," he said. "It is a release completed by the person doing it. Not a document. Not an absence. A demonstration, in a public room, with witnesses, and no substituted cue used, followed or accepted in the window. That is the single sentence this country's problem-solving office has handed to a twenty-two-year-old, and I have read it, and it is in a file in his coat, and I have had that shape of sentence in front of me for nine years, in a standard, in a warranty clause, in a training pack I have never opened — and I have never once seen the one in the middle, which is the one that matters."

Nobody said anything.

"And that is exactly the thing I have just demonstrated the opposite of." Oren Vey did not raise his voice and did not soften it, and Marek Senn watched a man arrive at the worst sentence of his week on purpose and stand in it. "I have just shown a room of forty people a route where the release is completed by somebody else, on purpose, reliably, every time, and every single one of you has said no to it, and every one of you has been right for your own reason, and I have got no argument against any of them. And I want to say out loud, in this room, that I have understood what I have just done, and I did it anyway, because it is a demonstration and I was asked for one, and because I would rather be refused in a market by forty people than find out in a hearing."

He closed the case.

"And I am not going to be able to put my hand on my heart and say I have not thought about the fact that a document exists which says the learner's own release was withdrawn, because a fair-minded man in a café on Friday told me that a fourth-year applied a scope early with the subject still in the load, twice, and I have not been able to put that down since. So the honest position is: I think the cuff is wrong and I think the problem is real and I think somebody with a reference number was right in September, and I am the person in this room who has just argued that we should put the choice back into the hands of a hundred and eleven young people and a boy in a sling, and I did that on a Monday morning for a commercial reason, and I am not going to stand here and pretend otherwise, and I would take it as a kindness if somebody in this room wrote that down."

Nobody wrote it down.

"I am not defeated," said Oren Vey, "and I want to be clear that I am not going to go off and be gracious about this in a corridor. I have been wrong this morning in a way that I did not expect and I am going to go and think about it and I am going to be back, because there has to be something in its place, and there is not one, and I have watched five people try."

He said the last part looking directly at Marek Senn, across a room, from nine feet, and it was not an accusation, and it was not quite an appeal, and Marek Senn did not nod.

---

They came out into the market at about twenty past eleven, and Farrow was the first one through the door, and Oren Vey was behind him saying something about a hip unit and where to get one that was not from his company, and Sarr was next, and Oren Vey said, "Mrs Sarr — I am not going to argue with a woman who has run a room for nineteen years, and if you ever want a plate for a classroom I will get you one and I will not put a name on it and I will not want anything back," and she said, "Write to me," and he said, "I will," and meant it, and she went.

Marek Senn came out last, and Nell Senn was standing in the doorway of the Exchange with a folded sheet of paper in her hand and Yvette Fenn eleven feet away by a trestle table looking at a noticeboard a month out of date.

He said her name, twelve days after the last time she had said his.

She did not look at him. She looked at Oren Vey, two feet to her left, who did not know her, and she said to him, in a perfectly ordinary voice, "What's the number for the ferry timetable."

"It went in 2019," said Oren Vey. "There's a bus."

"Right." Nell Senn looked at the noticeboard again. "There's a bus."

And she walked out into the market with Yvette Fenn, and Marek Senn stood in a doorway for a moment and did not follow her, which was correct, and which was the twelfth day.

---

He wrote it up at the bench at the Spine at twenty past one in the afternoon of the Monday of week 12, in the middle of the working day, because he could not wait, and Sera Quill came past at about ten to two, looked at him, and said, "Not on that bench," and he said, "Sorry," and went and sat in the mess room, and wrote it in the mess room at a table with a cup of coffee on it that somebody else's had been in.

*Week 12, Monday, 13:20, Spine 7 mess room. I am writing this in the wrong room because I was told to.*

*Forty people in a market back room. A cuff. A tone. A man in a polo shirt and a procedure and an assistant who has a name on a certificate.*
*Five noes and a stop. I did not say a word. I was asked to be outside the room for the asking and I was outside the room for the asking and I was nine feet from the boy and in the room for the rest of it, and I did not say one word for an hour and ten minutes, and I want that in a book as a fact and not as a virtue, because the reason I did not say a word is that the room was full of people who had come for their own reasons and I could not think of a single sentence that would not have made it about me, and if I had said it then I would have been doing the thing.*

*What the five said, in their own words, and I am not summarising, because this is the first week in ten that I have had material that is not mine:*
*— D. K., 27, who runs the kitchen at the back of this building and has been handing a hot pass off to somebody else eleven times a service since the Thursday of week six: that thing tells the cook the load has settled. I need to know about the man at table four. There is no machine in this city that can hear that.*
*— R., 22, six weeks on the Saturday rate: the route has to be there every trip, and every trip is money, and the day the money goes somewhere else there is no bell and nobody tells me, and I have been sold a service my whole life by people who are not in the yard at six in the morning.*
*— M. F., who has been the floor: I am the only one of you that has been the floor.*
*— I. S., who has been the word in her own room for four months and found it over a tea on a Friday: I would use it. On the first hard afternoon, against a nineteen-year-old I have known four months. And I would not feel anything about it.*
*— R. P.: you have not changed anything, you have put the man in a chair with a lanyard and given him a procedure.*

*And then O. V., to the room, unprompted, in his own voice, having earned the right to say it and knowing exactly what it cost: the thing I have demonstrated is the opposite of the one thing a panel can act on, and every one of you is right, and I did it on a Monday morning for a commercial reason and I am not going to pretend otherwise.*

*So the demonstration requirement is not a technique I have to go and invent. It is the exact thing the five of them just refused, and it is the exact thing that gets a plate out of a boy's hand and a wrist off a man's back. That has been true since the Thursday of week one and it has taken me eleven weeks to be able to write it down, and I have got it out of other people's mouths and not out of mine, and I am not going to pretend that is a coincidence, because it is not a coincidence: I have never once in this term been in a room where somebody was willing to say the thing out loud, because a room with me in it is a room where the man in it is the thing being said.*

*And: R. gave his name. He said it in a room and I heard it and I did not ask for it, and I want to write that down exactly: on the Tuesday of week 10 in a fish office yard I chose not to buy a name and I wrote the price of it in this book as one number, and the price was wrong, and the price was not that the case went nowhere. The price was that I have spent nine days walking round a city as the man who gets things out of people by showing up, and the actual price was that a boy of twenty-two had to walk into a room on a Monday morning and say it himself, to a stranger, with a case he had never been asked about, and he did it, and it took him four seconds, and it cost him nothing, and it is mine that I made it a decision about.*

*Twelve days. She has not spoken to me for twelve days and I said her name in a doorway and she asked a stranger what the number for the ferry timetable was, and the ferry stopped in 2019, and I do not think that was a coincidence either, and I am not going to be allowed to ask her.*

*Do not know: what the five of them would put in its place. Nobody in that room could answer him. I have been eleven weeks asking a version of that question and I could not have answered it either, and that is the first honest thing I can say about myself in a term.*
*Do not know: whether I am going to accept the job. I have had the card for four days and I have not refused it, and I have not been asked to, and it is in my coat.*
*Do not know, and I am writing it because I am going to be tempted not to: whether five noes and a stop, in one room, on one Monday, is worth anything at all on a Friday, and the answer is that I do not know, and that I have got four sentences in my own hand that are worth more than the room, and that the room is the first thing in eleven weeks that was not built out of people who are fond of me.*
