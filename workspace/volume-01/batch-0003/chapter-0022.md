# Chapter 22 — What the Hands Do

The Exchange kitchen at twenty to six on the Tuesday morning of week 10 had four cookers going, a girl of nineteen on the fish, two hundred covers booked for that evening, and a man with a notebook standing in the corner who was told on the Thursday that he could stand in the corner.

"Terms," said Dessa Kwan, without looking up from a pan. "One: you stand there. Two: you don't ask anybody anything before eleven, and after eleven you ask one person one thing and you don't ask them the same thing twice. Three: you write in your own book about what you see in this room, and if you put a person's name in that book you can take it out again on paper in front of them, and neither of us will mind and neither of us will help you. Four: nobody in this room is a source. Not me, not the girl, not Peta, not the man on the bins. If you come back in here in six weeks with a list of names I will put you out on your ear, and it won't be because I'm hard, it'll be because a kitchen is a hot room with a schedule in it and the day my working life turns into a laboratory is the day I stop being able to run it."

"Understood."

"Say the fourth one back."

"Nobody in this room is a source."

"Good." She put the pan down. "Kettle's on. Don't talk to the girl till she's finished that tray, she's got nine seconds of fish in her and she's doing it with her tongue."

---

Marek Senn stood in the corner of a kitchen from twenty to six until half past seven and watched a woman of twenty-seven hand off eleven hot passes, and he wrote nothing for the first two of them and then wrote so fast that his hand hurt.

Dessa Kwan did not perform. He had been in a room on the Thursday of week six where somebody performed, because he had asked, and he knew what it looked like, and there was none of it here. She worked. She named out loud what the pan had touched — *that's the shallots, that's the butter, that's the egg that's gone over* — and then she put the pan in the pass and took her hands off it and left the pass, and the pass stood there hot with nobody standing over it, and the second cook came and took it, and nobody had been told they could.

Eleven times.

On the fifth one she said something different. She said, "Off you go, that's yours, it's hot, it's yours," and put it down and walked away, and Marek wrote: *she says who it is and then leaves. It is the same event every time. She is not showing anybody. She is not being watched. There are two hundred people coming tonight and neither of them is in this room.*

On the ninth one she said, "No," to the girl, and the girl said, "it's the same as the last one," and Dessa said, "it's the same as the last one and the last one went in the bin because the last one was a bit over," and the girl put it down and started again, and nobody said a word about it afterwards.

"That one," Marek said, out loud, and then stopped, because it was before eleven and he had not asked anybody anything and he had said it into a working kitchen.

Dessa turned round.

"What one."

"That one was a boundary. That was you saying what the thing was allowed to be and putting it down and the person taking it deciding. That is—"

"Say the rest of it," she said, "and then say who it's for."

"I was going to say it's a fourth beat."

"Then don't." She turned back to the range. "It's Wednesday's stock. It goes in the bin on Thursdays and the bins go out at nine. That's the whole of it. And if you write *fourth beat* in that book about my kitchen then in about four months there'll be a man in a nice coat in here telling me my kitchen's a technique, and I will have taught him the sentence, and I will not have wanted to."

She did not raise her voice once in two hours and he wrote that down too, in the *Do not know* section, because he did not know where else to put it.

The fish office yard at twenty to ten on the Tuesday of week 10 had a cold that came up through the concrete and settled in the ribs, and Peta was taking a hand truck's worth of boxes in at a rate of about nine a minute, and Mal Farrow was at the end of a bench with a knife and a cigarette and a man called Reuben who was twenty-two and worked Saturdays.

Marek came in with forty boxes' worth of nothing to do and the right to stand there, and Farrow looked at him for about four seconds.

"You're not on the fish today."

"No."

"Nothing."

"No."

Farrow went back to his knife, and after a while he said, without looking up, "Then go and stand somewhere else, because the fish doesn't need a man with a notebook in it, and if he does it's the fish's business."

Marek went and stood by the scales with his hands in his pockets for an hour and forty minutes.

The market office is the only room Marek Senn had ever been in where eleven people did the same job all morning and not one of them was bored. It is also the only room he had ever been in where a plate came out of a man's hip. So he stood by the scales and watched, and the watching turned into something he had to write down in a car park afterwards because he could not do it and stand still.

Reuben was twenty-two and on the Saturday rate and had done six weeks. He was on the crates, taking a full box of roe up onto a shoulder and putting it on the run, four trips, five, six. And on the fourth trip, at the top of the run, when the load was on and his hand had come to the box and the box was settled and there was nothing left to do but put it down and go back for the next one —

He turned his head.

He turned it about thirty degrees, to the left, and looked at a man in a coat by the run who was not the foreman, and there was nothing on that man's face at all, and Reuben waited there for about a second and a half, and then put the box down and went back for the next one.

Marek Senn felt the whole of the last nine weeks go out through the soles of his feet.

It was the same shape. It was the shape from the back of a classroom above a community hall on the Wednesday of week 9, three of them, in a row, at one place, every time — a head turning towards the man who is asking. Except that there was no man in this building who was asking, and no notebook, and no scope, and no timetable on the table, and no woman with a clipboard who could have stopped it, and it had taken twenty-two years and six weeks and a Saturday rate to put it in a boy who has never been in room two.

He took his hand out of his pocket.

It was the single hardest physical act of his week. He had one question. It was a good question, it was in one sentence, it was exactly the question he had been walking round a city for nine weeks to be allowed to ask, and if he asked it Reuben would have told him, and a name and a job and six weeks would be on a page in a book, and that was the case, that was *the case*, that was a courier's balance step and a teacher's cue and a girl's palm on the copper except that this one had been *installed* in a market on a Saturday by nobody, in front of nobody, for money at the Saturday rate, out of a kit.

He got about eleven inches toward him.

"Reuben—"

Farrow's knife stopped.

It stopped for about a second and a half and then it went back to work, and Farrow did not look up and did not say anything, and Marek stood there for the length of a whole fish, and understood that a man of sixty-one had just, deliberately and without a word, given him a no on behalf of a boy of twenty-two who did not know he had been given one.

He put his hand back in his pocket.

"Never mind," he said.

Reuben said, "Yeah?" and Marek said, "Yeah," and stood there for another hour and ten minutes and did not ask him anything else.

Later, in the car park, with a fish cold store behind his eyes for the second time in a term, he wrote:

*Week 10, Tuesday, 10:20, fish office yard.*
*I have got it. Not as a source — as a thing I saw with my own eyes from four feet, on a Tuesday, with nobody performing anything.*
*Reuben, 22, six weeks, Saturday rate, crates off a truck up a run, four to six trips an hour. On the fourth trip, at the top of the run, with the load settled and his hand on the box and nothing left to do but put it down: he turned his head about thirty degrees to the left and looked at a man in a coat who was not his foreman. He waited about a second and a half. Then he put the box down and went back.*
*There is no one in that yard asking him anything. There is no lesson. There is no instructor. There is no man with a clipboard. He has been in that building six weeks and there has been no occasion, at any point, on which anybody has been waiting to tell him he may stop.*
*I have been in this case for nine weeks trying to find a fourth source. I have had one handed to me for free on a Tuesday morning and I have not got his name, and I am not going to get it, because he is twenty-two, he is on the Saturday rate, and there is no version of the next four weeks in which a plate belonging to a third-year with no licence comes out of this yard attached to his name.*
*F. saw me start to ask and put his knife down for a second and a half and did not say one word. I am not going to write down why he did that. He did it and it cost him nothing and it is not mine.*
*And I do not know who the man in the coat is. I have been in that yard three times and I have never been introduced to anybody in it, and I have been turning him over since, and the honest state of it is that I do not know whether he is a second body in a 4-19 with a bad frame, or a stand-in for a foreman who is off, or a man in a coat who works there, and it may be that I built him out of a head turning thirty degrees to the left and I would not know the difference from four feet, and I have written that down because I would rather have written down that I was wrong than have written down that I was right.*

Then, underneath:

*The cost of today's rule, in one line, because the rule is supposed to cost something and it has not cost me anything since Saturday and that means I have not been using it:*
*I wanted that name more than I have wanted anything since the Friday of week nine. And I have not taken it. And the case is no further forward for it, and I have to be honest that if I had taken it I would have had four.*

He looked at that for a while and did not cross it out, and it was true, and it was the first time in nine weeks the not-taking had cost him a number.

The ramp off the Cordage at half past two on the Tuesday of week 10 was a twelve-degree slope with a rail on one side and a doorway at the top, and it was where two couriers and a man with a hand truck worked a Saturday and a Tuesday run, and Rafi Pell came round the bottom of it at about ten past three with a bag of parts he did not own.

He did not stop at the bottom. He stopped, put the bag down, and held up two fingers to Marek, said *one* and *two* out loud with no particular hurry, looking at nothing, and picked the bag up.

"One thing," he said. "And it's going to be no, and you can have it, and I'm not going to explain it afterwards because you haven't earned the explanation and I'm not going to be a thing that gets explained about."

"Go on."

"I'm not doing my step for you." He shifted the bag. "That's it. That's the whole of the no. Not because of you. Because of what my step *is*."

He went up the ramp.

He went up it the way he had gone up it two hundred times, which was: the bag on the right, the left foot first, the two-count in the middle of the ramp where his weight went over, and his left shoulder to the rail at the top because there is no fourth beat in the thing and there has never been one, and you have to put something somewhere. The bag came off his shoulder and onto the sill and the shoulder mark took it.

He came back down and said, "That," and stopped.

"That's it?"

"That's it. There's no last part."

"Raf—"

"I'm going to give you one thing because you asked for one thing and I'm not going to be a man who takes and doesn't pay, and this is me paying, and this is the end of the trade and I'm never mentioning it again." He put the bag down and leaned on the rail. "You keep asking me for the fourth beat. You keep asking everybody. And I've been telling you for two weeks that I haven't got one, and you keep hearing that as *your technique is incomplete*, and it isn't. It's not incomplete. I never had one. Somebody had to put one in me and they didn't, and that is the sentence."

Marek did not move.

"And do you know what that is?" Rafi said. "That's not a man who's failed a course. That's a man who came out of a gap in the wall on a good day, and I'll tell you why, because you can have the reasoning now and not the feelings. Everything in that building has a card on it. Everything in that building has got a voice in it. My step came out of a courier crew and a doorway in Nine Bridges and Gus Haldane shouting *keep it up the rail* at me when I was nineteen, and there was no card and there was no voice, and I made one up out of a wall, and it has been the worst habit of my working life and it has also not once put me in a hospital. And you are the only man in this city who is allowed to be in a room with me, and you are going round telling rooms that I'm a *variant*."

"I haven't—"

"You wrote *variant* in that book in week nine and I read it over your shoulder at the kitchen table because you left it open on the table, which is a thing you do." Rafi's voice went up about one notch and came back. "I'm not angry. I'm a courier. I'm angry for about nine minutes and then I've got a bag. But you need to hear this, because you've spent nine weeks looking for people with holes in them and I have not got a hole in me, and that's the only good news either of us has had since the start of term, and if you turn it into evidence then you and me are going to have a very short friendship and I'm not doing a two-count about it."

"Deal," Marek said. "Not in it. Not as a variant, not as a source, not as anything."

"Good."

"And I'm sorry about the word on the table."

"Don't be sorry, be careful," said Rafi Pell. "Sorry's a waste. I read it in about a second and it stopped me for about a day and a half and then I was fine. Just shut the book."

He shut the book. And then Rafi picked the bag up, and went up the ramp a second time with his shoulder against the rail, and did the two-count again at the top where the two other couriers could see him, exactly the way he had said he would in a room above a laundromat on the Saturday morning of week 9, and neither of them said anything about it, which was the correct thing and was not nothing.

The Saltmarket waste loop on the Tuesday of week 10 came through the yard off the canal at about twenty to seven in the evening, and the yard had a compactor in it with a reversing alarm that was making a noise that three people on the canal path could hear, and a woman of forty-nine was walking round it.

Not round the yard. Round the machine. Nine paces, starting at the tailgate, ending at the offside front corner, at a steady walk, every single time, with her hands in her pockets.

Marek had been standing at the gate for ten minutes.

"You're the college one."

"Yes."

"You're stood in a working yard in the dark and there's a machine coming and I've got a witness now, which is you." She came round the tailgate. "So say your thing quickly and then step back six feet because I do not know you and I do not owe you a reversal indicator."

"My name's Marek Senn. I write down what people's hands do. I can't do the thing you do, I don't know anybody who can, and I'm trying to work out whether there is a piece of this in a card that's in a department in my college, because if there is then there's a whole load of people doing your job and not doing it."

The alarm stopped. She had not touched it. She had walked to the offside corner and finished her nine paces and come back to the tailgate, and the machine had stopped on its own because she had put the tip of her boot under the pedal and had done it nine paces earlier on the way past.

"Right," said Vesna Kolar. "Say that again slower and I'll tell you what I think of it."

---

They sat on the tailgate of a skip with the engine off and a cup of tea from a flask, and she listened to the whole of it — the card, the booklet, the eleven pages, the four sites, the boy with the sling — without interrupting him once, and he understood halfway through that he was doing the thing again, the thing Talia Venn had named on the telephone on a Saturday, because he had been narrating her job back to her for four minutes and she had let him, and the letting him was a professional courtesy and not agreement.

He stopped.

"Sorry. That's four minutes of me telling you a story."

"Go on."

"No. That's the shape of the thing I've been doing for nine weeks and you've been polite about it, and I'd rather you weren't polite."

Vesna Kolar drank her tea.

"Alright," she said. "So what you actually want, out of a working yard, on a Tuesday, is nine paces."

"Ten minutes ago, yes."

"Well, you've not got it, and here's why, and it's got nothing to do with me." She turned her cup round in both hands. "I don't do a check before I go in. There's no check. I walk round the machine because the blind spot moves — it comes round the front when you come round the back, and there's a man selling mackerel off a barrow on Saturdays who is exactly where the blind spot is at four o'clock on a Saturday, and he has been exactly where the blind spot is every Saturday for nine years and he is not in my way because I'm not in the yard at four on a Saturday. That's it. That's the nine paces. It's a road."

"And at the other end?"

"At the other end I'm in the Exchange yard, and there's four hundred people in there, and a compactor is not a person and it will not hit anybody who is not looking at it, and if there is anybody in there who ought not to be there then the fact that they are in there is a thing for a person in a high-vis to find out about and not a thing for my nine paces to find out about." She shrugged. "So the walking doesn't check anything. It just puts me somewhere I can see. And when I can see everything, I'm the one who decides, and there's nobody in the yard who has to say so, and I have never once in twenty-six years had somebody stand at a gate and make me explain that, and I'm not going to start."

"You've got a fourth beat and you don't know it," Marek said, and heard himself say it, and put his hand over his mouth, in a yard, in the dark, at a skip.

"No," said Vesna Kolar.

"It doesn't need a name—"

"It's got a name," she said. "It's called walking round the compactor and it is not a beat and I am not having it printed." She took the tea back. "And that's not me being hard. That's me being a driver. If that goes in a book somewhere then somebody in an office has got to ask me about it, and the day somebody in an office asks me about how I work is the day I'm a person in a form, and I've been a person in a form once and it cost me a round a week for a year." She nodded at the machine. "That was twenty-two years ago and I'm not going back into a form about my walking for a man at a college."

"Noted."

"Don't note it. Just don't come back."

"I'm not coming back."

"You'll come back," said Vesna Kolar. "Everybody comes back once. If you come back, ask me a proper question about the yard, not about me, and I'll answer that all day."

He had a bundle of eleven cables in his left hand at half past ten that night on the first floor of a laundromat on Pell Street, because he had carried them up instead of using the lift out of habit and because his right shoulder had been bad since a gate in Nine Bridges in September and he was carrying them with the hand he braced frames with.

It was not forty kilos. It was eleven cables and a roll of conduit and it was about nine kilos and he could have put it down on any landing.

He stood on the second landing in the dark with a bundle of nine kilos in his left hand and the load book in his right and did the thing he had been not doing for ten weeks, which was to try it on himself.

Anchor. He got that; the weight found his hip and his knee and the floor came up under the bad shoulder.

Load. The rail, the stairwell, the bend at the top of the flight; the whole of it arrived in about four seconds and it was not difficult, and it had never once been difficult, and he had known that since the Thursday of week one.

Close. The hand came round and the bundle came off the shoulder and onto the rail and the weight went into the wall.

And then the fourth place.

He stood in a stairwell on Pell Street at half past ten on the Tuesday of week 10 with a bundle of nine kilos against a rail and he waited for something to arrive the way he waited for a word to arrive out of his own mouth, and nothing arrived, and he waited a bit longer, and then he heard himself, out loud, in his own voice, in a stairwell, exactly the way it goes:

"*Scope—*"

He stopped it. He actually stopped it, with his teeth, mid-word, at about a quarter of a second in, and put the bundle down on the step, and sat down on it, and put his forehead against the rail.

There was nobody in the stairwell. There had not been anybody in the stairwell since the Thursday of week 1 and there was going to be nobody in the stairwell for the next four hundred days, and it made no difference whatsoever. The word was *there*, in the place it lives, at the fourth place, on the beat, with the pitch and the fall of a man of forty-seven in an old gymnasium, and it arrived about a quarter of a second early, before his own judgement, the way a foot arrives on a stair that has always been there.

He wrote it up at the table in the room above the laundromat with the bundle of cables still on the step behind him, in the smallest handwriting he owns, and it took him forty minutes, and he did not cross any of it out.

*Week 10, Tuesday, 22:30, second landing, nine kilos of cable, on my own, no witnesses, nobody asked me to do anything.*

*1. It is in me. That is the finding and I want it in this book in the first person and in this handwriting before I do anything clever with it. There is no version of this in which I am a person who has not been trained to wait at the fourth place. I have been saying for ten weeks that I cannot do the fourth beat, as though that were a thing about my nerve. It is not. It is a thing about my mouth. My mouth goes to the word before I do.*
*2. I am not writing down the word. I am not writing it in this book. It is on page three of the 4-19 card in a hundred and eleven hands and I have got it in mine and if I write it here then this book is a second copy of the card and I have decided it does not get to be one.*
*3. Four ordinary people held a better version of the fourth beat in this city on one day this week than the Institute has held in a hundred and eleven people for a term, and not one of them has ever been taught to call it that, and one of them refused in writing and one of them refused out loud and one of them does not have one at all and that is the good news of my entire term. And a fifth, who is twenty-two, has one he was never taught and does not know he has.*

*Four hands, and what they had, in their own words, and I am not going to improve on any of them:*
*K. — she names what the pan touched, she says who it is, and she leaves the pass. Eleven times.*
*F. — get it off yourself or leave it, it's your call, I've got you. (March 2011. Three ribs. Nobody had ever asked him.)*
*P. — he has not got one, it was never put in, and the wall in his shoulder is a wall he made out of a doorway and Gus Haldane shouting, and it has cost him two years and it has not put him in a hospital.*
*V. K. — she does not do a check. She walks round the machine and it is called walking round the compactor, and it is not a beat, and if it goes in a book she is a person in a form for a year.*

*And the fifth, which is not on anybody's list and is the reason I am writing all this down:*
*A boy of twenty-two called Reuben, six weeks on the Saturday rate, who turned his head at the fourth place to look at a man who was not his foreman and waited a second and a half for a word that nobody in that yard has ever said.*

*What I have at the end of today: a vocabulary. Five things in five languages. Not one comparison. Not one scope. Not one share, and I could not get a share out of any of them if I tried, and I am not going to try, and the reason I am not going to try is that the only way to get one out of a person who has not offered it is to be the person they cannot refuse, and I have been that person four times this term and each of those four times cost somebody a plate or a tendon or a pair of ribs.*

*And the four explanations are still all four. I have checked. A bad print run. A card printed against the wrong revision. A supplier assembling somebody else's book. A person who chose it. I still cannot tell them apart. A woman in a cardigan told me on Monday that I have not been choosing between them, I have been carrying the worst one round in case somebody gives me a reason to put it down, and I have written her down as the only person who has said a useful thing about it and I have not written a single word of it anywhere else.*
