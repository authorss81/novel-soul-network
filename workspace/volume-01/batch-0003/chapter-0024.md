# Chapter 24 — Nineteen Seconds

He told Rafi on the Thursday morning of week 10, in the room above the laundromat, over the mugs, before either of them had gone to work, because a boy with a sling had told him on the Thursday of week 9 that if somebody wants to know what he thinks then he comes and says it in his own voice or they wait, and that applied to a man of twenty-one as much as to anybody.

"Thursday," Marek said. "Ninth Street. Her class, seven to nine. I said I'd be in it."

Rafi put the pen down.

"Right." He held up two fingers and said *one* and *two* out loud, unhurried, with the mug in his other hand, and Marek stood there and watched him do it, and Rafi said: "You'll last about twenty minutes."

"Probably."

"No, mate, you'll last the whole thing, and that's worse, and I want to explain why so you can be annoyed about it in advance." He turned the mug round. "You don't last twenty minutes because you can't sit still. You last twenty minutes because at about eighteen minutes in, somebody in that room will do the thing at the fourth place, and they'll do it in front of a man who's come to watch, and every bone in you is going to come up and go to the fourth place with them, because that's what you *are* now. And the class won't notice. It'll go on. You'll sit there and get nothing and it'll be the two most boring hours of your term and you will have earned them."

"That's fine."

"It's not fine, it's Tuesday you get to come home and be furious at yourself about," said Rafi Pell, "and if it's not fine then you'll have gone for the wrong reason, and I'd rather you went because she'd have taken you out by the arm."

---

Ines Sarr's Thursday class on the Thursday of week 10 began at seven with a woman of sixty-two doing something to a length of rope on a bench at the front, and it finished at ten past nine, and Marek Senn sat at the back on a chair against the wall with his hands on his knees and did not write one word.

He counted. That was all. He counted things, in his head, in the ordinary way, because counting is what a man does with his hands.

He counted eleven people and two staff. He counted the number of times a person went to the fourth place — there were a great many more fourth places than he had ever imagined, because it was not a technique class, it was a class — and he counted how many of them turned their heads.

Three turned their heads.

Two of them were Institute-trained and one of them was a man of about thirty-four who had done a course at the Cordage, and all three of them found Marek Senn in a chair against the back wall with about a second and a half of delay, and he felt it three times like a coin dropping into a cup.

He did not move. That was the whole of what he did. He sat against the back wall and he did not lean forward and he did not open his mouth and he did not go and stand near anybody, and at about twenty-five minutes past eight o'clock he understood that the reason the two men in the front row kept finding him in a chair against the back wall was that he was the only person in the room who was not doing the work, and that a man who is not doing the work in a room is a thing every body in the room checks, and that no amount of not speaking fixes it, and that the only fix is to be doing the work.

He was not going to be doing the work. He had come once, in week 8, at Sarr's invitation, and the invitation was not repeated and he had not asked for it to be, and a thing that is not repeated is not a thing that is on offer.

At about half past eight a woman of thirty-one asked the room a question about her shoulder and Marek Senn had a sentence in his mouth, fully formed, about a plate and a copper contact and a load that had gone through it unreleased, and the sentence was *true*, and it would have changed that woman's week, and possibly her shoulder.

He did not say it.

He waited until the end, and then he said it to Ines Sarr in the corridor, quietly, and she looked at him for a moment longer than he expected.

"You had it at half past eight," she said.

"Yes."

"And you didn't say it."

"No."

"Why."

"Because I've said it in four rooms in nine weeks and in every single one of them it turned the room into a room I was in," said Marek. "And because she's twenty-nine and she asked about her shoulder in a class and I would have been the man who came to her class with a case."

Ines Sarr leaned on the wall with her arms folded.

"Right," she said. "Two hours and ten minutes and you took no notes and you didn't speak and you didn't once come up the front, and I've run that room for nineteen years and I want you to know what that cost you, because nobody ever tells you what it costs." She tipped her head at the room, which was emptying. "You did not get anything tonight. Not one thing for your file, and not one name, and there is nothing in this building you can go and look at in a book because of this evening. That's what sitting in it is. Everyone thinks it's an observation. It's not. It's an evening where you have to be satisfied with being a body in a chair, and most men cannot do it, and the ones who can usually can't do it twice."

"I've been doing it since the Thursday of week 8."

"Yeah, and the first time you were in here you were so busy not writing anything down that you came out of it shaking, and I could see it, and I let you go, and I have thought about it every week since." She pushed off the wall. "Go on. And come on Thursday, or don't. But don't come on the Thursday and sit in that chair for two hours and ten minutes if you're going to spend the whole of it waiting to be useful."

"I'm not arranging—"

"No," said Ines Sarr. "You're not. That's the first time I've believed it about you and it took two hours, so you'll take that as the size of the thing you've actually done this month."

Tam Arrick asked Mal Farrow on the Friday morning of week 10 at twenty past six in the fish office yard, in his own voice, on his own, with nobody arranged.

Marek Senn was not there. He was at Civic Spine 7 and he did not know it had happened for another twenty-two hours.

---

Farrow's conditions took about four minutes and there were three of them.

"One," Farrow said. "Not you. When he asks me, and not before, and he asked me, and that part's done. And what it is for is him and only him. I'm not doing it for a college and I'm not doing it for you and I'm not doing it for a man in a sling who's had a good week and is feeling brilliant. If anybody else wants watching, he can ask me himself, and he'll get what I gave him, which is probably nothing, and I won't be offended."

"Agreed," said Tam.

"Two. I can stop it, and when I say stop you stop, and you don't put the box down slowly to give me time to change my mind, and you don't look at me first. You stop like the box is hot."

"Agreed."

"Three, and this is the one that matters." Farrow put the knife down. "You want me to be able to say *no, that wasn't a release*. You've put that in your plan and I've read your face and it's the only reason I've said yes. So it's this. I say whatever I saw, in words, out loud, at the time, and you have to take it and you are not allowed to argue with me about it, ever, not on the day, not in a week, not in a corridor. And if I say it wasn't a release then it goes down in writing as *not a release*, in my handwriting, and if you then go round this city telling people I said he did one, that's the end of it and I will have had it."

"Agreed," said Tam Arrick. "In writing, if you want it in writing."

---

Tam Arrick asked for it in writing and Farrow wrote it out on the back of a market docket in biro, in a hand that got worse halfway down, and read it back twice before he signed it.

**M. F. — observer — Sennet Row / Saltmarket Exchange — from the Friday of week 10**
*What this is for: watching T. Arrick attempt one thing, once, in a working yard, and saying out loud what I saw, in my own words, at the time.*
*What it is not for: a college, a department, a report, a document, or a book anybody else is going to read.*
*Who may stop it: me, by word, and him, by word, and if either of us says stop the box goes down like it's hot and nobody writes a paragraph about it.*
*Who may read what I said afterwards: nobody, unless I am asked again, and I will say no the second time as well as the first.*
*How long this stands: to the end of week eleven. After that anybody who wants me asks me again from nothing.*
*Agreed and signed: M. Farrow.*

"Right," said Tam Arrick, taking it back. "That's the first thing anybody's written down for me that I'm allowed to hold."

"It's for me to hold," said Farrow. "Don't you be reading it in a corridor."

---

The weekend of week 10 was bad, and what made it bad was not the pain.

It was that on the Saturday he did the two voices in a queue at the market — a queue of eleven people outside the wholesale kitchens at seven in the morning — perfectly, twice, in his head, while a woman argued about fish, and the woman stopped arguing. She stopped in the middle of a sentence, and looked at him, and said, "Are you all right, love?" and he said yes, and she went on, and he got the eleven minutes of the queue back afterwards in a rush and could not account for a single second of it, and that had not happened to him in three years of doing that job.

Nobody had said anything to him in that house for three days, and the not-being-said-anything was worse than the being-told-off, and on the Sunday night he wrote eight lines in the grid and then drew a line through six of them and then wrote them again underneath the line without crossing anything out, which is what he has done with his own work since week 1 and had never once done with anybody else's.

He also broke his own column two on the Sunday, and wrote it down at the time, which is the rule and has been the rule since the Monday of week 9.

*12. Sun wk 10, 21:10. Column two says the hand is not going to be told to do anything I would not write down. I did a full run with the four-kilo bag on the table and I put my hand under it and I rolled it, and that is column two broken, and it is broken on a Sunday at nine o'clock with nobody in the house, and I am writing it down at the time and not on the Wednesday. Four kilos is inside the department's limit and my limit was the plan, and the plan is the thing I wrote to stop me, and I have written on it in pencil that if I ever break it, I break it alone and in daylight and I write it down. So: Sunday, twenty-one hundred, four kilos, hand underneath, and it hurt, and it worked, and I am not sorry, and I do not know what to do with any of those three sentences.*

---

The strap came off on the Monday morning of week 11 at twenty to ten in a room off the Ninth Street corridor with a physiotherapist called Mrs Ollerton who had been spelling her name on the wall chart since 1988.

It came off in about four seconds, with a soft sound, and the hand came out of the padded dorsal splint, and it was white and it was the size of a child's and it did not look like a hand that had done anything at all, and then in about nine seconds it filled with blood and went the colour of a plaster, and it hurt so much that Tam Arrick had to put his forehead on the table.

Mrs Ollerton held the wrist and let him.

"You're crying."

"I'm not."

"You are, and it's fine, and it is thirty-two days, which is a bit over four weeks, and that strap has been on since the Friday you had it, and I have watched about thirty of these and about half of them cry at the strap, and it is not a small thing to take off, and nobody tells you that the good bit comes after." She put the strap in a bin bag on the desk. "Index and middle finger. Open."

They opened.

Middle finger did not.

"Right." She did not write anything on it and she did not say *normal*, which Tam noticed, because he had decided in advance that if she said *normal* he would take the hand off her and never bring it back. "Ring and little finger, full range, no pain, good. Middle finger, nothing at the DIP, nothing at the PIP, and I am not going to tell you that is expected, because I do not know that it is, and you get more in a fortnight, and I have said what I have to say and it is on the card."

"What do I tell people."

"You tell them the middle finger has not moved, and that you are not to write it down as good news or as bad news until the second week of next month." She gave him a printout. "No resisted work. No lifting over two kilos. No rotation against anything. And the weight date, which is the last Friday in the term, and that is the registrar's date and not mine, and the last Friday in the term is the first day you can put a hand under a load and not one day before it, and if somebody tells you otherwise, including a boy with a bookmark, you ring this number."

He rang the number before he got home. It rang out. He rang it again and it rang out and he left a message with a boy's name and his own on it and then stood in a bus shelter for eleven minutes being furious about a form of words.

The strap went in an envelope. He wrote four things on the front of it, badly, in biro: **T. A. — wk 11 — RETURN TO DEPT — M. S. IS A SPINE MAN AND HE IS NOT MY MATE'S BROTHER HE IS A REPAIRER AND HE SAID HE'D TAKE IT.**

He gave it to Marek Senn at the Spine at half past four that afternoon, and Marek put it in the parts cage with a note and his name on it and did not make a thing of it, and Tam watched him not make a thing of it and then said, "You've made a thing of it with your face," and Marek said, "Sorry," and Tam said, "It's all right. You can have a face. Just not a paragraph."

---

The failure was on the Monday afternoon at half past four, in a room over a chip shop, with nobody in it.

That was the whole of the design and it was Tam's design, not Marek's: on the Monday afternoon at half past four on Sennet Row there is no one. Deb is upstairs. The prep cook does not come in until five. There is a table, a chair, a bag of rice that came up to four kilos in the week he wrote the plan and has been there ever since, and there is no load on the table at all unless he puts one there.

He sat down at half past four and did the first three beats at a table with a bag of rice four kilos away from him that he did not touch.

Anchor. Load. Close.

And then the fourth place, and there was nothing in it.

Not a difficulty. There was no *shape*. For two and a half weeks the fourth place had been a place where his hand could find out where a load was going, and there was no load, and his hand went to an edge that was not there and there was nothing to read, and his shoulders came down about a quarter of a second after his hand stopped, which was the *getting ready for it*, and which was not a fourth beat, and which he had written about on the Tuesday of week 9 in a hand that had gone strange.

He tried it eleven times. He got the same nothing eleven times, and then he did the two voices, both of them, perfectly, in about half a second, twice, standing at a table in an empty room, and both of them worked, and the second one still went deeper than the first.

*13. Mon wk 11, 16:35, Sennet Row, nothing in it. Eleven attempts, no load on the table, so there was nothing to find out. Then both voices, clean, twice, in half a second each, and I hate it. Everything I have got in my head is a voice and there is no room in this house for a load. Table, four kilos of rice, and no edge.*

*14. Mon wk 11, 22:40, same room, later, and this is the one I am actually frightened of. The strap came off at ten o'clock this morning and I have had a hand out of a splint all day for the first time since the Friday of week six, and everything I can do got better today. Not the useful thing. The voices. I have been good at them for three weeks and tonight they were better than they have ever been, and I could do them in a queue, and I could do them in front of a mirror, and I did them twice in a bedroom on my own because there is nothing else in the bedroom to do. That is not recovery. That is the machine getting better at the wrong job. The strap is off and I have got a hand and the hand is the only thing in this whole business that has ever worked, and the thing I have got good at is the way of not having to use it.*

*That is a real thing and it is a bad one and I am writing it down at twenty to eleven at night on the day, which is column four, which is: somebody who was never taught the card, and I want to know they said no.*

---

The Exchange back yard on the Tuesday morning of week 11 at twenty to seven was cold, and the fish was coming in off a truck, and Peta had the hand truck out, and Dessa Kwan had the back door propped with a crate, and there were four people in the yard and one of them was a man of sixty-one with a knife and a bit of cardboard and no stool that day because there had not been time.

Mal Farrow had not been asked to be the observer. He had asked to be the observer, on the Friday, in the way that people ask for things they have already decided to do: *he asked me and not before, so you can tell him I've said yes, and don't make it a thing.*

He had also brought a stool.

He put the stool down against the wall on the side of the bar that Tam could not see from, and he stood up again, and he said the only thing he had come to say, which was to Tam and not to Marek.

"I've read your bit," he said. "I've had the back half of it off a girl at the clinic and she'd not put it in the post and she read me the top of it over the phone, which I am told she is not allowed to do, and she was right and she shouldn't have and I am not going to be talking about her." He put the knife in his pocket. "So. Three things. I can stop it. You don't look at me to find out whether I'm going to stop it. And when it's over I say what I saw, in words, and you don't get a second go at my words. Agreed?"

"Agreed."

"Right." Farrow looked at Marek Senn, who was standing by the back door of a kitchen with a sheet of paper in his hand and his coat on. "You. Are you in the yard."

"By the door."

"That's the door." Farrow nodded at the door. "Go on, then. Not out of the yard, I'll not have the lad doing it in a street with a wall behind him. But back against that door, and if you move before he starts then I'll know why you moved and I'll stop it before it starts. Agreed."

"Agreed," said Marek Senn.

He went and stood against the door. He was nine feet away and he was the only person in the yard who was doing nothing, and by half past past six in the morning every body in that yard knew where he was.

---

Peta put a box of roe on the bar. It was about nine kilos and it was the same box, because Peta had been putting the same box on the same bar every Tuesday and Thursday for a month and had stopped thinking of it as an event in week 9.

Tam Arrick stood in front of it with his right arm out of the splint for the first time in a bit over four weeks, and his hand open, and his face doing something that Marek Senn could see over nine feet and could not have described to anybody.

He read the sheet to himself in his own handwriting, standing up, in the cold, at six thirty in the morning in the back yard of a kitchen.

**T. ARRICK — Exchange back yard — Tues, wk 11, 06:35**
*What this is for: one attempt, one time, at the bar, with a nine-kilo box, my right hand flat on the top edge and not taking the weight, to find out whether the thing I did in this yard in week nine still happens now the strap is off. If it does not, we do not do it again for four days.*
*What I am not going to be told to do: look at M. S. while it happens, look for anybody, and say the word. That is the first line on this sheet and I have broken it once already in a room with nobody in it and I am not writing it again.*
*Who is watching: M. Farrow, and he can stop it, and if he says stop it stops like the box is hot and nobody writes a paragraph.*
*What I want him to be able to say: no, that wasn't a release. He can say it and I have to take it and I am not allowed to argue with him about it, not on the day, not in a week, not in a corridor.*
*Who is holding this: Marek Senn, and he is holding paper, and he is not allowed in the yard while it happens, and I told him that on Wednesday and he did not argue.*
*Afterwards: he can read it. He cannot copy it and it cannot go anywhere with his name on it or mine.*
*If I lose it, it stops. That is the whole of the stop-word: if I lose it, it stops, and nobody is to talk me into a fourth go.*

He put the sheet on the bar, folded once, and did the thing.

The box came up onto the shoulder. Nine kilos, and no strap, and a hand that had been white twenty hours ago. It came up the way it had come up in week 9, in a line so plain that a man who had never been taught anything could have drawn it, and the hand came round and put itself flat on the top edge of the box, spread, on the place where the load was about to go wrong or about to be fine.

And the fourth place.

And the head did not go anywhere.

That was the thing. Marek Senn, nine feet away, against a door, in the cold, watching a boy do the only thing anybody in this city had ever seen him do — and the boy's face did not turn. It went down, and it went to the box, and the shoulders came down about half a second after the hand was on the edge, and the eyes stayed on the top of the box and moved along it, left to right, about nine inches, and came back.

Nineteen seconds.

Then the hand came off, and the box went down onto the bar with the left hand, and the right arm came in against his side, and he stood there with his eyes shut.

Nobody said anything for about four seconds. A gull went over the yard. Peta put the hand truck down.

Farrow said, "Nineteen. I counted."

"I counted too," said Tam.

"Stop it, he's not lifting anything, nobody touch him, Deb's upstairs and nobody's going up there—"

"No, I'm all right—"

"I didn't ask." Farrow was already crouching. "Look at me. Palm up. All right. That's an old injury and it's not a new one. He was in a sling a month and he is a kitchen boy and he is going to be all right, and I want it written down that I did not see him do a release and that I was not asked whether he did."

He stood up.

"Right," he said. "Now I'm going to say the part you asked me to say and you're not going to like it, and I'll say it plain so there's nothing to argue with."

"Go on."

"That wasn't a release." Farrow said it out loud, to the yard, in a voice that carried off the walls. "And I've been in this building forty years and I'm not giving it a name. It was a lad putting his hand on a box to find out where it was going, and he took it off again, and it isn't a technique, and I don't know what it is, and I'd not put it in a book with a spine on it."

"Okay."

"And the other thing." Farrow took the knife out of his pocket and turned it over and put it back. "You decided. I watched you decide. Your eyes went left to the soft corner and back and *then* your hand came down, and that's the bit I'd want written down, and not the hand, and I'd want it written down in my handwriting because I've been in this market forty years and I know exactly what happens to a lad like you if somebody tells him he did a fourth beat."

---

Marek Senn wrote the whole thing up on the back of a form at the Spine at one in the afternoon, at a bench, with the strap envelope in the parts cage under a note with his name on it.

*Week 11, Tuesday, 06:52, Exchange back yard, on the facts supplied by M. F., in his words, which is the only account of this in the world, and I have not been asked to write mine and I am not going to put mine first.*

*Witnessed. Out loud, in a yard, by a man of sixty-one with no stake, no card, no teacher, no reason to want the result, and a stool he brought himself and put where the boy could not see it.*

*Subject: first three beats, nine-kilo box on the bar, right hand on the top edge, hand not bearing the load.*
*Result: 19 seconds. No head-turn. No voice. No second voice. Eyes tracked the soft corner of the box edge left to right and back before the hand went down. Box lowered with the left hand. No assistance. No pain event.*
*Observed conclusion, in the witness's own terms: "That wasn't a release and I'm not giving it a name."*
*Witness's second statement, recorded because I asked for both: "You decided, and I watched you decide, and that's the part I'd want written down, and not the hand."*

*What this is not:*
*It is not a technique and it is not mine and it is not a source and it is not a comparison and there is no line in it.*
*It is one boy, in a working yard, in the cold, on a Tuesday, doing one thing on a bar he chose, and the only reason it is in a book at all is that the observer was somebody nobody had arranged, and the observer brought his own stool and put it out of sight, and the observer has a stop-word and a date on him and says he will say no to being asked a second time.*
*Eight seconds longer than the eleven in week 9, and the difference is entirely the strap, and the strap was off for one reason, which is that it was time, and not that anybody found anything out.*

*What it does not do, and I am writing this down on the day, in the same pen, before I have had a night to be pleased with myself:*
*It does not meet the requirement. The requirement is a public room, witnesses, a named practitioner, no substituted cue used or followed or accepted inside a scored window, and this is a back yard at ten to seven in the morning with one man, one boy, one box and a stool put against a wall, and the named practitioner in that sentence is not me and never has been and is not going to be for a very long time, and on a Tuesday morning in a yard there is no room and no panel and no window. It also would not have been worth anything if it had been in a room, because a thing four people have said no to in a market is not a demonstration, and four of them said no to it in a market, and I was one of the walls.*
*So: the thing I have been told by a stranger is the only thing a panel can act on, and I have now seen it once, and I cannot do it, and the boy who can do it is not allowed to put weight through his hand until the last Friday in the term, and on the last Friday in the term he will be six weeks post-op, and I will have a term review behind me and no licence and no date.*

*And the thing that is doing to me, and I have to write it because nobody else is going to:*
*I can do both voices perfectly. Both. In about half a second. I did them in a queue of eleven people on Saturday and a woman stopped talking and asked me if I was all right. I have got better at them every week since the ninth week and I did not ask to and I cannot stop doing it and it is the only thing in this term that has got better in me, and it is the exact opposite of the thing I went looking for, and I have written it on the grid on the Sunday night and I am writing it here so that there are two places in the world where it is written down and I cannot pretend on Friday that I have not known it since Tuesday.*

*Do not know: whether nineteen seconds at 06:52 on a Tuesday is a practice, a habit, a fluke, or a thing his hand has done for twenty years and nobody has ever once asked him about. Do not know whether it survives a bad week, a bad month, or the sound of somebody in a coat who has never been taught the card. Do not know what it is for, because it is not for anything, and that is the first thing in this whole term that has been true of anything.*
