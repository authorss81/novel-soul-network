# Chapter 23 — Four Unaccounted

The Saltmarket Exchange on the Wednesday morning of week 10 was doing about nine hundred pounds of fish through four doors and a Choir of Practice that had a hundred and forty pages of its quarterly on a trestle table and one man and one woman and one book.

Marek Senn got there at ten past ten with a list of nothing.

"I want to ask about the practicum drill sequence," he said. "Anchor, load, close, scope. It's printed in the booklet and it's taught every Thursday to a hundred and eleven third-years, and it's the oldest thing I know of in the case and I have not asked one person about it."

Ekow Nartey wrote it in.

"Before you answer that," he said, "I have to tell you what it costs, because the order in which people tell you things is the whole of what this table is and I do not want you finding out afterwards."

"Go on."

"That sequence will be in the quarterly. It's been in for eleven years, it was entered by a member who taught it in a school district for twenty-two years and retired in the year the co-operative started. What is in the quarterly is a lineage in short form. Origin, revisions, boundaries, refusals — a paragraph each, in plain language, and it is free, and it is on that table, and I will read it to you if you would rather I read it."

"Fine."

"The full sheet is a different thing. The full sheet is every revision, with dates, with what changed, with who changed it, and with what they said the reason was — the long entries, not the short ones — and the source reference for each one, and the boundaries as they were written and as they were amended, and the refusals, which are the longest part of any sheet we hold, and they are not short and they are not kind." He put his pen down. "A full sheet is five pounds and it comes out of the print fund, because it is about forty pages and the quarterly is a hundred and forty. There are four pounds and change in the tin, which is a fact I tell everybody, and which is also the reason the tin exists."

"And the other way?"

"The other way is a public-safety request. Any lineage that concerns a technique with a body in it can be requested free on a safety ground. The request goes into this book in full — your name, the technique, the ground, the date — and it is read out at the next market day to anybody who comes to this table, which is between nine and forty people, and if the request is upheld the quarterly names you as the person who asked, and it names the safety ground, and there is no mechanism anywhere in this co-operative for taking either of those back out."

Marek looked at the book.

"Then I'll do the request."

"I know you will," said Ekow Nartey. "You had that face on Monday." He turned the book round. "Spell out the ground, and spell it in the third person, and do not put *I think* in it, because *I think* is the single most expensive word in this book."

Marek Senn stood in a former ferry waiting room and looked at a man of thirty-three with a hard-backed book.

"A rescue brace supplied to eleven third-year students in September by the Nacre Institute of Applied Practice, revision 4-19, prints a boundary on the fourth beat of its own printed card and instructs in its accompanying instructor notes that the beat is not to be performed by the learner. A plate of that revision, when a load is put through it and no release is performed, has set its copper return contact in the shape of the load path. It is known to at least two named people at that department and to a Continuity Office field assessor, and four sites in this city have placed a local hold on stock of that revision on their own authority. The request is for the full lineage of the practicum drill sequence, because the drill sequence is the technique that revision is built to deliver, and the boundary-release beat of that sequence is the beat the department's own 3-08 manual says the practitioner performs and the 4-19 script says the instructor performs."

He stopped.

Ekow Nartey read it back, exactly, without changing a word, and then wrote it in the book in one line that ran off the side of the page and onto a strip of paper taped underneath, which was the ordinary way of doing it and which meant that anybody who came to the table could see the whole of it in two feet of handwriting.

"Upheld," said Marda Szabo, from the other end.

"Ma'am—"

"Upheld, and I am not going to give you any credit for it, because you have not been a member and a safety ground is a safety ground, and if it were on a ground of *my curiosity* you would get nothing today." She had her cardigan sleeves pushed up. "Sit down. Ekow is going to read you the short form and then I am going to tell you the thing that is going to spoil your week, and I would rather spoil your week on a Wednesday at ten in the morning than on your own at three in the morning."

---

The short form took eleven minutes and Marek Senn wrote all of it down.

*Origin: Nacre Institute of Applied Practice, methods group, first written 2016 for the third-year practical course. Written by staff of the group. The group did not record who wrote it.*

*Revisions: several. The most recent is 4-19. The full sheet has every one of them, with dates.*

*Refusals (short): 2019, the Institute declined the Choir's request for the methods group's working papers, on the grounds that a working group is not a public body. 2021, the Institute declined a second request. 2023, the Institute declined a third request. The Choir has asked three times in five years and has been refused three times, and the refusals are printed here because a lineage that only records the people we agree with is a lineage of friends.*

"That last line," Marek said. "Three times in five years. Somebody in that department keeps saying no to a question about a card their own students are holding."

"Somebody in that department keeps saying no to a co-operative of market traders who have no standing in a college, and they are right, and I have printed them being right three times," said Marda Szabo. "You may photograph that page if you want to and Ekow will initial it, because it is a fact about a document and not about anybody."

He photographed it. His hands were not steady and Ekow put a book under his wrist to hold the page flat, which was the single kindest thing anybody did for him in week 10 and cost a co-operative member about eleven seconds of a market morning.

"Now the bad part," said Marda.

"4-19," said Ekow. "Revision 4-19. *External. Issued under distributor pack. Design records not held by the originating practice; none supplied.*"

The market went on behind it. Somebody was shouting about a crate.

"Say the rest of that," Marek said.

"That is the whole of the entry." Ekow turned the book so he could see the corresponding line, which was in the request book and had a second sheet under it. "I will read you the plain-language version off the sheet itself, because that is what it says on the printed page, and I am not going to paraphrase it for you. It says: *This revision arrived in a box. Nobody in this co-operative has ever performed it, and no member has ever practised a technique from it, and it was entered in this book by a member who attended a demonstration and declined to be taught it. We are printing a lineage for a thing that arrived, which we should not do, because a lineage is for things people do. We are printing it because the alternative is that it does not exist in the only record in this city that says the word refusals.*"

Marek put his pen down.

"So there's nothing behind it."

"There is nothing behind it," said Marda Szabo. "The reference for every revision we hold is one line that says the change was made in house, by the methods group, and the reference for 4-19 is a sentence saying that the record of the change is not with us and was not supplied when it was issued. Ekow has written to two distributors about it. Both wrote back. Both said the same thing in the same words, which is that a distributed practice is a commercial product and its design record is proprietary."

"How long ago did you get that answer?"

"Second one, in the spring."

"And you've had it since the spring."

"We have had it since the spring," said Marda Szabo, "and there are four sites in this city holding stock of that revision, and a plate that takes a shape when nobody releases a load through it, and a request in this book with your name on it in handwriting you would recognise from a distance. So yes. We have had it since the spring."

"And the full sheet."

"The full sheet is five pounds out of a tin with four pounds and change in it, and you made a safety request instead, and the safety request is in the book with your name on it, and the sheet will be on this table on the next market day the co-operative can get to it." Ekow put the pen down. "That is what a Wednesday gets you and not a same-day. I am telling you that because I have watched four people leave this table this year believing they had the thing in their hand."

---

They came at him from three angles in the next forty minutes, which he wrote down afterwards because it was the best education he had had in nine weeks.

Marda, on the four columns: the sheet is not evidence, it is a *record of assertions*. Every line in it was written by somebody with an interest, and the only reason the Choir prints them is that the alternative is a silence that reads as agreement. "In four years I have printed four lineages that I am quite sure are wrong, and I have printed them anyway, with the year I became sure in the margin, because a co-operative that only prints what it believes is a church."

Ekow, on what a request actually gets you: not a document, a *copy of somebody's claim*, made under a freedom-of-information route by a member with a spare afternoon, and *that is the whole of our authority in this city*, and it is nothing.

And Ekow again, on the sheet itself, pointing at the foot of the printed 4-19 entry without touching it: "That last line. In the Choir's own words. It isn't a fact about the brace, it's a fact about us. We are the only record in this city of who wrote a thing and when and what they changed, and we do not hold the change for the one thing anybody has come in about. You have understood that, haven't you. You haven't come in here to be given a thing. You have come in here because you'd rather be handed a bad answer than hold an open question in your own hands for another week."

"Yes," said Marek.

"Good. Put your hand down, you've been holding that pen like it's a knife."

At the end of it, while Ekow was tearing the strip of paper off the request book and taping it into the spine so it could not be taken out whole, Marda Szabo said, without any change of tone at all:

"There is one design record in this co-operative's holdings."

Marek did not look up.

"One. Not mine. It came in from a member, in a shoebox, in a brown envelope, and the member wrote one line on the envelope and the line was *you keep this, it is the only one anybody has ever sent us*, and it has been in the box ever since." She put her sleeves back down. "And it does not come out on a request, from anybody, for any reason, including you, and the reason it does not come out on a request is not a rule. The reason is that the last time a document came out of that box on somebody's request, the person who brought it in was named in a newspaper, and we have one of those already, and she is a member, and she still comes to market day, and she does not speak to nine of us." She waited. "I am telling you it exists because it is Wednesday and there are eleven people at this table and somebody would have said it anyway. I am not telling you what it is, and I am not going to be asked, because if you ask me I have to say no, and then we have both spent a day."

He did not ask.

It took him about nine seconds and the nine seconds were the hardest work he did in week 10, and he wrote it down afterwards in one line, and the line is the rule and not a rule and he never decided which.

*W. 10, Wed, 11:05. She told me there is one design record in the co-operative's holdings and she told me on purpose, and I did not ask, and I will not ask, and it will not be asked by anybody else, because I am not allowed to arrange. Cost of not asking: unknown and total. I am not going to pretend that was easy. I nearly asked twice.*

---

The Fenns lived two doors from his mother on Nine Bridges, on the same side, with the alley between, and Marek Senn had known Yvette Fenn for eleven years through a gate at a primary school and had never once been inside her front room until he knocked on the door at about twenty past seven on the Wednesday evening of week 10.

She opened it with her hands wet.

"Marek. Is Lena—"

"She's at work. She's on until six."

"Oh." She wiped her hands on her cardigan and did not invite him in, which she had not done the other two times he had been round the Fenns' gate. "What is it."

He had prepared three sentences on the bus and they all went.

"Two things. One, I want to talk to Nell. Two, and I'm sorry about the order—"

"About the wrist thing." Yvette Fenn did not move. "Come in, then, both of you, if you're going."

The hallway was narrow and there was a school bag on the stairs and a pair of football boots and a plate on the wall with a form on it that was yellowed and had a bend in the top left corner and a box marked 4 in it.

Marek did not look at the form for about four seconds and then looked at it for a good deal longer than that.

"That's the September form," he said.

"That's the September form," said Yvette Fenn. "Go on. You're doing the thing where you don't say a sentence. Say a sentence, love, I've got the dinner on."

"The brace in Nell's bedroom is a revision 4-19 wrist unit, and there are four sites in this city holding stock of that revision on a local hold because a plate of that revision has been shown to take a shape when a load is put through it and no release is performed, and I want to take it back, and I have to be straight with you, Mrs Fenn: the hold is on four institutions. It does not cover your hallway. There is no document anywhere in this city that says you cannot have it. I am asking you for it."

Yvette Fenn stood in her own hallway with her hands wet and did not say anything for a while.

"I know what that is," she said. "I've read the thing in the Gazette. There's a man's photograph and he's the third year and the college has taken it off him." She looked at the plate on the wall. "That's a shop."

"That's a shop."

"Right." She took her apron off over her head and hung it on the newel post, which was where the apron lived. "Now. Before I answer that. I bought four of them in September at that open day. Cash. Four wrist units and a booklet. Thirty-one pounds fifty, and I have got the other two in the cupboard under the stairs in a tea chest, and there was a woman from the college with a clipboard and she was very nice and she said the second one is for a friend, and I said the second one is for my daughter, because Ama has got a wrist that goes, and I said *both* my daughters because you don't choose between them, do you, you just buy four. So: one on Nell's wrist, one on Ama's, and two in the tea chest, and that is all four, and I have not got them written down anywhere."

"That isn't what's on the form," Marek said.

"No," said Yvette Fenn. "It isn't. Look at the form."

He looked at the form.

It was a single side of A4, printed, with a tear-off stub and a carbon copy still attached by a small yellowing piece of gummed tape, and there were boxes. It had a box for the purchaser's name and address and a box for the number of units and a box for the practice group the units were for, and a box headed *supervised by*, and a signature line at the bottom, and it had a reference number printed in the top right in a typeface that was a different typeface from the rest of the page.

*Purchaser*: Yvette Fenn, 31 Cornerfield, Nine Bridges.
*Units*: 4.
*Practice group*: —
*Supervised by*: —
*Purpose (community/family use)*: for a girl of fifteen with a wrist — a girl of fifteen; Nell is sixteen, she was fifteen in September and she is sixteen now, and there is a second dash, and a second thing under it in a different pen: *and one for my older one, she does the boxes at the weekend.*

"Two dashes," Marek said.

"Two dashes," said Yvette Fenn. "And that was legal, was it? You tell me. The woman with the clipboard did not read it back to me. There was no reading back, there was a queue, it was the third week of September and there were about forty people at the tables by the car park and I had Ama in a wheelchair because of her wrist and I did not read two dashes. Nobody read two dashes."

"No," said Marek. "Nobody did. It was legal. And about ninety households did it in September."

Nell Senn was standing on the third step of the stairs in her own house at seven twenty-five in the evening with her arms folded and a brace on her left wrist and had clearly been there for some time.

"Whose name is on mine," she said.

"Nell—"

"Whose name is on mine, Marek. You told me in September that the whole city was one delivery. That was a good line. That was the best thing you'd said in a year. So whose name is on the two dashes in Mrs Fenn's hallway."

He had a case in his head. He had eleven things he could have said and every one of them was a *lead* and two of them were true sentences that would have made a sixteen-year-old girl understand more about a card in a department in a college than anybody in this city currently knows.

He said: "I don't know."

"Right," said Nell Senn.

And Marek Senn, standing at the foot of his mother's stairs in a house he had been inside four hundred times, watched his sister go back up to her own room without another word, and heard the door, and did not follow her, which was correct, and which was the most expensive correct thing he had done all week.

Yvette Fenn went up the stairs behind her daughter with a tea towel and came back down.

"Leave it with me," she said. "I'll have it off her and I'll bring it round to the Spine on Saturday, and I don't want you in this house again until somebody's told me one useful thing, and not one of them to be about a wrist."

"Understood."

"And Marek." She got the tea chest lid up under the stairs without needing to move anything else out of the way, which meant she had known where it was. "You asked me for a thing in my own hallway on the strength of a bit of card with a woman's name on it that does not apply to me. I'm giving it, because she's fifteen and it's frightened her. But don't you come and do that again without somebody older than you standing next to you, because I am not your mother and I am not your department and I am not going to be a room you can get a thing done in. Write to me. On paper. Like a person."

"Yes."

"Off you go."

---

Lena Senn came off nights at eleven and was in the kitchen for about four minutes before Marek told her, and he told her badly.

"You went to the Fenns' by yourself."

"You were on."

"I have been on for six years," said Lena Senn. "That is not a reason you stop needing a mother, that is a reason you need one more, and it is not the same thing, and I have not got the energy tonight to explain it twice." She put the plate in the rack. "What did you say to her."

"I said I was sorry about the order."

"And before that."

"I told her there was a plate in a man's hip pocket." He heard it leave his mouth in the front room of his mother's flat at eleven o'clock at night and it did not sound like anything at all. "I said there's one in a man's pocket. I said it in a hallway. To a woman with her hands wet. To a girl of fifteen standing on the stairs."

Lena Senn stood at the sink with her back to him.

"Forty minutes ago she was in my kitchen and she was telling me a thing about a form with two dashes on it and she was *excited*, Marek, she hasn't been excited about anything since August—"

"I know."

"You don't know, you were at work." She turned round. "That's the worst thing you've done since September and you did it in a hallway at seven in the evening to a family, and the reason you did it is that you could not be in a room with a question left in it. That's what it is. I have watched you do it to a nurse and to a boy with a sling and to a woman in a market and now you've done it to me in my own kitchen at eleven o'clock and I have been on since yesterday afternoon."

"Ma—"

"I'm not going to say anything else about it tonight because I have got four hours' sleep and I have to be back in at half past six, and there is a woman of ninety-one in bay four who has decided that this week she would like to discuss the state of her kitchen." She took a cloth. "And I'll tell you the thing that is actually the thing, and then I'm going to bed. It isn't the hallway. The hallway's just how it came out."

"Then what's the thing."

"The thing is that there's a plate on that wall in Yvette's hall with two dashes in it, and there were about ninety of them in September, and forty have come back." Lena Senn shut the tap off. "So you've spent nine weeks in a city of two million people going round every room you could get into, and the one that told you where the fifty are is a piece of paper in a hallway two doors down that you've been able to see since you were eleven. And the only reason you saw it tonight is that I wasn't here."

Marek Senn did not say anything to that, because there was nothing to say to it, and because his mother had gone to bed at eleven with four hours' sleep and a plate in a rack, and because on the Sunday of week 12 he was going to have to go and read the other forty.

He wrote the whole day up at twenty past midnight in the load book with a long table of entries and four headings that were not the four headings, because it was not that kind of page, and then he wrote the last four lines in the smallest handwriting he owned.

*W. 10, Thur, 00:20.*

*Cost ledger, one column, no analysis, because I have done enough analysis today to last a fortnight:*
*1. £5.00, or a name in a quarterly going to nine hundred addresses. Took the name.*
*2. Did not ask about the box. Rule three. Cost unknown and total.*
*3. Asked a woman of forty-eight for a thing in her own hallway on the authority of a card that does not apply to her. She is right and I would still have to do it and there is no better way to do it and that is the horrible part: there is no better way to do it.*
*4. Said "there's one in a man's pocket" at my sister on a stair. She is sixteen. She was excited about a form with two dashes in it and I have been the man who brings the case to the table for nine weeks and tonight she found out what that looks like from the other side of the stairs.*

*She is not speaking to me. I am not going to write in this book that she will be. I am going to write that I did that, on the Wednesday of week ten, in a hallway, on purpose, because I could not be in the room with a question left in it, and I have known exactly what I was for a year.*

*Do not know, and this is the new one and it is the biggest one I have written in ten weeks: how many people are holding a plate in a house that nobody has any reason to think about, and what the only tool in this city for finding them out is. It is a form. It is the only thing in this entire case that has ever worked, and I have spent nine weeks in rooms where people trusted me, and a form is the one instrument nobody has ever picked up, and it is going to cost exactly what M. S. said on Monday it costs, which is everybody's name, in a room, for ever.*

*And there is one design record in a shoebox in a co-operative on a market floor, and I did not ask, and on Wednesday of week 10 the city of Nacre contains one design record that has ever been given to a co-operative of two hundred and twelve people, and I am the only person in this city who knows it, because a woman in a cardigan told me on purpose, which means somebody else in the building knows it too, and neither of them is going to tell me.*
