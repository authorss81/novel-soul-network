# Chapter 27 — Volunteered

The Threadline service depot in Lattice was behind a tyre place on a road that had four depots on it and no name any of them used, and it consisted of a shed the size of a church, a mezzanine office with a counter on the ground floor of it, a goods-in bay, and about eleven thousand square feet of racking with a forklift that worked.

Petar Vance came through the goods-in bay at ten past two on the Friday of week 11 with a service case in each hand, having been at the Cordage and the clinic, and he put them down on the counter, and he did not take his coat off.

"Right," he said. "You telephoned yesterday and the girl told you the run was the twenty-eighth, and you said *I know it was the twenty-eighth*, which is the sentence of a man who already knows."

"Yes."

"Then you know it's nine days." He put his hands flat on the counter. "I've got the book. I'll do it now if you want it now, before I've been in this building an hour, because if I do it at four o'clock this afternoon I'll have had two hours to think about it, and by four o'clock I will have thought of a reason to do it properly and by properly I mean on a form with three copies."

"I've brought a witness."

"You've brought a witness." He looked past Marek Senn at the door and then back. "You have not, actually, have you."

"No."

"Right." Vance picked up the phone. "Kerry, are you free for twenty minutes. There's a statement to take and it's mine to take and I would rather it wasn't me. Can you come down." He put the phone down. "She takes the minutes. She has been here six years, she has no stake in anything, she types, and she will write down what you say and not what you mean, which is the only qualification that matters. And if I take it I will write down what I think you mean, and I have been doing that for eleven days."

---

The witness was thirty-one and called Kerry Amoah and she had a machine and a headset and she asked Marek Senn's name and spelled it two ways and had to be told which one.

The depot book is a hard-backed A4 book with a printed grid and it lives on the counter in a plastic sleeve because it has to be in three buildings a week. Petar Vance turned back to the middle of September and put his finger on two lines in the middle of the page.

"Read them out," he said.

"The Wednesday of week six?" said Marek.

"The Wednesday of week six and the Friday of week six. Nine days before you started. I wrote the dates out because you have a habit."

Marek read them out.

*Wed, wk 6. Institute kit store. Crate 3 seal lifting at the lower corner. Asked S. M. Senn whether he had seen the wax. Senn: "I don't remember."*
*Fri, wk 6. Institute kit store, second visit. Asked S. M. Senn the same question. Senn: "I don't remember."*

Both in a small engineer's hand. Both initialled.

"Right," said Petar Vance. "Do you want to add a third?"

"Yes."

"Then add it yourself and I'll countersign, and then she types it and you both sign the typing, and it takes four minutes, and then it's done and it's the truth and it is the only page in that book that is going to cost me anything." He took the pen and held it out across the counter. "I'm not going to write it for you. If I write it, it is a thing a service engineer found out about a student, and if you write it, it is a thing a student came in and said. That is a completely different sentence in a completely different language and my warranty file knows the difference."

Marek Senn took the pen and read the two lines and then wrote the third one underneath, in the grid, in a small hand that was not steady.

*Fri, wk 11. S. M. Senn states: the wax paper was on crate 3. I took it off. I folded it back over the seal and pressed it with my thumb and it went on and it held. I was in a queue of about forty people outside a car park on the Tuesday of the first week of term and I had my own hands on nine crates that day and I did it because that is what I do with a lifting seal and I did not think about it. I was asked twice and I said "I don't remember" twice, and the second time I said it because I had said it the first time and I could not think what else to do about it. I have not spoken to my mother about this and I have not put it in a document, and I am putting it in a document now, nine days late, in a depot, with a witness, because a man in a corridor on the Friday of week nine gave me the chance to do it and I did not take it.*

Vance read it twice and countersigned it with a line under the last word, and Kerry Amoah typed it and they both signed the typing, and the whole thing took nine minutes.

"Right," said Petar Vance. "That is in the book and in the file and it is dated today, and it is late, and I want to say one thing about the lateness and then I am going to do my job." He took his coat off. "You said you'd come before the twenty-eighth and you came nine days after. I would have been civil about it either way and I would have taken the statement either way and there is no version of this in which you are in trouble, and I want you to hear that from me and not work it out on your own, because a man who works it out on his own will be polite about it for a week and then decide he is a bad person."

"That's twice," Marek said.

"What is?"

"Twice now. A woman in a corridor on the Friday of week nine and you, on the Friday of week eleven." He put the pen down. "I answered a no the same way twice."

"Yeah, well. Don't get used to it," said Petar Vance. "It's not a system, it's a bloke with a book."

---

"What do you actually want," he said, at ten past three, at a table in the mezzanine office with a kettle and two mugs and a stack of dockets. "Because you've had your statement and you're still here, and people who have had their statement usually go."

"I want to see the intake pack for lot TL-4-19-0311."

"You're not allowed to see the intake pack."

"Then tell me it exists."

"It exists," said Petar Vance, "and I have pulled it, and I am going to show you the component summary and I am not going to show you the pack, and the reason I am going to do that is that it is on the summary and you would get it out of me in a fortnight, and I would rather you had it now and got it right."

He turned a sheet round.

It was a component intake summary, one side of A4, printed, with a stamp, with a lot number, a date, a supplier, a consolidation point, and a table of eleven lines.

*Lot: TL-4-19-0311. Received: first week of term, on a Tuesday. Supplier: Meridian consolidation point, lot onward. Component count: 24 practice plates across 9 crates, 1 instructor notes booklet per 3 crates, 1 card set per 3 crates, 26 boundary labels, 8 folding frames.*
*Upstream technique bundle: [ruled line, empty.]*
*Upstream licensor: [ruled line, empty.]*
*Upstream revision: 4-19.*

"There," said Vance. "Same rule, same day, both empty, and I'll tell you what I can tell you because it's the same thing Pratt's office got in a telex, and there's nothing concealed in it, and I have been sick of saying that for a fortnight."

"Twenty-four practice plates." Marek put his finger on the line above it. "One instructor notes booklet per three crates. And one card set per three crates."

"That's a supplier pack," said Vance. "It came off a consolidation point in about eleven thousand pieces and we picked our twenty-four out of it and boxed them and labelled them and that's the whole of what this company did to a rescue brace."

"Whose card set."

"Upstream's."

"Whose eleven pages."

"Upstream's," said Petar Vance. "That's the bit I want you to have, because I've been carrying it round since the Thursday of week nine and nobody has ever asked me the right question. Nobody writes the instructor's book. An assembler doesn't sit down and write a four-beat drill script with an emergency header on page four, because we haven't got a methods department and there isn't one and I've worked here for nine years and I'll put my hand on the wall. That book came in a box. There were three thousand of them. And there is nothing on any of the eleven pages, and I have had this exact book in my hands about four hundred times, and there is no name and no date and no initials on any of them, anywhere, and I have looked, because I did not used to look and I started looking in week nine."

He put both hands flat on the table.

"That's the honest end of it, Senn. Everything you're going to get out of me is on one side of that sheet. Eleven pages with nothing on them, and a revision number that says 4-19, and a lot that came off a consolidation point on the Meridian, and two blank lines ruled the same day with the same rule." He sat down. "I cannot tell you who wrote it. I'm not being cagey. I have been in this building nine years and I have never once been asked a question about a design, because the day a service engineer asks a question about a design, the design is not his problem, and the day it is somebody's problem is the day somebody pays me to ask it."

"How many lots came off that consolidation point with the same revision."

"Two more that I know of." He got a box out from under the table without looking for it, which told Marek he had been expecting the question. "TL-4-19-0307, the nineteenth of August, twenty-two plates across four boxes, and it went out to a courier crew at the west berth on the Saltmarket waterfront. And TL-4-19-0322, eighteen units, and that one went to a district clinic in the Ashfields direction that I have never been to." He shut the box. "Same revision. Same consolidation point. I can tell you that because I sign the manifest on all three and I have signed about eleven hundred manifests in nine years, and the revision is the only interesting thing on any of them, and it is the same on all three, and that is a *lot*, Senn, that is a supply chain. It is not a man. I have been looking for a man for eleven days because everybody in this case needs one and there isn't one in this building."

---

"The bulletin," Marek said.

Petar Vance went for a shelf rather than a box, and pulled a card-index drawer out of a cabinet with a label on a piece of masking tape.

"Service bulletins live here because they are numbered and they are the only thing in this company that has to be findable inside two minutes," he said. "You've read the sheet. I am not going to read you the sheet. There is nothing on the sheet that you have not got in your bag, and I have had this drawer open in front of me every day since the first week of term and it has never once told me anything." He took a hardback ledger out of the bottom of the drawer and put it on the counter between them, and opened it at a page with a strip of masking tape down the side of it, and turned it round so the writing faced Marek.

"Van book. Outbound, first week of term." He put his finger on the top of the column. "Copy one, copy two, copy three, copy four. Four lines, four dates, four signatures, and the signatures are not mine because I do not go on the van, they are the driver's, and the driver's name is on the front of the book and he is called Kev and he is not brave about it. So four of your five addresses are signed for by a man with a van who could not tell you what a boundary label was if you paid him."

"And the fifth."

"The fifth has got a date and it has not got a signature." Vance took his hand off the page so that Marek could look at it. "Copy five is the one with the registered office on it, the one with a postal town on it that is not Nacre. It went on the van on the same day as the other four, and Kev signed one, two, three and four, and then he came back to the counter with the fifth still in his hand and asked me whether he was meant to sign that one as well, and I told him to put it back on the shelf. That is the entire of my involvement in copy five, and it has been sitting in that book for six weeks with a date against it and nothing else."

He shut the book.

"So the fifth never left the building," said Marek Senn.

"No. It has been on the second shelf since the first week of term, under a pile of manifests, with a regional routing stamp on it and a postal town on it that is not Nacre, and I have never sent a van to that address and I have never sent a man to it, and if you want to know the thing I have not been letting myself think about for six weeks, it is that there is a line in my own book with my depot's name at the top of it, and a date, and a blank." He put the drawer back. "And that is your envelope, by the way. A man at my counter on the Tuesday of week nine put an envelope on this counter with Senn on it and asked me to bring it in if I came, and I brought it in on the Saturday. And I have been thinking about that envelope for a fortnight because there is one thing about it that has not been sitting right with me and it is not the envelope, and it is not the label, and it is not his handwriting. It is that I do not think he came in here for you."

"Which is."

"The routing slip was still attached." Vance turned round. "You do not put a routing slip in a hand-carried envelope. The routing slip is a copy, it is carbon, it stays with the copy, and if you are sending a thing by hand to a man who works in a repair shop, the routing slip does not go in it. And you do not write a person a note and then take the label off the front with a fingernail and leave the corner. That's a man who did not want his handwriting on it, which is fine, that's his business, and I would have done the same."

"Who was it?"

"I cannot tell you. I can tell you it was a Tuesday, and I can tell you it was a man, and I can tell you he did not give a name and did not leave one, and I can tell you he stood about four minutes at that counter and he did not sit down." Vance pulled the counter book round and turned it to the Tuesday. "There is a line against the Tuesday in the visitor column and it says *counter, unaccompanied*, and that is all, and it is a thing that happens about four times a month and it is the most honest line in this building."

---

"Senn." He had his coat on and the case in his hand. "Let me tell you the thing that is actually going to happen to you, because I have been where you are and it took me nine years to work it out and I can save you the nine years."

"Go on."

"That bulletin." He pointed at the drawer without looking at it. "*Return contact, revised for district distribution. Not a fault. Do not claim. Prior practice continues unchanged.* Right. Now. What is the date on that?"

"The first week of term."

"Three days after we took delivery," said Petar Vance. "The day after the last crate went out of this gate. And nothing had happened, and nobody had complained, and there was not one claim in this company against that revision, because there was nothing to claim." He put the case down. "So a man in an office two districts up wrote, in advance, that this is not a fault, do not claim it, and everybody who already has it should carry on exactly as they were. Five weeks before a boy of twenty went into a theatre with two tendons gone. Eleven years before a man of sixty-one stopped being able to get a full breath on a fish market, and there is still no form in this city that says what happened to his ribs."

He picked the case up again.

"That is the sentence I cannot sleep behind," said Petar Vance. "Because the day anybody in this city claims anything about that card, my job is to walk into a room and put that sheet on the table, and the sheet is true, and it is dated before there was anything to claim, and I am right, and everybody in the room knows I am right, and the argument is over in about four minutes." He was in the doorway. "That is the worst thing I have ever had on a shelf and I have had it since the first week of term and I have not told a living soul, and I have just told you, and I would like it noted that I told you in a depot about a bulletin I am not allowed to have an opinion about, and that in about a year somebody in a nice coat is going to make me regret it, and I have made my peace with that on a Monday night and not tonight."

---

The deputy came up the stairs at about half past three with a piece of paper and stopped, because Petar Vance was in the corridor with his case and did not go past.

"Sorry, Petar, are you—"

"Two minutes, Kev. He's leaving."

The deputy — a man of about forty in a very good coat — stood in the corridor and did not go in and did not leave, and there was something practised about the way he did neither, and Marek Senn put the photocopied overleaf back into his coat before the man had got the door open.

"Petar. I'm glad you're here."

"I'm on my way out, Kev."

"Right. Then this is quicker with you in it." The man turned to Marek Senn and did not put his hand out. "Marek Senn. I'm Oren Vey. Operations. I don't do service, so you haven't met me, and I've been in the district since Wednesday and I asked for the depot specifically, because I read a file on Tuesday that had four sites on it and a department that has never had one site on it."

Petar Vance said, "I'll be—"

"Yes, do, thank you." Oren Vey watched him go down the stairs and then turned back. "Three things and then I've got a train and a thing at four, and I'd rather say them in a corridor than in a room with a table, because a room with a table is where people start asking you to sign things."

"Go on."

"First: I have read the whole of your file, and the Office's, and the department's, and the file summary of a document that a co-operative on a market floor is refusing to print, and I have read the plate photograph that your own line did not have an explanation for, and I am not going to tell you I think the kit was defective, because I have read the service record and I do not believe it was, and I am not going to stand in a corridor and pretend to you that I have a different opinion because the price of my having a different opinion is somebody's operation." He was entirely composed. "Second: you have done something I have watched four other people do badly, which is to gather evidence out of people, and I would like to tell you that the version of that where you end up holding a piece of paper and a bunch of people holding a piece of you is the version where nobody wins, and I say that as a warning, not a threat, and I am not going to insult you by calling it anything else."

"And the third?"

"Third," said Oren Vey, "I would like to buy you lunch on Monday, in a place with a table, and there is a job, and I have a train at four."

---

Marek Senn stood in a corridor above a depot on a road with four depots on it, at about ten to four on the Friday of week 11, and worked out that in four days he had produced a document nobody could act on, a service list that named six people, a piece of paper in his bag he has not shown anybody, and a job.

"Which job," he said.

"The one you turned down at your college in week six," said Oren Vey. "Not the same one. Worse pay, worse hours, better building, and it comes with a card in your name that says what it says, and I am going to be extremely straight with you about the fact that I have read about a boy with a sling in a file and I have thought about the career you are going to have, and I would like to have a conversation about it this week rather than over the long weekend."

He looked at his watch.

"Monday. Half past one. There's a place on the corner by the Exchange with a table you can hear yourself in." He put a card on the counter, which was a thing nobody had done for Marek Senn in eleven weeks, and it had a name on it and a mobile number and no institution's crest on it anywhere. "I am not asking you for anything on a Friday afternoon, and I am not asking you to bring anything. I am going to buy you lunch and I am going to be straight with you about a card that everybody who trains this year is quietly hoping somebody will turn up and invent."

"That is exactly what I have," Marek said.

"Yes," said Oren Vey. "So have lunch. Bring the four sentences if you want to. I have read a summary of them in a file and I have never seen the page and I am not going to ask you for the page, and I would rather we argued about it across a table than about it in a corridor."

He went down the stairs, and Marek Senn stood there with a buff envelope in one hand and a card in the other, and the afternoon went grey over Lattice, and he did not open the card again, because it had his name on it and his name is not permission, and he had learned that from a woman with a clipboard and it was the only thing anybody had ever taught him for free.
