# Chapter 30 — Somebody Else's Wall

On the Tuesday morning of week 12, at about ten past eight, before the queue for the breakfast counter had properly formed, Marek Senn wrote a service bulletin number on a sheet of A4 in his smallest handwriting and took it down to the Saltmarket Exchange and put it on the noticeboard a month out of date with four drawing pins off Dessa Kwan's tray, under a notice about a lost cat.

He wrote six lines and no names.

*A number, and nothing else.*
*SB-4-19-03.*
*It is a service bulletin number. It is not a fault, not a recall, and not a claim. A company can tell you what it means if you ask them for it, and they are not obliged to.*
*It is written on the wall because a number can be followed and a name cannot. If you have a card with a number like this on it and you do not know what it means, this is the number to say out loud.*
*Take this sheet down. It is here for anybody.*

He did not put his own name on it, which took him about four minutes of standing in front of a noticeboard, and the four minutes were the most disciplined thing he did in the term.

---

Rafi Pell read it at half past six that morning over the mug in the flat above the laundromat, standing up, with his coat on, and then he said, "That is going to be a very bad week for somebody."

"Probably."

"You're going to get a letter."

"I know."

"No, mate, I'm not doing the thing where I tell you what I think you already know, I've been told off for that." He held up two fingers, said *one* and *two* out loud over the table without hurrying, and put them down. "I'm saying *you're going to get a letter* the way a man says there's a bus in ten minutes. Because that number's on a wall in the one room in this city that nine hundred people walk into, and it isn't your number, and it isn't his, and you didn't write anybody's name, and that means the second person who rings that number isn't going to mention you. That's not a complaint. That's the first time you've done something in eleven weeks that I don't have to worry about."

---

The first one came at half past nine and it was a man of about fifty from a technical college in Lattice who wanted to know whether the number on the wall was about the collars, and who went away slightly annoyed when it was not.

The second one came at ten, and she was sixty-eight, and she had come in through the front rather than the back, and she stood at the noticeboard for a while, and then she came to the counter and asked the girl on the till, and then asked Marek, who was in the room.

"Are you the one who put that up."

"Yes."

"It's about these?" She had her right hand out of a cardigan pocket and she did not take it out. "Mine's a wrist."

"Could be. Would you like me to write down where the number's been for you, or would you rather just have the number?"

"Go on then."

He wrote the number on the back of a docket and gave it to her, and she put it in the cardigan pocket, and then she said, "You've been up there three years."

"Sorry?"

"You've been up there three years." She nodded at the ceiling. "Above the shop. I take your washing. I've had this since before you moved in and I did not know there was a number."

---

Petar Vance rang the Spine at eleven o'clock on the Tuesday of week 12, and Marek Senn took it on the bench with a resistor in one hand.

"Senn. Petar Vance, Threadline service depot, Lattice. I'm going to say a thing and then I'm going to ask you a question and you're allowed to say no to the question."

"Go on."

"SB-4-19-03 is on a wall in the Saltmarket Exchange." Vance said it without any heat in it at all, which was worse. "I've had eight calls this morning. Four of them are our own service staff, who have all read exactly what I have read, which is that there's a sheet of A4 on a noticeboard with a service reference on it and no allegation in it. Three of them are trade, who are asking me whether it's a recall. One is a journalist from the district paper, and I have given her the service notice on its merits and she is going to write four hundred words about a noticeboard."

"I didn't name anybody—"

"I know you didn't, and that is why I'm ringing you instead of reading about it." A chair went back on the other end. "So here is the thing, and I'm going to say it as a service engineer and not as a man, and then you can put the phone down. That number is issued from an office two districts up, and the only office in this company that writes about a practice is that one. The distribution list on that card has five addresses on it and four of them are in this city and one of them is a registered office with a postal town on it that I have never sent a van to, and that office is not on the distribution list, because the office *is* the distribution list. So the day a number goes on a wall and nine hundred people a week read it, that office finds out inside nine days, from a trade, from a paper, or from one of us."

"And then?"

"And then it stops being a number." Vance's voice went about half a tone down. "Right now it's a reference and a reference is a thing a person can look up. The moment somebody in that office hears about it, it becomes a sequence, and a sequence has a custodian, and a custodian has to be able to say who started it. And the first name in the sequence is going to be mine, because I am the service engineer on that bulletin in this district and there is a book in this depot with your initials in it twice and once of my own, and I have been in this building nine years and I have never once been able to say a customer's name in a room, and I would like to keep it that way."

Marek Senn stood at a bench in a repair shop in Lattice Ward with a resistor in his hand.

"I'll take it down," he said.

"You'll do what you'll do," said Petar Vance. "I'm not your department and I'm not your mother. I am telling you the price of the wall so that you know the price of the wall, and then I'm going to write this conversation down in a book in this depot at half past eleven this morning, which I am going to do because I write everything down, and I am going to do it whether you ask me to or not."

"Petar."

"Yes."

"I'm not taking it down."

"Right." There was a pause of about two seconds, and it was not a hostile one. "For the record, Senn — that's the correct decision and I want it noted that I think so, and I have got no way of helping you and I have just told you the cost of it in a way that is not a threat. I am going to ring the district office and tell them to expect a call about the noticeboard, and I am going to tell them the truth, and then that is out of my hands and I have a service round at the Cordage this afternoon and a plate at the clinic that is a woman's own stitching and I am not going to be late for it."

He put the phone down. Marek Senn stood there for a while, and then he wrote the call up on the back of a fault form, dated, with the time on it, and signed it, and put it in the file, which was a thing he had never done in his life with a thing anybody had said to him on a telephone.

*Cost of the wall, named and dated, on the record, at 11:20, Tuesday of week 12: it becomes a sequence, and a sequence has a custodian, and the first name in it will be a service engineer's in a depot in Lattice. I am not taking the sheet down. I am the man who put it up, nobody's name is on it, and it stays up.*

---

Aurel Kosta came at half past four.

He was fifty-eight and he had driven a shunter for twenty-six years and he came in with his hands still warm from a cab and asked whether the number on the noticeboard was about the loading card, and Marek Senn said that he did not know, and that he had written the number up because a number can be followed and a name cannot, and Aurel Kosta said, "That's the first thing anybody's said to me about it that isn't a sales job," and then he stood in the doorway of a repair shop in Lattice Ward and talked for twenty minutes and would not come in.

"There's a card on the wall in the yard office off the Meridian," he said. "Been there since I started, which is thirty-one years ago, which is longer than the yard has had this name. Blue card, bit of a bend at the top corner where somebody's sat on it. It says *load secured — released by yard control*, and under that, where there'd be a line for a name and a time, there isn't one, there's just a rule, and under the rule it says *release on horn*."

"Who made the horn?"

"Yard control. There's a horn on the control post." Kosta put his hands in his pockets. "You get the load up, the wagon's on, you've got the pin in, and then you stand there and you wait for the horn, and then you take your hands off. Every time. Thirty-one years, and there's about forty men on that yard and I could not tell you one that doesn't do it that way, and the new ones ask about it, and the answer is *that's the card*."

"Does anybody teach it?"

"It came in a pack." He shrugged. "A training pack. From the district, years back. Everybody's got a pack like that in a drawer. It came in with the paperwork on the day the district took the yard over, and it's got about thirty cards in it and yours is one of them, and there's a date on the front of the pack, and I will not swear you to the month, but the year on it is the year I started, so it was somebody's card before it was ours."

"Did it come from the college?"

"From who?"

"The Institute. Four-19 kits. Practicum cards."

"No," said Aurel Kosta, with more force than anything else he had said in twenty minutes. "No, it didn't. There's no school in that, that was never a school thing, that's a yard thing, that's a district thing, and I'll tell you the exact thing that made me notice: my mate Vic went to that college. Twenty-two, thirty years ago. Went to the technical side, was a fitter. And Vic does not have a horn, because Vic did the old way, and Vic's way was you said it out loud to yourself and you took your hands off, and Vic has never once in his life stood on a wagon waiting for anybody in a control post, and Vic is a stationmaster now."

Marek Senn wrote it down on the back of a fault form with a date and a time, and asked the next question, which he asked carefully, in one sentence, and got the answer he expected.

"And your shoulder," he said.

"2019," said Aurel Kosta. "Third wagon, shunt went in crooked, and I had a bad one for two years and I've never been right and I never went to anybody, because you don't, because you're on the Saturday list and if you go to anybody about a shoulder you come off the list, and there's no form for it anywhere and there's nobody in this city that's ever asked me." He stopped. "And I'll tell you the other thing because you'll not ask me twice. That card is on the wall in that office and anybody can walk in there. It's got a chair and a window and a kettle in it and the door's open from six. If somebody had walked in there in 2019 — one person, any person, four of them have a plate and it's in a clinic on the Saltmarket route — they'd have written a very short letter."

---

He went, and Marek Senn put the fault forms in the file and went and stood at the bench for a while with a marker in his hand, and then he telephoned the Exchange, and Ekow Nartey answered, and Marek asked one question, in one sentence, and Ekow wrote it down.

Ekow Nartey read it back on the telephone from a market floor to a repair shop in Lattice, in a small flat voice.

"*A request is entered in the request book: is there a published lineage for a load-secure release taught on a district transport authority training card, in use at a goods yard off the Meridian Corridor, and the pack it came in is dated on the front, in which the release is performed on a horn sounded by a person in a control post, and in which the card bears a word and no performed step underneath it. Requester: Senn, M., third year, no licence, Tuesday morning of week 12. Status: not held. Reason not held: no member of this co-operative has ever practised it, and the card is not a technique, and a lineage is for things people do.*"

"Read the last line again."

"*A lineage is for things people do.*" Ekow paused. "That last paragraph is not mine, that last paragraph is Marda's, and she has just come over and read it over my shoulder and she says leave it in exactly like that, and that is the first time in four years she has wanted a line in a book that we cannot help with."

---

He did not say no to the card in his coat.

He said so, out loud, on the Tuesday evening of week 12, to a man on a couch in a flat above a laundromat, who had been doing a sum for an hour and had not been listening to it.

"I'm not going to say no to it yet."

Rafi Pell put the pen down.

"Right," he said.

"I'm going to wait and see what happens in that yard on Wednesday, and whether a district authority has a card of their own on a wall somewhere, and I am going to wait and see whether the woman at the laundrette rings that number. That's it. That's the whole reason."

"Two." He turned a finger over twice out loud, unhurried, and then put his hand down. "I'm not going to tell you that's a bad reason, because I don't know if it is and neither do you. I'm going to tell you it's the first time you've said *I'm going to wait* about anything in about three years, and you know exactly how many times that's happened, and I want you to notice that I noticed it, and I want you to notice that you're not going to be able to do anything with me noticing it."

---

At half past eleven that night he ruled a clean page in the load book and gave it four headings and did it in one sitting, and it is the shortest of the long pages, and it is the last one in the term that has a name on it that is not his.

**WHAT I KNOW — four things, of which three are documents and one is a man on a wall.**

*1. TRC-419-88. A design approval, raised the eleventh of August, approved the fifteenth, four days, and the nine crates went out in the first week of term. Three paragraphs of reason, and the reason is good, and the card was not to be issued to households, and about ninety households had it four weeks later on a form with a dash in it.*
*2. A service bulletin, issued three days after the last crate went out of the Lattice gate, saying not a fault, do not claim, prior practice continues unchanged — written before there was anything to claim, and it is the reason a service engineer in a depot cannot sleep and the reason a company can say four words in any room in the country.*
*3. Four sites on a distribution list, put there by a company, before any of this, and a fifth address that is not in this city, and a number on a wall in Saltmarket that nine hundred people a week can read.*
*4. A card in a goods yard office off the Meridian Corridor, blue, bent at the corner, dated on the front of a training pack from the year a man of fifty-eight started, that says a load is released by yard control and tells a man to release on a horn, and a man of fifty-eight with a shoulder out of 2019 that went in front of nobody because there is no form and going to anybody about a shoulder means coming off the list.*

**WHAT I WANT — one thing and it has moved.**

*5. Not the licensor now. The *sequence.* Four thousand one hundred and six bundles in this district, nine hundred and forty blanks, and now a card in a yard that no school supplied, from a pack from a different year, from a different kind of company, with a man in a control post doing the fourth beat with a horn for the last four years to about forty men. Whether those are one thing or two things or nine things. Nobody in this city can count them. Hollis Pratt counted nine hundred and forty on a Sunday with a pencil and was frightened of the precedent, and he was right, and the precedent was not the hard part.*

**WHAT IS NOT EVIDENCE — the six from week 9, and one new one, and the new one is the same shape as the old ones.**

*6–12. T. V.'s brace. The hip variant. M. F. R. P.'s step. O. F. Three people who agree. A boy of twenty-two at a market who turned his head at the fourth place. Four noes in a market on a Monday. All of it stands. None of it is a source and none of it is going to be one, and the week I stop writing the same six lines is the week I have started arranging.*
*12. NEW, and it is exactly the shape of the week 9 six: four noes in a market, and R. B. giving his name out loud to a stranger, and D. K. saying *there is no machine in this city that can hear it*, and I. S. saying *I would use it on the first hard afternoon*. All of it true. None of it mine. The room was not built out of anybody who is fond of me and I said nothing in it for seventy minutes and the only reason I said nothing is that the room was already full.*

**WHAT I DO NOT KNOW — three things.**

*13. Who signed TRC-419-88, and where the record is, and who the member of the Choir is who copied it by hand and has not been named and would not be printed if they asked. One person in this city. Possibly two counting a woman in a cardigan. I am not going to be the man who makes them findable, and I have written that sentence three times in three weeks and I will write it a fourth time if I have to.*
*14. Whether a district transport authority has a card of their own on a wall in a building, and what is on it, and how many men are standing on wagons at this moment waiting for a horn in a voice they did not choose, in a card they have never read, in a yard four miles from Civic Spine 7.*
*15. What happens on the Wednesday. There is a monthly meeting at the Exchange on the first Wednesday and a noticeboard a month out of date and I have no vote in it and neither does T. V., and anybody who stands up in that room and says *this is important* gets four minutes and is then ignored, and there is nothing in this entire book that is a plan for standing in a room and being boring for four minutes and then being very good at something else.*

**AND THE ONE THAT IS NOT UNDER ANY OF THE FOUR.**

*The demonstration requirement is still hanging with nothing behind it. There is no date, no room, no licence, and no technique, and the last public room in this city in which anybody performed a release for the purpose of a panel had four people say no to it in a market on Monday, and the person who demonstrated it said the sentence himself before anybody else in the room did, which I have written down twice now and will not write down a third time because it is not mine.*

*The term panel adjourned on the Friday of week eleven with no matter before it. The variance stays open at the department and the next sitting is at the end of term, and a woman in an office in the practical department is still going to have to stand in front of forty-one people and defend four sites, and she has not asked me for anything, and she is not going to, and I have to be able to stand in my own life knowing that the only thing I have ever handed anybody in this case was a document and a date.*

*And the last of it. I have been a school problem for eleven weeks. I have been a school problem because a card in a college store room was printed against a practice written by a methods group in that same college, and that made it a school matter, and the Continuity Office said so in writing without ever saying it, and a man of forty-five in Nine Bridges worked it out on a Friday over a cup of tea four months before I did.*

*It is not a school matter. A horn in a goods yard four miles from here is not a school matter. A woman who takes my washing has had a plate on her wrist since before I moved in and there is no form and no letter and no name. A plate that is on a list because four people in four buildings acted on their own authority and did the only decent thing anybody has done in this term is now a thing with an audit programme attached to it, and a plate in a man of sixty-one's hip is on a list, and a brace that came out of a hallway in Nine Bridges is in a tea chest under a stair, and the form it came with had two dashes in it.*

*The hole is not in a card. The hole is in a supply chain that starts somewhere outside this city, at a function with a reference number, and goes out through a company that prints what it is given, into a car park, onto a form, and into about fifty houses, and into a hip pocket, and into a corridor in Nine Bridges that no school supplied, and into a goods yard off the Meridian where a man of fifty-eight stands on a wagon and waits for a horn in a voice he did not choose.*

*And there is a woman at a laundrette under my own bedroom who has had a plate since before I moved in, and I have known her for three years as a woman who takes my washing, and I did not ask, because I have spent eleven weeks in rooms where somebody was the answer, and I was in the wrong rooms.*

*Which is the answer to the only question anybody has ever actually put to me in ten weeks, and it was put to me on the Wednesday of week nine by a woman of twenty-four in a vehicle on the Saltmarket route, and I have had it written down in this book ever since and have not once been able to do anything with it until tonight.*
