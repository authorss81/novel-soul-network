# Chapter 21 — A Queue Is a Map

On the Monday morning of week 10 Marek Senn copied four sentences onto the back of a work order in a hand he had never used for anything, folded it in four, and put it in his breast pocket, and then sat at the back of a practical class in room two for fifty minutes and watched a man of forty-seven teach a hundred and eleven students a shape with a hole in it.

He had been in the class. He was not in the class. There was a difference and it was held in a file somewhere and he had stopped asking.

The four sentences were these.

*Stop arranging.*
*Do not chase.*
*Ask one person one thing at a time, and take the answer.*
*Stop believing that being right is the same as being believed.*

He had written the fourth one twice on Saturday night and then added half a sentence to it, and the half-sentence was: *I have known that since the Thursday of week one and I have been going round the city trying to be believed.* He had folded the paper so that the half-sentence faced inward, which was a small thing and was the only preparation he had made all weekend.

---

Darla Vint was at the counter at ten past nine with her coat still on, because she had come in from the rain, and she had a way of looking at somebody that made it clear she was already three minutes behind.

"Senn. If it's about the file I can't and if it's about the stores—"

"One thing," Marek said. "The purchase docket for lot TL-4-19-0311. I want to make an application for the department's own copy. I don't want to see it today. I want to know which form it is and what it costs and who can see that I asked."

Darla Vint stopped with her hand halfway into a drawer.

"Say the last part again."

"Who can see that I asked."

"That's not the part I want again." She put the drawer back. "Everybody can see that you asked. That's what forms are. You fill in a form and the form has a line for the applicant's name and the name is on the front of the folder and the folder goes in the rack and the rack is in a room that twelve people walk through every day of their working lives. A form is the least private thing in this building, and half the people who file them have never read one."

Marek had not thought about the part where his name went. He had thought, for about nine days, about the part where the document was.

"What form," he said.

"PD/9, access to a department record held under a purchase series, and it's got a box for the purpose and the box is a free text and I am telling you that what you put in that box is the only part anybody will ever read." She wrote something on a slip and pushed it across. "And it takes eleven working days, and I'd put it in on a Monday because Mondays get put in the tray and go up on the Tuesday, and if you put it in on a Friday it sits."

"Thank you."

"Don't thank me, I've told you where it hurts." She took the slip back and filed it in a spike on her left, which was a spike she used for things that were not yet forms. "Senn — and this is the same voice I used in week seven, so you know it's the serious one. If you write *fraud* in that box, you will get a different answer than if you write *provenance*. And I am not telling you which one to write, because I have been the woman who rewrites other people's words in this building for eleven years and I am extremely aware of what it looks like when I start doing it for free."

---

Bo Halloran was not at the Cordage in the middle of the day. He was at the Cordage in the middle of the day, in fact, because a rope works does not care what time it is, and he took the call on a mobile in a shed and shouted over a machine.

"Senn. No."

"You don't know what I'm asking."

"You've asked me four times in nine weeks and you're asking me a fifth, so no." The machine cut out. "And I'll tell you what it is so you don't waste the call. It's *come and be an exhibit at the Cordage*. You've come four times and every time you've come with a question that ends with me standing in it. I was in a room for an hour and a half on a Wednesday and I took four pounds off a woman out of her own petty cash and I stood in front of nineteen-year-olds and told them what a sack of sand does, and that happened once, and you can have it once. I've got twenty-nine years on the water and a dead hand and fifteen plates in a bucket on a high shelf that I took down off my own wall, and if your name goes on a piece of paper with mine under it then this is a rope works and everybody in it knows everybody, and there'll be a thing said at the market on a Friday."

"Understood."

"Are you all right? You sound odd."

"I'm all right," said Marek, and stood in a corridor holding a dead telephone for a second longer than he needed to.

---

Anneke Roos was in the corridor outside her office at a quarter past ten with her coat on and a folder under her arm and the expression of a woman who had already done the arithmetic on the week.

"I'm going to say no and I'm going to say it in about forty seconds and then I'm going to go and do a thing I have to do," she said. "What is it."

"I want to know whether you'll enquire. Design. Not a defect — a design enquiry, about who approved revision 4-19 and under what authority, put to the licensing side in the ordinary way, with your name on it and not mine."

"No."

"Ma'am—"

"Four seconds of it, and then I'm going." She did not raise her voice and she did not slow down. "I have a review on the Friday of week eleven with forty-one people in the room and the only sentence I have got to stand up and say is that this department took a documented action on a documented variance. If I put a design enquiry into the world this week, from this office, on a third-year's evidence, then what I say on the Friday is that this department also opened a licensing investigation it has no standing to open, and the licensing side will write back and the letter will say the words *outside the department's remit*, and that letter will be in the same file as my forty-one people, and I will have spent the only thing I own."

She stopped, and looked at him, and something in her face moved and she put it away immediately.

"You want to know the honest part."

"Yes."

"The honest part is that I would have had a real reason," said Anneke Roos. "Six to nine months, a new lot number, a form, a stamp, a file — and I would have got it, and I would have got it because I have been in this building nineteen years and I know exactly which sentence makes a piece of paper go into a tray. And it would have been a lie of omission dressed as diligence, and it would have used up the one thing that makes a woman of fifty-four dangerous in a building where she has no friends, and I would have done it for a boy who cannot read two documents aloud in a room without a witness in it." She straightened the folder. "No. And I want it written that the reason is the review and not your case, because the reason is the review, and I am not going to stand in front of a panel in a fortnight having also said no to the only person in this building who has a document nobody can unwrite."

"That's fair."

"It's not fair. It's arithmetic." She opened her door. "Ask somebody who isn't frightened."

---

Marek Senn got as far as the stairs before he understood what had happened in the hour since the counter, and he sat down on the third landing with his back against the wall and the folded work order still in his pocket and found that he was not angry about any of it.

That was the part that took him about four minutes to notice, and it took him a further six to work out why.

Because every single one of them had told him something.

Vint had told him that a form is a room with his name in it and eleven working days in it. Halloran had told him that a rope works is a small place and a name in a document travels through it in an afternoon. Roos had told him that the department's entire remaining strength is one sentence spoken out loud on a Friday, and that he was on the same piece of paper as that sentence.

Three noes, and he had three facts he did not have on Saturday night, and not one of the three had cost him anything, and all three of them had been given freely by people who had no reason to give him anything and would not have given him anything if he had asked twice.

He got out his load book on the landing, which was against the rules in a stairwell of a working building, and wrote with the book flat on his knee.

*Week 10, Monday, 10:40, the third landing.*
*Three noes before eleven o'clock and I have come out of it with more than I came in with, and I want to write down what that is before I lose it.*

*It is this. A no is not the end of a thing. A no is a location.*

*A yes is worth nothing. A yes is what you get when a person is fond of you, or late, or in a bad mood, or a bit guilty, and I have been living on four of them since the Thursday of week one. A no is a person telling me exactly where the edge of what they'll carry is, in a sentence, at a cost to them, and no two people in this city have the same edge, and I have been treating every no as a wall to go round instead of a line on a map.*

*Three noes, three facts I did not have at eight o'clock this morning:*
*1. My name on a form is not free and is not private. If I get the department's own purchase docket, my name goes in front of twelve people, and what I write in the box is the only part anybody reads. This means the docket is not just a document. It is a document with a performance in it.*
*2. The Cordage is small. Halloran will not be in anything with his name under mine, and he is right, and it is the same reason I am not in anything with Farrow's name under mine, and it is the same reason T. A. walked eleven minutes to a laundromat instead of telling a second person.*
*3. Roos's whole position is one sentence on the Friday of week eleven. If I push a design enquiry into that office this week, the sentence dies and so does the four-page file and so does the four-page file's only useful sentence, which is the demonstration requirement.*

*And the one underneath, which I am less sure about and which is the actual lesson:*
*I did not want to argue with any of them. I have wanted to argue with somebody in this case for nine weeks and I have never once wanted to argue with one of the three of them. I did not want to be right in front of them. I wanted the man on the market to say that the four houses are a school matter.*

*That is the whole of the wanting. Stop it. Not because it is shameful. Because it is unearned.*

He read it back, and did not cross any of it out, and went down to room two for the last ten minutes of the class with his hands in his pockets and a pencil behind his ear and nothing at all in the front of his mind, which was the hardest four minutes of the day.

---

The Saltmarket Exchange was a former ferry waiting room with trestle tables and a noticeboard a month out of date and a parts bin at the end with dead relays in it that people were allowed to take for nothing if they cleared the shelf.

The Choir of Practice had a trestle table of their own at the wet end, next to the noticeboard, and it had been there on market days for longer than the market had had a name.

Marek had never been near it in nine weeks of walking through this room, and he had walked through this room on the Thursday of week six, and on the Wednesday of week nine, and on other days he could not date.

There were nine people at the table, and a tenth chair, and the ninth person was a man of about seventy with a hearing aid and a stack of paper-clipped cards, and he was on the point of going.

Marek stood about six feet away for a while with his hands behind his back, and then he thought of a rule he had written on Saturday night, and he said it out loud before he could improve on it.

"I'm Marek Senn. I'm a third year at the Institute with no licence. I want to ask you one thing, and if the answer is no I'll take it, and I won't come back with a better version of it this week."

The woman nearest the middle of the table was about fifty and had a cardigan with a pen in the pocket and a way of looking at a person that was not unkind and was not soft. Beside her a man of about thirty-three in a market coat was writing in a hard-backed book, and he put his hand flat on the page when Marek started, which Marek understood to mean *hold on, I am the one who takes the notes*.

"Sit down," said the woman. "Are you the one from the Spine?"

"I'm the night repairer at the Spine, and I'm not here about the Spine."

"Then sit down anyway, because you're standing in the way of a seventy-year-old man's hat." She waited until he was on the chair. "Marda Szabo. I do the lineages. Ekow keeps the request book, which is the only important thing on this table, so if you want anything written down you say it to him and not to me. Go on. One thing."

"Does the Choir hold a published lineage for the third-year practicum rescue brace, revision 4-19."

"No," said Ekow.

"Then what I want is—"

"You've asked your one thing and the answer was no, and you have just asked for a second one in the same breath, which I want everybody at this table to notice, because he did it inside four seconds and he did it to a man holding a pen." He wrote something down. "Say the rest. It doesn't count as a thing you asked, it counts as the thing you are doing."

Marek sat with the back of the chair against his knees and had about six seconds.

"I'm not here to complain about the brace," he said. "I'm here because four sites have stopped it and I can't say why in a sentence anybody can write down. I have a booklet. I have a card. I have a plate that set its own contact in a man's hip pocket. I have a department that says the transcript line is the truth. And I cannot find out whether anybody chose to leave the fourth beat out of it, and I wrote down four ordinary reasons in week five and I have been unable to tell them apart ever since, and I would like to know whether the Choir has ever published a lineage for a technique that is sold in a box."

Somebody at the far end said something that was not quite a laugh.

Marda Szabo turned her chair round.

"Right," she said. "Now we're talking. That is a question and it deserves a real answer and it is going to take about four minutes, and you are going to sit there and not say anything, and at the end of it you are going to be wrong about something in front of nine people, and one of them is me, and I am going to be the one who says it."

"Go on."

"No. That's mine."

Ekow turned the hard-backed book round so that Marek could read it. It was a column ruled in three, in a small neat hand, and the last forty entries were in a completely different hand, and every one of them had a name in it.

"Here is what we are," said Ekow. "Two hundred and twelve members. Nurses, repairers, two teachers, a midwife, four drivers, a man who mends organ pipes. We meet on market days on this table and we do two things. We answer questions from anybody who walks in, and we keep the request book, and both of those are in the book with the date and the name of the person who asked."

He turned the book another fifteen degrees.

"Anyone can ask. There is no fee for asking. If a thing is in the quarterly you can read it here free, and I will read it to you if you want it read, and the quarterly is four times a year and it is a hundred and forty pages and it is printed in the district and it goes to nine hundred addresses, and every copy of the last one is on that trestle table and you are welcome to sit down and read it until I have to move the table for the fish."

"Then why is it worth anything?"

"Because of the four columns," said Marda.

She turned her own sheet round.

"Origin. Revisions. Boundaries. Refusals," she said. "That is the whole of it and there is no fifth column and we have argued about that for eleven years. Origin: who wrote it, and where, and what they were doing at the time, in a sentence a person can understand, not a department's word. Revisions: every time it was changed, when, and what changed, and by whom, and — " she tapped the second column twice, "— and against every change, what they said the reason was. Boundaries: what the technique does not touch, and what it may not be used for, and who may stop it. And refusals: what we asked for and were told no."

"That's —" Marek started.

"Read the rest of the sentence," said Marda.

"— that's everything. That's the whole record."

"It is the whole record of everything we have been *given*," said Marda Szabo, "by two hundred and twelve people who mostly cannot write, out loud, in a room, to a stranger, what they changed and why. And it is the only record in this city that says the word *refusals*, and the only reason it says the word is that I spent four years asking a district authority to tell us what its emergency procedures had deleted, and they wrote back and said that deletions are not a category." She put the sheet down. "It is also a co-operative of eleven people on market day with a hundred and forty pages four times a year and a printing bill, and it is capable of being wrong. It is capable of being unkind. Ekow is going to explain the part where we are unkind, and then I'm going to tell you what is wrong with you, and you're going to take the first one."

Ekow turned the book round again.

"Everything we publish about a person is published about them by name," he said. "Corrections, disputes, requests, refusals, all of it, in the quarterly, in the column, with the date. We do that because a lineage that only records the people we agree with is a lineage of friends. So when somebody asks us a question and they are right, their name goes in the quarterly, whether or not they wanted it to. And when somebody asks us a question and they are wrong, their name goes in the quarterly, whether or not they wanted it to, in the corrections column, under their own name, with the date, and it does not get taken out when they are proved right later, because the correction was also true and it was also published."

Marek looked at the book.

"What have you written about me?"

"Your name, the time you arrived, and the thing you said at the door." Ekow turned the book so the others could see. "M. Senn, third year, no licence, asked after a published lineage for the practicum rescue brace 4-19. Says he wants to know whether anybody chose to leave the fourth beat out. Correct so far."

"And you read all that out."

"I read all that out," said Ekow, "because it is the book and it is not a private book, and the next person who comes in gets it read to them as well, and that is the entire arrangement and it has been the entire arrangement since the second year."

Marek said, "Thank you," and then heard himself, and took it back. "That was reflex. What I mean is — I understand, and I'd like to know the cost."

Ekow wrote a line.

"Reading a quarterly is free," he said. "Requesting a full lineage sheet for a technique we hold, where the sheet is longer than what we print, is five pounds and it comes out of the print fund, and there are four pounds and change in the tin, so if you pay the five pounds you are paying for the printing of a thing that other people will read in three months. And a lineage that concerns a public-safety technique can be requested free on a safety ground, and that request goes in the book in full, with your name and the reason, and if the request is upheld you are named in the quarterly as the person who asked."

"And you can refuse my request."

"We can refuse your request, and you can take it to the Saturday meeting, where there are nine of us and a vote, and where Marda will be extremely difficult."

Marda put both hands flat on the table.

"Now the part where I am unkind," she said. "And then I am going to be extremely difficult and I am doing it in a straight chair, not over a kettle, because you have not got a kettle."

She turned and looked at him properly for the first time.

"You said *chosen*," she said. "You said *whether anybody chose to leave the fourth beat out of it*, and you said you have four ordinary reasons you cannot tell apart, and one of them is a person who chose it. Now: I have read your file. Not your case — your *request*, which is four lines long. And I am going to tell you what you have, and then I am going to tell you what you do not have, and then I am going to be wrong at you in front of nine people, and I am going to be wrong about the word, and I am going to be right about the shape, and you are going to be able to tell the difference by Friday and I do not think you are going to like it."

"Go on."

"You have a printed card with a boundary label and nothing performed underneath it. That is documented and it is a fact and you could prove it by reading two documents aloud to anybody, and if you cannot do that in a room with the equipment off then the problem is not the card." She held up a finger. "And you have eleven pages of a script that calls the fourth beat a support beat and not a step in the technique, and that says the learner is not to proceed on a self-initiated release. That is a document. That is two documents, in fact, and one of them has a version number on it that is a version *older* than the thing you are holding, which means somebody's department knows."

"Two documents. Right."

"So you have a shape," said Marda Szabo. "You have the shape of a completion that was removed and replaced with a voice, and the shape is real, and the shape is what this co-operative exists to look at, and I have looked at it for about ninety seconds and I agree with you about the shape completely."

"Then—"

"And *removed*," she said, "is a word with a person inside it."

The table got quiet in the way a table does.

"Removed," Marda Szabo said, "means somebody did it. Removed means a before and an after and a hand in the middle. You have written *removed* and you have written *chose*, and I want you to hear them, because you have not been choosing between four reasons. You have been doing something much cleverer and much worse, and it is that you have found the worst one and you have been carrying it round for five weeks in case somebody hands you a reason to put it down."

Marek felt the back of the chair against his knees and did not move.

"That is not fair," he said.

"It is completely fair," said Marda Szabo, "and here is the fourth of the four, and I have not said them yet, because I want you to know that I have got them and that I am choosing the same one you are. A print run goes wrong. A card gets printed against the wrong revision. A supplier assembles a bundle out of somebody else's book and loses a page. Or a person sits down and decides. That is your list, that is my list, and they are the same list, because they are the only four there are, and I have had a file in front of me in this room with three of them in it — a misprint in a district nursing card, a book assembled from a hospital handout with the second page missing, and a hospital handout that was never anybody's to begin with — and none of those three things took nine weeks and a boy with two tendons, and I have still got them in the book, because one of these days somebody is going to come in here with the fourth."

"Then the request is refused."

"The request is refused," she said. "Not because you are wrong about the shape. Because you cannot answer one question, and a lineage is a set of answers, and if I put your four sentences in a printed quarterly and one of them turns out to be the second reason, then this co-operative has told two hundred and twelve people that somebody cut a beat out of a rescue brace on purpose, on the word of a third-year who could not tell me the name of the person who printed it."

Marek Senn sat in a chair on a trestle table in a room that had been a ferry waiting room for sixty years and was aware, in a way that arrived in his chest rather than his head, that he had wanted very much for the last four minutes for the woman in the cardigan to be wrong.

Not for the file. For him. He wanted to be a man who had been told no about a shape and had not been told no about the rest, and there is not one person in this city who has ever printed that on a label.

"Right," he said.

Ekow Nartey turned the book round, uncapped a pen, and held it above the page.

"Corrections column," he said. "Shall I read it back."

"Read it back."

He read it back, in a small flat voice, to nine people, in a market.

*"M. Senn, third year, no licence, asked after a published lineage for the practicum rescue brace 4-19. States the fourth beat was removed. Correction: Senn holds a card with a boundary label and no performed boundary-release beneath it, and a script of eleven pages which names the instructor's spoken cue as the completion of that beat. He has not established who, if anybody, chose the removal. The distinction between a shape and a hand is not a nuance and this co-operative does not print a hand it cannot name. Query stands open. Recorded at the Exchange, week ten, Monday, in the corrections column, initialled E. N."*

"Nine hundred addresses," said Ekow, capping the pen. "Next quarter."

---

Marek walked to the canal end and sat on a wall for a while, which was where he went when he could not go anywhere else, and it was about a quarter past five on the Monday of week 10 and the light on the water was doing a thing he had never once been able to describe.

He had been told, in front of nine people, by a woman he would never see again, that he had the shape right and the word wrong, and that the word was the only dangerous part.

He wrote the evening up in the load book in the room above the laundromat at half past nine, and Rafi Pell was on the floor with the courier bag over his legs doing a sum that had been wrong and had been corrected twice, and Marek sat down opposite him and did not ask him for anything, which took him four separate attempts and two false starts and cost him more than anything that happened on the Monday.

Rafi did the two-count out loud anyway, at the top, before he said the thing he was going to say, and he did it slowly, and he was not going to be embarrassed about it later.

"You've been out," he said. "You've come in with a face on like somebody's bin. I'm not asking. I'm just noting it in case you want to throw it at me and I'm not there."

"Noted," said Marek. "Thank you."

"Don't thank me, that's the whole—" He stopped. "Two. There. Right. Yeah, don't thank me, that's the thing I meant." He put the pen down. "What was it?"

Marek thought about it.

"I got told I'm wrong," he said. "In front of nine people. By a woman in a cardigan. And she's right, and I knew she was right for about eleven seconds before she said it, and I still do not know which half of it she was going to say, and that is the part I have not written down. And I want you to know that I went in there this afternoon wanting her to be wrong, and that I would have paid for it."

"Course you would."

"Not in money. In — " He stopped and did it properly, because he had a rule now and it was the only one he had. "I wanted it more than I wanted the thing to be true. That's the sentence."

"Huh," said Rafi Pell. "Well. That's the first one of those you've said where you didn't sound pleased with yourself about it afterwards." He picked the pen up again. "Mine's the wall, you know. Still there. Two years. Not going anywhere in week ten."

"I know."

"Ruf."

"Ruf."

"I'm not doing the thing where I'm a good mate about it," he said. "I just wanted to say the wall's still there so that you don't get round to thinking that because you said a hard thing out loud to a stranger everything in this flat's been sorted."

It had not been sorted. Marek wrote that down, in the load book, under a heading he had not used in nine weeks and had to invent on the spot, which was *What I Said Today And What I Got*, and the only line on it was one line, and he left it there.

*Week 10, Monday. I was told I was wrong in front of nine people about the only word I have got, and she was right, and I want to remember what the wanting felt like, because on Friday of week eleven I am going to be sitting in a room with four people on a panel and I am going to want it again, and it will feel exactly like this and it will not feel like anything else.*

*Do not know: whether a co-operative of two hundred and twelve people who meet on market days is going to be the only place in this city that says the word refusals. Do not know whether that is a fact about them or a fact about me.*

*Do not know: what the fourth beat was called before somebody printed a card with the word on it and nothing under it. Nobody in this building has said yet, and I have been in this city nine weeks and I have not asked one single person what the fourth beat is called in the trade.*

*Not known, not written down, not to be used: anything I was told today by anybody whose name is on a form, a bench, a truck or a counter. I am not doing it again. I am not building a thing out of the people who answer me first.*
