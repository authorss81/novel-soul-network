# Chapter 28 — Compatible

The term practice-variance panel of the Nacre Institute of Applied Practice sat at eleven o'clock on the Friday morning of week 11 in a seminar room on the second floor with the blinds down, and it was not a board, and Marek Senn had been in the building since eight.

There were four members. The chair was a woman of about sixty called Professor Onyeka whose field was assessment and who had, in Marek's four weeks of reading the file, no interest in him at all. The second was a man of about thirty-eight from the trust. The third was a woman of about fifty from the district's practice standards office. The fourth was Davan Rusk, who had not put himself there and could not get off it, and who came in at ten to eleven and sat down and put his hands flat on the table in a way that Marek had seen him do at the front of room two for a term.

Darla Vint sat at the back with a folder. She did not speak for fifty-four minutes, which was longer than the fifty she had done in the conference room, and Marek had written the number down in week 9 and it had gone up again.

Deputy Registrar Anneke Roos presented the department's position in about six minutes and she was very good at it, and Marek Senn heard his own ten weeks described in the language of a woman building a defensible position, and none of it was a lie.

The department had received a documented variance from a trainee on the fifteenth of week 6. The department had acted on it in the ordinary way. Four sites had placed limited local holds on stock from a single assembly lot. Those holds were local, reversible, and placed on each site's own authority without indemnity. The department had raised a vendor enquiry through the ordinary channel, which was true and which would take six to nine months. The department had made a documented offer of conditional progression to the trainee and the offer had been declined with a reason recorded, and the reason was not about the department, and the department had not treated the trainee's reason as a grievance.

Nobody had mentioned a boundary-release beat. Nobody had mentioned an instructor's cue. Nobody had mentioned a card.

Marek had a four-page file in front of him that he had still not been allowed to read, and a photocopied overleaf in his coat, and a buff envelope in a bag in Lattice, and he sat in a room on the second floor of his own department and listened to the best six minutes of advocacy he had ever heard, about him, without him.

Then Professor Onyeka turned to him.

"Marek Senn. You may speak for five minutes and then I have four questions. I am going to ask you the fourth question first, because I am tired and because it is the only one that matters."

"Go on."

"The Continuity Office has recorded a view, and I am going to read it because I want you to hear it in a room and not on a photocopy." She turned a page. "*A term panel is able to act upon a demonstrated non-hinged boundary release, performed in a public room by a named practitioner, with witnesses, and with no substituted cue used, followed or accepted within the scored window; and no other matter within this office's knowledge is capable of being placed before that panel to the same effect.* Reference and date, the Friday of week nine." She put the page down. "That is the whole of what this panel can act on. Not the label. Not the booklet. Not a photograph of a plate and not a report and not a suspicion. Have you performed a non-hinged boundary release, in a public room, with witnesses, with no substituted cue used, followed or accepted?"

"No," said Marek Senn.

"Have you, at any point in this term, performed one?"

"No."

"Thank you." She wrote something. "Then there is no matter before this panel this morning, and I am going to say that out loud, because in about four years somebody is going to need to know that a panel said it in a room rather than in a letter, and because I do not believe in leaving a young man with the impression that silence is a strategy."

And then Davan Rusk spoke for the first time, and he spoke in a voice that Marek had not heard from him before.

"Chair. May I."

"You may."

"Have you read a design approval record for revision 4-19?"

Marek Senn looked at him.

"Yes."

"Where from."

"It is on a published lineage sheet at a co-operative on a market floor in Saltmarket, and it is in a file at the institute, and I have a copy of the sheet in my coat and I am not going to produce it unless you ask me to and I will produce it if you ask me to."

"Then I am going to say one thing and I am going to stop." Rusk did not look at him. "This department has held a stores file since November 2019 about a return contact that had set. It is on the third shelf of the second press. I opened it on Wednesday morning at half past seven, at my own desk, with nobody watching, and I am not giving it to you and I am not giving it to this panel. I initialled the disposition line in it in about nine minutes, in a corridor, in a hurry, on the same day it came in, and the disposition says *not a fault, no claim, prior practice continues*, and I wrote that, and I have told about four hundred students since then that a plate which sets under load is a wear fault, and it is not a wear fault, and I have never once said that out loud in a room, and I am saying it now, and I would like the minutes to record that I said it on my own initiative and not because anybody in this room asked me."

Professor Onyeka looked at him for a moment.

"Recorded," she said. "Mr Rusk, that is not a matter for this panel and you know it, and I am going to say something that is not unkind: you have told us about a file that nobody asked about, and it is going to sit in a set of minutes, and it is going to be read by somebody in about four years, and it is not going to do one single thing for you this morning."

"I understand, chair."

"I do not think you do, but that is not my problem and I have a panel." She turned to the second member. "The item is adjourned. There is no matter before us. The variance stays open at the department and it is not withdrawn, and the next sitting is at the end of term, and I am aware that that is a euphemism."

The room emptied in about eight minutes. Anneke Roos went past without touching him. Darla Vint touched his sleeve at the door and said nothing at all, which was a thing she had never done before and which he understood to have cost her something.

---

Oren Vey was standing outside the department office at twenty to three in the afternoon of the Friday of week 11 in a very good coat, and he had not been at the panel, and he was not in the building on any business that the building had invited him to.

"You've come out of a room where nothing happened," he said. "I can tell from about nine seconds of the way you're walking, and I want to say the obvious thing first, which is that I am sorry, and I am not going to pretend I know what it was about, because I know what it was about and being coy about it would be insulting to both of us."

"It adjourned."

"Right." Oren Vey took out a card and put it face down on the ledge by the door. "There's a place on the corner by the Exchange with a table you can hear yourself in. I have got forty minutes and then a train, and I told you Monday, and Monday is a nicer day for you, but I am not going to sit on a card for two days after a thing like that, because that would be a technique and I don't believe in my own."

"Technique."

"A thing you do to people," said Oren Vey, "and I do it for a living, and if I let you go away for two days and think about it alone you will do what you have done for ten weeks, which is talk to a man in a corridor at eleven at night until he gives you what you wanted, and I have watched four of you."

---

The place on the corner had a carvery on one side and a coffee machine that had given up, and Oren Vey ordered a thing and a coffee and moved the sugar so that Marek Senn had both of them in reach, and did it without looking at him, which is the harder way to do it.

"Right," he said. "I am going to be straight with you in an order, and I have written the order down, which is a thing I do."

"That's a good start."

"One. I have not come to give you a sandwich." He put his hands flat on the table. "Two. I stopped the audit programme on the four sites on Thursday afternoon, without being asked, and I have written that down and I am telling you now so that you cannot say in six months that nobody in my company thought about it. There were people in those four buildings getting telephone calls about serials and lists and it is not acceptable and it is not a fault and it is a warranty function doing its job badly in a situation it did not make. It stops today. I do not need you to thank me for it and if you thank me I will think you are going to pay for something later."

"Nobody has thanked you."

"Fine." Oren Vey took a drink of his coffee. "Three, and this is the one that matters, and I am going to be aware for about the next twelve minutes that you have read something I have not, and I am not going to ask you for it, and I am not going to pretend I do not know there is a document, and I am not going to help you with it."

"Go on."

"Four." He turned the cup round. "You are going to do this job. I am not asking you a question about it, I am telling you that as a matter of professional judgement, having read your file and watched you work in a corridor for two minutes, it is the correct outcome and I would say so if I had no company at all."

"That's a strong thing to say to a man with a transcript line."

"Yes, it is, and here is why it is not arrogance." Oren Vey leaned forward about four inches. "In the nineteen years I have been in this trade there have been four people who did what you have done. Three of them ended up in a room with a form and no technique, because being right is a service nobody buys, and one of them — and I want you to hear this part, because it is the only part of this lunch I care about — one of them ended up building a practice library on the side, out of the spare hours, for free, for about eleven years, and she was very good at it, and she died in a corridor in 2019 waiting for a train, and I have thought about her roughly once a fortnight for six years."

"That is not an argument, that is a story."

"It is a story *about* an argument, and the argument is: this city has no way to pay for what you do, and if you spend four years refusing every way of getting paid for it, you will be doing it for free until you cannot, and the thing you built will go when you cannot, and the people who were depending on it will be depending on a man instead, because that is what a man is for, in a city like this, and it is a terrible way to run a civic system and I know that better than almost anybody alive."

Marek Senn put his hands on the table and left them there.

"What's the job."

"District service. Salary thirty-four, rising at the end of the first year, four weeks' holiday, a card in your name, and a reference that any workshop in Nacre will honour inside two years, because Threadline's card is the one that does not lie." He said all of it in about nine seconds, and he had clearly said it to himself before. "You would service, you would train, and after about a year you would be the person who sits with a new third-year in a room in week six, before anybody has told them anything, and you would tell them what a boundary is, because you have not got anybody else who can say that sentence and mean it."

"And the card says—"

"The card says what it says. It does not say *assisted*. It says *may practise under supervision*, and *supervised* in this trade means a person in the room, and that is the job, and I am telling you that there is no version of this company that asks you to perform a release on your own, and there never has been, and I will put that in writing this afternoon if you want it in writing."

"You do not know that."

"I do know that." Oren Vey said it very evenly. "I have been the operations director of this company for two years and four months and I have never once asked a depot to tell a service engineer to lie, and if a depot did it I would find out, because I read the manifests, all of them, about nine hundred a year, and I have never in my life asked somebody what they think a document means."

"Then what do you think it means?"

"I think it means somebody very careful decided that the beat was dangerous, and I think they were frightened, and I think they were right to be frightened, and I think the whole industry has spent forty years responding to that fright by making the beat *look* present." He did not smile. "And I know there is a thing in a file about that, and I am not asking, and I am telling you that I have thought about that sentence for about a year and a half, at a kitchen table, in a house I have lived in for eleven years, and I have never once written it down in an office, because in an office it is a resignation, and at a kitchen table it is a problem."

---

"And now the part I have been building to," he said, "and I am going to keep my voice at this level, and I am not going to apologise for any of it, because if I apologise for it I will be doing it again in a fortnight and we will both know I did."

"Go on."

"Read a document about a scope applied early with the subject still in the load. Two times in six months. One of them a fourth-year assistant, one of them a person who was hurt." Oren Vey did not look at Marek's coat. "Now tell me what you want. You want that back on the fourth-year. On the nineteen-year-old. On the boy in the sling, who is a better person than all of us, and who you have already written in a book that a man could do a fourth beat for if the man were the wrong person."

"That isn't—"

"I know it isn't. I'm not arguing about your boy. I'm telling you what the *design* is, and there is a difference, and the difference is the entire content of this lunch." He put his hands flat. "Here is the problem with 4-19 and it is not the missing beat. It is the *quality* of the missing beat's replacement. The cue that was installed is a human voice, on a bad day, at forty feet, once a fortnight, sometimes by a tired man who is also running a room of thirty people. That is not a safe release route. That is an *unreliable* release route, and a thing you are told to trust at the moment you are least able to judge is not safety, it is a coin."

"And your answer is a better voice."

"My answer is a *reliable* one," said Oren Vey. "That is the whole of it and I am not going to dress it up. Not a man. A route. A completed, documented, taught, certified, insured release route that is there every time, with a professional standing behind it, and a learner who knows before they start that the word will come, and will not have to decide anything at ninety kilos."

"That is a key-holder."

"Yes." He did not flinch at it. "Yes, it is, and I know the word, and I have read the same file you have, and I know what it is when a key-holder's cue becomes the only exit anybody can find. And here is the part where I am going to be honest with you instead of clever, so listen. A key-holder is only a problem when the key-holder is *arbitrary*. When it is a man in a room, forty feet away, twice a week, in a bad mood — that is arbitrary, and it is coercive, and it makes obedient people out of frightened ones, and everything your file says about that is correct and I have not got a single argument against any of it. But if the key is a *procedure*, and the procedure is taught, and the procedure is the same every time, and there is a person behind it whose job it is and who is paid and trained and can be complained to, then it stops being a person you obey and it starts being a rule you can rely on, and that is the difference the whole argument in this case has been missing, because everybody on every side of it is talking about *who* is in the room and nobody is talking about *whether the room is the same*."

Marek Senn did not say anything at all.

Because in his coat, folded in four, on a photocopy, in a co-operative's plain language, in a member's own hand, quoted, there were three paragraphs that said the same three sentences in the same order, and the middle one had the same two incidents and the same fourth-year assistant in it, and the third one ended: *Design review accepts the loss of the practitioner beat as within tolerance for a supervised course.*

And a man in a cardigan across a table had just said it out loud, better, and with a solution, and was right.

"The middle paragraph," Marek said.

"Say it again."

"You have just said the middle paragraph of a document I have read, word for word in the substance, twice the length, and you have not read it, and you have not asked to, and I am not going to give it to you, and you have just told me that a function, with a reference number, decided before this term began that the fourth beat should be taken out of a hundred and eleven young people, and you agree with him, and you have improved on him, and I want you to understand that I have not said a word for about ninety seconds and it is because I have not got anything to say."

Oren Vey sat back.

"Right," he said. "Well. That is the best conversation I have had about a document in nine years, and I am not going to insult you by pretending I did not know there was one."

"You knew."

"I knew there was a design question. I have a file summary of a document, four sentences of it, in a system I am not supposed to be reading. I did not know there was a record, I did not know there was a member of a co-operative who had a copy, and I have never seen a page of it, and if I had seen a page of it I would not be having lunch in a place with a carvery, I would be having it somewhere with a lock on the door." He refilled his own coffee. "What I want from you is not the document. What I want from you is a month."

---

He laid the job out again in a slightly different order, and then he did something that Marek Senn did not expect and that turned the whole hour over.

"One more thing, and it is the only thing I have actually come for, and then I have got a train."

"Go on."

"Take the job for a month. Not as a favour to me — as a *trial*, with a card in your name and a wage at the end of it, and at the end of the month you hand it in if you want to and nobody will chase you and I will not make it difficult, because I have had men hand things in before and it is the correct thing to do and I have never once made it difficult." He put the sugar back. "And in that month I want to put a working completion route in front of people who do the work. Not your department. Not a class. A kitchen, a market, a ramp, a corridor, wherever you like. I want to watch it work, and I want to watch it work on a man with a bad shoulder at a fish market and a man with two tendons who cannot close his hand, and I want to do that in public with people who do not have to be polite about it."

"Why?"

"Because if it works, then the honest version of this whole thing ends where it should — the practitioner keeps the beat and the route exists underneath them for when they cannot do it, and nobody in this city has to be frightened in a room again, and the whole of your case becomes *we found something better* rather than *they were wrong*." Oren Vey looked at him steadily. "And if it does not work, then I have spent a month and a wage finding that out in public instead of quietly, and I would rather know in public, because a thing that does not work in a kitchen in front of a cook does not work anywhere, and I have been in this trade for nineteen years and I have never had that argument settled in the open."

"That is not a favour to me."

"It is not a favour to you. It is a favour to me, and it is a favour to nineteen-year-olds, and you are the only person in this city who is in a position to say the word no in a room where it counts, and I would much rather be refused by you in a kitchen than not turned down at all."

He looked at his watch.

"Which brings me to the last thing, and it is a question, and I would like an actual answer rather than a good one." Oren Vey put his cup down. "The thing you have got in that coat. Say it is the whole of the case, say the department's position is wrong, say the transcript line is a lie, say all of it. What do you do with it?"

Marek Senn looked at a man of forty-one in a café on a corner in Saltmarket.

"I have spent three days trying to give it away," he said. "A department has filed it. A Continuity Office has told me it cannot act on it. A co-operative that holds it cannot print it without putting two of its members in a witness box. And my own repairer put it on a nail on the wall and asked me not to bring it back in."

"That's a good answer."

"It isn't an answer. It's the absence of one. I spent three days finding out there was nobody to give it to, and the reason there was nobody to give it to is that a design is not a practice variance, and the only forum I have is a practice variance, and I have got a photocopy of a document that says the card was never meant to go into houses and there is not a single body in this city that can do anything about a house."

"Right." Oren Vey put money on the table — too much, deliberately, in a way that was generous rather than careless, and Marek noticed it and did not say thank you. "Then here is what I would like, and it is a question, and I am going to be slightly rude about how I ask it, because you will hear the nice version and agree to it and I don't want the nice version."

"Go on."

"You are right that there is nobody to act on a design," he said. "That has been true for a hundred and forty pages, four times a year, to nine hundred addresses, since this city got a co-operative, and it is the most important unsolved thing I have ever heard of and I am not being ironic, because the co-operative is eleven people on a market floor and it is the only institution in this country that says the word *refusals*. And you have just found the one thing in this city that could change what a design record is allowed to be, and you have spent three days working out that there is nobody to hand it to."

"Yes."

"So don't hand it to anybody." Oren Vey leaned forward. "Put it in a room. Not a department, not an office, not a ministry — a room. One room, with trestle tables, that anybody can walk into off the street, that meets once a month, where you can stand up and say *this is what is in a card in a hundred and eleven children* to a room of people who sell fish and teach ropes and drive vans, and where nobody can adjourn it, and where the only thing that gets you the floor is that you are right."

"That room exists. There's a co-operative on a market floor and a noticeboard a month out of date."

"Exactly," said Oren Vey. "And I have not been in it. And I don't want to buy it. And I will not buy it, and if somebody tried to buy it with money I would hand in my notice, and I am telling you that in a café as a piece of information rather than as a promise, because promises like that are worth nothing and a man who says it before he is tested has not said it."

He stood up and put his coat on.

"Monday of week twelve. Ten o'clock. The back room of the Exchange, which is where a lot of things happen that are not on anybody's rota. Bring whoever turns up. I don't need a room full of people. I need a room with five people in it who do the work." He got to the door and turned round. "And Senn — the boy's consent. If I put a route in front of him and it needs his hand, I need his consent, in writing, from him, on the day, and I do not want you in the room when it is asked, because you will fill the room. So when he is asked, you will be outside. Can you do that?"

"Yes," said Marek Senn.

"Good." Oren Vey was in the doorway. "Then Monday. And Senn — bring the four sentences if you want to. I have read the summary and I have not got anything clever to say about it, and I would rather argue about it across a table than in a corridor, and I think that is the whole of what I have."

He went. And Marek Senn sat on his own in a café on a corner in Saltmarket with a card in his pocket that he had not refused and had not accepted, and the wrist unit from the Fenns' hallway in a tea chest in the parts cage at the Spine with a note on it with his name on it, and his sister had not spoken to him for six days.

---

He wrote it up at one o'clock in the morning on the Friday of week 11 at the table in the room above the laundromat, and it is a short page and it is the only page in this book that is written almost entirely about another person, and he has said so himself in the margin.

*Week 11, Friday, 01:20.*

*Thirty-four, rising, a card in my name, a reference, four weeks' holiday, a room with a heater in it, and a person who does the work put in front of nineteen-year-olds in week six, and a man who will put me outside the room when a boy of twenty is asked, because he will fill it.*

*I have been ready to refuse that since the Wednesday of week six and I have had a form ready in my head for ten weeks, and I did not refuse it, and I want to write down why, in the exact words, because I think I have got it.*

*Because I have spent ten weeks being the man who says no, and it turns out that being the man who says no is a job, and I have been doing it at a wage of nothing and out of the last piece of food in my own chest, and about six people have been injured on my watch in the doing of it, and the person sitting across the table from me at one o'clock this afternoon knew that, from a file, and did not use it, and offered me a wage.*

*And because of the overleaf, and this is the one that will not leave me alone:*
*He said the middle paragraph. He said it word for word in the substance, in order, and I have it in my coat, and he has never read it, and he said it better, and he improved on it. The record said: not intended for unsupervised practice, acceptable for a supervised course, for one term of one department, in one city. He said: a permanent procedure, taught, insured, staffed, available to everybody, for ever, in every kitchen and every market and every ramp in the country.*
*That is not the same decision. It is a bigger one. It is a better one. It is the right answer to a question I have been asking the wrong way for ten weeks, and it was arrived at independently, by a man, in a café, with no idea that a document existed.*
*And if the bigger one is the right one then the case I have spent a term on is not "somebody took the fourth beat out." The case is "somebody took it out because the alternative was frightening, and then we all spent ten weeks arguing about the man who put it back instead of about the fright."*

*Three things I have to write down before I sleep, and I am not going to be proud of any of them:*
*1. He knows. He was in the building at twenty to three on the Friday afternoon and he was not there for the panel, and the panel adjourned at about ten past twelve, and there is no reason on this earth for the operations director of a company to be standing outside the practical department office of an institute on the Friday of week eleven unless somebody in that company has read a file and decided this week is the week.*
*2. He stopped the audits. Unasked. In writing, he says. And that is the correct decision, and it is also the decision that means there is now no service contact at those four addresses, and therefore no routine reason for anybody from his company to be in those four buildings, and therefore nobody in this city can check anything he says he did.*
*3. I did not tell Nell. Nine days. She has said nothing to me since the Wednesday of week ten, in a hallway, and I have not asked her to and I am not going to, and I went into a café this afternoon and did not think about her once for fifty minutes, and that is a fact and I am writing it in the first person, and it is the worst line in this book and it is in the same week as the light going out of a plain sentence in a stairwell on a Tuesday.*

*The demonstration requirement is still hanging with nothing behind it. The panel adjourned. There is no matter. There is no date. There is no licence. On Monday a man is going to put a thing in a room that works, and four people who do the work are going to have to look at it, and it is going to work, and I am going to have to sit in a room and watch it work and then decide what to do about the best piece of paper I will ever have, which was made by somebody who did not know I was going to get it.*
