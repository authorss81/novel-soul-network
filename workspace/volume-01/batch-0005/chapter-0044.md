# Chapter 44 — Until One O'Clock

The practical building at ten past eight on the Tuesday morning of week fourteen had the particular hollowness of a place that is being used by everybody in it and by none of it, and the corridors smelled of floor polish and hot dust off a strip light that nobody had switched off in June.

Room one is on the first floor at the end of a short corridor, past the sports hall and a fire door that does not latch, and the door of room one itself was locked, and Darla Vint had a ring of caretaker keys on a loop and the brass tag on the one that opened it said 1.

"You're not on the rota for this morning," she said.

"I know."

"You are not on the rota, you are not on the department's list of people attending, and the caretaker's letter for this room says *out of hours, department use*. That is a piece of paper with a word on it and no name on it, and if anybody in that building asks me why a room is open at eight o'clock on a Tuesday in the summer I am going to hand them that letter and it will be true and it will not be any use to anybody." She had the key half in and was not turning it. "I have been an administrative officer in this building for nine years and I have watched four different people open a room on a morning they should not have opened it, and two of them did it because they were right about something and two of them did it because they wanted to be seen, and the two who were right are the two I stopped asking about. So before I turn it: what is the room being opened for, in one sentence, and I am asking for the record and not for you."

"The department's letter of Thursday says the Office's assessor may determine this morning whether this room is a public room, and the department has said in writing that it does not find that it is or is not, and has said the department will comply with the determination."

"That is a sentence," said Darla Vint, and turned the key, and the door went in about two inches and stopped on a chair, and she swore under her breath and put her shoulder into it.

"If the determination is that it is not, the department will comply with that too," Marek Senn said, "and I would like it noted that I have said that in a corridor at eight in the morning to a person who has nine years of doors in her."

"Noted. And I will say what I said in week six and I will say it in the same words: if the door is off the lock then anything that walks in walks in, and if it goes in a list it is in a list for ever, and I have been asked to keep a list for this morning and I have not been asked what is on it, and that is a decision somebody is going to have to make at about a quarter to nine and it is not going to be me."

The chair under the door was a stacking chair with a nursery sticker on the leg and Marek Senn moved it and did not look at what was behind the door, because he had been told in a corridor on the Thursday of week thirteen by a man who was not allowed to be in the room not to walk in there and look at it and decide it was fine, and he had decided, standing in this corridor at ten past eight in the morning, that the way to do that was not to go in first at all.

He came in at five to nine, behind the assessor, and it was the first time he had been in the room since the Thursday of week one.

---

It is a teaching room, about forty feet by twenty-five, with a strip light on a chain, a whiteboard with a tray under it, a strip curtain across a doorway at the far end that goes to a store, and about thirty stacking plastic chairs in two rows at the back that are not bolted down and do not belong to this room. There is a folded table at the front. On the long wall there is the demonstrator panel: a plywood board on a steel frame, nine feet by four, with a laminated card clipped to the top of it, a bell on a bracket, a fold-down practice frame at knee height with a canvas bag of practice mass in it, a perspex cover over a rack of eight wall plates standing on their edges, and a strip light above the whole thing that is on a separate switch and is the only light in the room anybody ever looks at.

The whiteboard had the term dates on it in two different hands, and one of them was circled.

Marek Senn looked at the circled one for about a second and a half and then stopped looking at it, and the reason he stopped was that the term ended on the Friday of week twelve and somebody in this building had been in here and circled the end of a term that was over, and that was a person who was going to be in a room on a Tuesday in September with forty-one faces in it, and there was no version of him that helped.

"You did not look at it," said Hollis Pratt.

He was fifty-one, in a coat over a shirt, with a hardback file and no coat over a chair this time, because he was not in the room for the two hours. He had a district assessmenter's number on a laminated badge and he had a piece of A4 in his hand and he was writing on it as he walked, which was a habit of nineteen years and not a performance.

"You've had a look at it," said Marek Senn. "You were in the room at the end of week one."

"I was in the room at the end of week one, and I have been in about forty teaching rooms in this building since March, and I noticed the strip light and the strip curtain and I did not notice the chairs until just now." He put the cap on the pen. "Sit down. No — stand where you like. Here's the shape of it. My office wrote you a sentence in the ninth week. It is the only sentence anybody in this city can put in front of a panel and it is the only reason your deputy registrar set a date on the last day she was allowed to set one. The sentence says *in a public room*. It does not define a public room. It has never defined one, because my office has never in nineteen years had to put a panel in a room and define the word at the same time, and I have checked, and there is nothing in the district's guidance, and I am not going to be a man who finds something in guidance that is not there."

"Will you rule?"

"I am the assessor appointed by this office to the assessment of Tuesday of week fourteen and I will rule, and the department will comply, and I want it understood that the department and not this office has chosen the room and has chosen to choose it on a day when there is one room in the building." He turned the A4 round. "I have made a list of what I have to decide and I am going to read it out, because a thing that is decided in four minutes in a room is a thing nobody can appeal, and there is no appeal and that is worse."

There were three people at the table. Professor Onyeka, who was sixty-one and had chaired a term practice-variance panel three times a term since before Marek Senn came to the building, and who had a hand-written agenda of four lines in front of her. The district woman of about fifty, whose office was on the Ashfields side and who had attended in week eleven and had said nothing in the second sitting. And a man of thirty-eight from the trust, who had been on that panel since its second sitting and who had a laptop he had not opened. Rusk's chair was the fourth one and it was pushed in, and Professor Onyeka said, without being asked, that she had no power to fill a seat in eleven days and had tried, and that the panel was sitting with three members, and that the department had not objected because the department could not.

"And the thing I have to decide," said Hollis Pratt, "is whether room one of this building is a public room. Not a room in public. Not a room with people in it. A *public* room, which is a room a member of the public is entitled to be in. And I am going to be honest with you about how difficult that is, because you are the candidate and you are entitled to know whether the man deciding is a man who has been handed the answer. It is the most difficult determination in this building and it takes about a quarter of an hour and I have not had a night over it, because I did not get told about this room until the Thursday afternoon when a letter went out with a number on it, which means I have had four days and four days is enough for a man who works in a room, and it is not enough for a man who works in a street. So I am going to do it here, in front of you, and you are going to be able to hear me think, and if you think I am getting it wrong I am going to stop and hear it, and that is the procedure and it is not a favour."

The room was completely silent except for a strip light.

"A public room is not a nice room," said Hollis Pratt. "It is not a room with an audience in it, it is not a room where something is being taught, and it is certainly not a room with a lot of chairs in it. It is a room a member of the public is entitled to be in, and every room in this building is that for about ten hours a day, and the department has let one out of term for a course and one out of term for a porter's office and this one out of term for a caretaker's letter with a word on it and no name on it.

"I am not going to find that a strip light and a whiteboard and a nursery's chairs make a room public. Any assessor who found that would be handing the district a sentence that says *a room is public if you put a light on in it*, and in about four years somebody would put a light on in an empty room in an empty building and a company would say the demonstration had been held." He turned a page over. "I am going to determine this the only way I can, which is on the state of the room, and the state of the room is a door and a key. Room one is locked at nine in the morning by a caretaker's key and it is locked at four in the afternoon by the same key by the same man, and between those two points it is not a public room and it is not anybody's room, and neither of those things is what the sentence in the ninth week requires.

"So. Three determinations, and I am making all three out loud and in order and the third one is the one that matters and I am going to be asked about it in six months by somebody who is annoyed.

"One. From nine o'clock this morning until this panel rises, the door of room one is unlocked and stays unlocked. That is the whole of the public part of it. The room does not become a schoolroom. It stops being locked.

"Two. From nine o'clock this morning until this panel rises, the corridor outside this door is not rostered. No member of staff is stationed in it, and no member of staff is permitted to ask anybody in this corridor what they are here for, and if you want the second half of that in writing, say so now and I will write it before I make the rest of it.

"Three. Anybody who comes through that door during the sitting stays. Their name does not go in a list, they are not asked to sign anything, and this office will not make, keep or ask for a record of who was in the room. And I want to say — and this is the part I would like the department's minute to carry, and Professor Onyeka, I am asking you to write that I asked — I want it said that the effect of the third determination is that this room is a public room for about two hours because the department let it be locked at other times, and that this office has created a public room in a teaching establishment for the length of a morning in order to hear a demonstration, and that is a thing nobody in the district should ever have to do twice."

He put the cap on the pen.

"That is the determination. Room one is a public room from nine o'clock this morning until this panel rises, and not at any other time on any other day, and I have made it in the room in front of the candidate and four witnesses and you heard all of it, and it does not lower the sentence of the ninth week and it does not add to it, and I am not a panel and I am not a board and I have no vote on anything after nine o'clock."

He looked at Marek Senn.

"Have I got it wrong."

"No, sir."

"Say why, quickly, because I have been asked once already today and the answer was by a man in overalls and it was better than yours."

"Because you decided it on the door and not on the room," said Marek Senn, "and if you had decided it on the room there is not one answer, because the room has thirty chairs in it that are not the department's and a whiteboard with a term on it that has ended, and you would have had to say which of those two things makes it a public room, and the honest answer would have been neither."

Hollis Pratt wrote something for about four seconds, and it was not what Marek Senn had said.

---

At about twenty past eight the witnesses came, in ones and twos, and every one of them had worked out a way to get there that cost them something and not one of them had been told the address.

Rafi Pell came at eight in a clean jacket with his courier bag, having left the ring road at ten past six on a Tuesday with eleven pounds in his pocket instead of in the depot's, and he stood in the corridor for about four minutes not going in, counting, visibly, twice, before he opened the door, and Marek Senn watched him do it and said nothing to anybody about it either then or afterwards.

Dessa Kwan came at ten past eight in her own coat, and the Saltmarket Exchange had been shut since six that morning with the shutters down and two men on the pass with nowhere to be, and she had said in the doorway of the repair shop on the Sunday that she did not want to know the date and had then asked what the address was.

Reuben Daw came at a quarter past eight in his work clothes, having left a hand truck standing across two doors in a yard in Saltmarket for a day and a half, and being twenty-two, and having told nobody why.

Tam Arrick came at twenty to nine, on a bus, alone, with his hand in the pocket of a jacket, and he had told Marek Senn on the Saturday that he was not going to be thanked, and Marek Senn had not thanked him, and they had looked at each other for about a second and then not looked at each other for the rest of the morning.

The notice on the practical department's board went up on the Thursday asking for witnesses to a witnessed assessment, which is a thing a department does, and Asha Reed read it on the Thursday and sent two people to it, and did not telephone him, and Marek Senn worked that out at about half past eight on the Tuesday from the fact that Toby Ansell was in a corridor in a first-year's coat with a folder under his arm looking as though he had been in the building since seven.

"Nobody told me," he said to Toby Ansell.

"Nobody told anybody," said Toby Ansell. "There's a notice. It says witnesses. It's a witness list and it's on a board and Asha put two names under it on Thursday night and she told me on Friday and I've been sitting on this stair since twenty to seven because I didn't know when the corridor opens." He shifted the folder. "She said, and I'm quoting her, *if he knows I'm sending you he'll talk to you, and if he doesn't know then it's four people who did not talk to him.*"

"Right," said Marek Senn, and had to go and stand somewhere else for a second.

Rhoda Ilesanmi was with him, nineteen, maintenance track, first year, and she had a hardback notebook and had been told by Toby Ansell to bring it, and she had a pen in it and nothing written on any page, and she had been in the building since twenty past seven because she had never seen a panel sit before and had wanted to see one before the thing she was here for.

---

Two things happened in the corridor before nine o'clock and Marek Senn was in the corridor for both of them.

Davan Rusk came up the stairs at five to nine with a teaching folder under his arm that he did not open, and he stopped six feet away and did not come closer, and he said one sentence.

"I am not permitted to say anything in there and I am not going to, and I am telling you that in a corridor before it starts rather than after, because I have found out this week that I am not a person who can be trusted not to."

Marek Senn did not say thank you and did not use it and did not ask him what he had found out.

"You'll be in the building," Rusk said.

"I know. You told me last Thursday."

"I have something to say to you about that and it is about a room and not about a practice, so I have to say it here and nowhere else, and it is this." He shifted the folder. "Do not walk in there and look at it and decide it is fine. Make them look at it."

"You said that on the Thursday."

"I did, and I have thought about it for two days and I have decided that the second half is the only half that is any use, and the first half is a thing a man says when he is frightened of being the man in the room." Davan Rusk put his hand flat on the fire door that does not latch. "Make them look at it. The assessor has just made a determination on a door, and he made it out loud, in the room, in four minutes, and that is the correct procedure and I have watched twenty-two years of this building do it the other way. So the question is not whether the room is public. The question is who has looked at it today, and I have been in it and I have not looked at it, and the man who is going to be examined in it is the man the room is about."

He went down the stairs to the ground floor where his office was.

And at nine o'clock, when Darla Vint turned the key and left it in the lock and put the ring of keys on the fire door shelf where anybody could take them, Professor Onyeka said, in the middle of the room, to nobody in particular, "The chair of this panel notes that the department's administrative officer has been asked to keep a list of those present, and that no such list has been produced, and that no such list is to be produced, and that I will minute that instruction came from the Office and not from this panel." And Darla Vint said, "There is no list, Professor, and there was never going to be one, and I did not want one." And the district woman of fifty said, to nobody, "Then that is the third thing said in this building about consent in thirteen weeks and none of them was in a corridor."

---

Sera Quill came in at nine o'clock with a tool bag and overalls and a job card, and she came in the way she came into everything, which was to a room she had a reason to be in.

"That's the Spine's," she said, to the panel, before anybody asked. "That wall's on the service round. Three college buildings, one clinic, the depot. Last change, the Thursday before the term started. Job card's on the board, it's got a date and my name on it and a number, and if any of you want the number it's 7-4-112 and it's a service round number and not a fault number, because there's no fault in it yet."

"Who's the assessor," she said to Hollis Pratt, "and what's he assessing, because if it's my wall I need to know what it is before I'm standing in it, and I'll be standing in it, and I'm not going to be asked."

Hollis Pratt told her, in about nine words, exactly what he was assessing.

"Right," said Sera Quill, and put the tool bag down, and rolled her sleeves, and said to Marek Senn, without any particular emphasis, "Right. Two rules about that board and then I'll leave you alone. One, the frame comes down before anybody puts a hand near it, and the bag comes out, and it stays out until the panel says otherwise in writing. Two, if a plate in that rack makes a noise, everybody on this side of the room steps back and nobody touches anything, and I'll do it, and it will take me about four seconds, and you will not have to ask me."

"Understood."

"I haven't been asked," said Sera Quill. "Nobody has asked me for anything and I'm here for a wall. I know what that's worth and I'm not going to be thanked for it and it isn't worth anything." She picked the tool bag up again. "Right. Where do you want me."

---

At two minutes to nine Professor Onyeka read out the requirement, because the requirement is the whole of the proceeding and it has to be said in a room and not paraphrased.

"Read by this office in the ninth week and not altered by this department: a working non-hinged boundary release, performed by the person performing it, in a public room, with witnesses, and with no substituted cue used, followed or accepted within the scored window." She looked up. "Nobody may add to that sentence and nobody may take from it, and I have had a telephone call from the department's own people about the window, and the answer is no, and if anybody else telephones me about it the answer will be no in the same words."

She said then, without stopping, "The assessment is a working non-hinged boundary release by M. Senn. The department has also put before this panel a plan, submitted in four parts, in four hands, none of which is the candidate's, and I am going to read out its nine headings because the panel is entitled to know what it is being asked to witness, and then I am going to ask whether the department objects to any of it, and the department has already said that it does not, in writing, on Friday."

She read the nine headings out. The room heard item five, which said that all five of the people present might stop it by word, including the candidate, and that one stopping ended the whole thing and that the ending would be recorded as an ending.

And then the door went.

It was not a knock. It was the door, which was unlocked and had been unlocked for four minutes, and a woman of about fifty came in with a hand truck and a clipboard and did not stop at the back, and walked down the middle of the room between two rows of plastic chairs to the front, and said:

"Sorry — sorry — is this the room with the nursery chairs in it? Because they're not mine to leave and I've been told twice this week that somebody would take them out on Friday, and Friday's come and gone, and there are four little ones coming on Monday in this room and I have got nowhere to put them, and if I can just get them out of here before your — is this your —"

She stopped.

She was looking at the whiteboard, and at the thirty chairs, and at the strip curtain, and then at the panel, and then at the strip light above the plywood board with the fold-down frame and the perspex and the laminated card clipped to the top of it.

Hollis Pratt stood up.

"Mrs — "

"Ena Truslove," she said. "The Fold, two floors up. I run it. I have run it for eleven years and those chairs are mine and I signed for them in twenty-twenty-one when the council did away with the second room, and I am not saying that to anybody because it is not anybody's business, I am saying it because I've been in and out of this door for eleven years and I want my chairs."

There was a dead silence of about two seconds and then Hollis Pratt said, "You are not on a list, and I will come to why in a moment, and nobody is going to ask you what you are here for. The determination I made four minutes ago is that this room is a public room from nine o'clock until this panel rises, and the reason for it is a door and a key, and the door is unlocked, and you are standing in it. Would you like to know what that means in practice?"

"No," said Ena Truslove, "I would like to get my chairs."

"Then take your chairs," said Hollis Pratt, "and I would like you to stay for about ten minutes if you can, and I want to be honest with you that I do not know why I am asking you that, and I have not decided whether I am asking you that."

She put the hand truck down.

"Right," she said. "Well. Get the chairs off the wall first, because there's a bracket and if you go sideways they'll go through the whiteboard."

---

At nine minutes past nine the strip light above the plywood board went out.

It was not a flicker and it was not a bang. There was a sound, small and hard and dry, like a contactor that had been asked to do something it did not want to do, and then the light went out and stayed out, and the room lost the only thing in it anybody ever looked at, and in the half second before anybody moved there was a noise from behind the perspex on the demonstrator panel, a sliding sound, small, the sound of a thing about four inches by three that has fallen over onto its edge.

Two people on a service round heard it first, because it was their whole day.

Sera Quill said, "Everybody on this side of the room step back. Now. Away from the board." And she was already across the floor, and one of her crew went with her, and the other one put his hand flat out in the direction of the people nearest the board, palm down, the way you stop a dog, and did not touch anybody, and everybody on that side of the room went backwards, and nobody asked him to.

Marek Senn was eight feet from the board and he could not move, and it was not fear, it was that he was the candidate and a person walking backwards in a scored room with a panel in it is a person telling a panel what he is frightened of.

The perspex had lifted about a quarter of an inch on its left edge, and behind it, in a rack, eight wall plates stood on their edges, and one of them had slid forward and sat down crooked on its shelf, and the copper return contact on its face was folded.

Sera Quill got the perspex up on its catches and looked at the plate without touching it for about three seconds.

"Right," she said. "Everybody's back. That's a folded return and that is a plate that has taken a load and not been let off it, and that's not a fault I've got a number for, and it is not anybody's plate, and I am not going to touch it until somebody in this building tells me in writing that I can." She turned round. "There is a light on that board and it is not on now and I am not going to be the man who puts it back on with a folded contact in the rack, and so this room has lost its demonstration rig, and the panel may wish to note the time, which is twenty-one minutes past nine, and that there is no rig."

Professor Onyeka had a pen and looked at her watch.

"Twenty-one minutes past nine," she said. "Noted."

And then a man who had been standing at the front of the room, in the position at the front of a room that forty years of teaching puts a man in without asking him, said a word.
