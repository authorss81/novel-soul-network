# Chapter 50 — The Back of a Form

He got to the pull-off off Fell Road at about ten past one on the Thursday afternoon of week fourteen in a van he had not needed to bring, and parked it thirty feet short of the men, because Corvin Ashby had told him in a lane in the spring not to park in a gateway and he had remembered that for eight months, which was the most useful thing anybody had ever taught him for nothing.

Ashby had said there would be ten and there were nine, and nobody made anything of it.

Nkechi Osei was on the top tread of the Cutbank with a bar in her hands, going down into the dark and coming back up and going down, and the count above her was still four, and Marek Senn could hear it from thirty feet away, because by the Friday of week fourteen he could hear a count in his own chest.

He had come to say a thing. He had worked out what it was on the steps of the practical building about four seconds before he got into the van, which was his habit, and the thing was this: *I was the fourth thing in a room on Tuesday and a rail came across a girl, and a boy of twenty held a line he was not allowed to hold, and neither of those two things is going to be in your book, and I would like to put them in it, and I have come to ask whether I am allowed.*

He stood in a car park for about forty minutes and did not say it.

Corvin Ashby brought him out a mug of tea at twenty to two and did not ask him why he had come, and said two other things instead.

"You didn't come up here to ask me for anything," he said. "You came up here to be asked a question and there wasn't one, and you have been doing that since the Monday of week thirteen, and I watched you do it on my bank with a flask in your hand, and I have watched you do it to about nine people this term."

"That's not the same thing."

"It's the exact same thing and you know it, so don't do the face." Ashby drank his own tea standing up. "And the other thing, and then I'll leave you to it. You want to be in a book in a ward. Well, the last time anybody wrote down who does what on the Weir, the report said *informal*, and the pump money went in the spring, and there has not been any since. The next report anybody writes about this ward is the first one anybody reads about it in four years. If it says a college was on the round in the flood season, that is the second bad one, and that is what you would be buying with it. So no. Ask me that one in the spring, and in the spring I will know whether any of the four of them have said the word college in a doorway, and you and I both know that I will not be able to write it down if the answer is no."

"Ashby."

"Don't."

They stood in a car park and drank tea and a woman went down some steps four hundred times, and at about twenty past two Marek Senn put his mug on the roof of the van and said the only sentence he had come up the hill to say, and it was not it.

"Nkechi's on four."

"She's on four," said Corvin Ashby, "and she will be on four on every round till the year's out, and there are about nine of them left, and you did that by standing on a bank, and I have told you I do not want it written down anywhere by anybody including me, and I have now told you twice, and that is twice."

The light was gone by the time he got down the hill and he did not put the radio on.

On the Friday morning of week fourteen there were three pieces of paper on the bench at Civic Spine 7, and by the afternoon there was a fourth thing on the wall, and none of the four had anything to do with the other three.

The fourth thing was the job list, which the trust's second condition had put up on the Wednesday with a drawing pin and a printed sheet in a plastic sleeve. The sheet said, at the top, in the trust's typeface: *this list is kept by the holder. It is open to any member of the public. It includes work the holder does not do.* It was the first public document in this city that anybody had produced on purpose in order to be read by strangers, and it was four lines long, and one of the four lines had his initials at the bottom of it, and the initials meant not done, and everybody who had ever worked that bench knew what initials at the bottom of a list meant.

Darla Vint came in at about half past nine with the department's own record of Tuesday morning in a plastic wallet, and squared it with two fingers in a way so like Marek's own habit that he had to look away for a second.

"That is the department's record of a room the department owns," she said, "one side, four items, and the reference number is ours, so it will get held up and copied to the regional office, because anything with a technique in it gets copied."

She turned it round so that it was the right way up for him and did not read it out, and let him read it, which was the most tactful thing anybody had done for him in thirteen weeks.

> *1. A witnessed assessment was held in room one on the Tuesday of week fourteen at 09:00. Room one was determined to be a public room for the length of the sitting by the assessor appointed to this assessment by the Continuity Office.*
> *2. A fold in the copper return contact of a wall plate in the demonstrator panel caused a loss of panel lighting and a mechanical movement of the front rail of the frame. The isolator was made and locked off at ten seconds after the event. The plate was removed, sealed and bagged, and is held by the maintenance contractor under service number 7-4-112.*
> *3. An assessment under the requirement of the ninth week was made and completed. No licence is granted.*
> *4. The equipment failure is a matter for the district practice standards office and is not a matter of technique variance before this panel.*

"That is a true document," said Darla Vint, "and it is the only one of the three things on that bench that anybody will still be able to rely on in nine years, and I have been an administrative officer in this building for nine years and I want you to understand that I am not proud of it and that it is not a lie. That is the difficulty with a document. It does not have to be a lie to leave four things out."

"What is not in it."

"Her name is not in it," said Darla Vint. "Her arm is in the panel's minute, once, with a cross-reference, and her own words are in a district return, and the department's record of the department's own room does not contain the name of a first-year whose forearm was cut in that room, and nobody had to decide that. It came out that way. By the time a district office has it the arm is a *matter* and the room is a *room*, and there is not one sentence in the four items a person could be hurt by. And there is a fifth item on the department's copy and not on yours, and it is the chair's determination, made on the Friday, that the panel sat on this matter in a room which is not a teaching room, because a panel which has just declared a teaching room to have been a public room ought not to sit in it to decide what to do about it. I have never in nine years seen a chair minute the thing that would embarrass her own department, and I did not hear about it until this morning."

"Professor Onyeka."

"Professor Onyeka," said Darla Vint, and picked up the wallet, and stopped. "I have not been thanked for nine years and I am not going to start on a Friday morning, and if you say thank you I will put the wallet back in my bag."

"I was not going to."

"I know. That is the whole of it. Right." She went out to the van.

The second piece of paper came in the post on the Friday morning, addressed to Marek Senn by name at the shop, with a request slip from the Saltmarket Exchange clipped to the corner of the envelope and a smaller slip from the Choir of Practice in Ekow Nartey's handwriting: *proof, one sheet, goes to press tonight, do not photocopy, the point is the print run*.

It was one side of the inside spread of a quarterly, in four columns and no fifth — origin, revisions, boundaries, refusals — and the rule of the co-operative is that everything about a person, published by them, is published by name.

The origin column said 2016, Nacre Institute methods group, and that the group did not record who wrote it. The revisions column said 3-08, 3-11, 4-02, 4-19, and for 4-19 it printed the design approval record: TRC-419-88, raised the eleventh of August, approved the fifteenth, three paragraphs of stated reason, and no name on the record at all.

Underneath it, in the same column, there was a ruled box with a heading over it and a single line under the heading, and the heading was **UPSTREAM LICENSOR**, and the box was empty, and under the box, in the same typeface as the rest of the sheet, four lines:

*Ruled by M. Szabo, fifth week. No member has supplied it and the originating practice holds no record of it. It is left empty because we cannot invent it and we will not print a guess. Readers are asked to treat this box as a hole in this document and not as an omission by us.*

And a hole in the document was the thing Marek Senn had been living inside since the Thursday of week one, and it had now been ruled, in plain language, by a records clerk of fifty-one at a trestle table in a market, and printed, and it was going to nine hundred addresses that night.

The boundaries column carried the four lines of the instructor script, the Institute's own 2016 note that a class which cannot make the fourth beat has been taught wrongly and not taught safely, the fact that the department's manual is still 3-08 and has not been reissued, and the service bulletin number. And then four sentences in the plainest English on the page:

*On the Tuesday of week fourteen, in a room made public for the length of a morning by a determination of a government office made on a door and not on the room, four people did four things they already did, and no new practice was performed. One of the four performed a boundary release on his own hand, standing up, with nothing in it, and declined to have it named; the panel recorded it and did not adopt it, and this sheet records it because the co-operative prints what people publish about themselves. The panel also recorded four seconds in which a line was not held, and a rail, and a first-year's forearm. The department's four-item record of its own room is true and incomplete, and those two facts do not cancel each other.*

The refusals column was the longest on the page and the reason nine hundred people were going to be sent it. It held the Office's recommendation to lift four local holds with its three riders, the four answers in four sets of words with four names against them exactly as they had been read into a panel minute on the Thursday, the department's five conditions and the trust's six, the co-operative's own refusal of a request by A. Reed, nineteen, to read a four-column book of telephone calls aloud in a market, and A. Reed's doing it the following Wednesday by arrangement with B. Nunn, which was now a practice. And it held the fact that this co-operative has one design record in its holdings, that it came out of the box on a vote of eight to three, that the three noes were Sarr, Beattie and Nartey, and that the member who carried it was told at her own table and has not withdrawn it. *We do not name her. She was named in a newspaper eleven years ago and she still comes to market day. We are printing the fact of the box because the box is a hole in our holdings, and a hole we do not print is a lie we tell politely.*

And third from the bottom, under a heading that said *requests received and upheld*, with a rule above it:

> *Request: the Institute's September open-day issue register and the counterfoil stubs, for the third week of the September in the term now closed.*
> *Ground: public safety, equipment traceability, no witness.*
> *Requester: **M. Senn.** Upheld. Printed with the ground and the name, because the rule is that everything about a person, published by them, is published by name, and a man who asks to be found in nine hundred addresses has been found in nine hundred addresses, and we would rather he knew it on a Wednesday than in a corridor.*

And lower down, in the same typeface, because a first-year had published it herself in her own words in a district return and it was hers to publish:

> *R. Ilesanmi, nineteen, first year, maintenance track. On the Tuesday of week fourteen a steel bar on the front edge of a wall panel came across four inches and caught the inside of her forearm. She saw it coming and got her arm round about a second after it did. Four stitches and a review in nine days. Her own sentence, printed because she asked for it in and signed it: I want this in.*

Marek Senn put the sheet down on the bench, squared it, and then took his hand off it, because he had squared it and had not noticed he was doing it.

There was no taking a name out of a printed quarterly. The rule that let a person be taken out of a book on a nail in a market had been made by an ice merchant in a room with about nine people in it in about eleven seconds, and it worked, and it applied to one object on one board, and Bea Nunn had said so herself — *the whole row comes off the book, not just the name, and the taker's name stays in the fourth column* — and the fourth column of a quarterly is a column about an institution, it does not take requests, and would not take one, and it had a press deadline of eleven o'clock that night.

He had put a card into a tin in October with his name on it being the only requirement, a girl of nineteen had had to ask the room twice, and a co-operative of two hundred and twelve people had printed his name and the ground and the fact that it was upheld, and every word of it was his own, and the reason for all of it was that he had asked to be printed.

The third piece of paper came through the letterbox at about ten past one on the Friday afternoon, in three copies, folded in three. The letterbox of Civic Spine 7 has carried a printed sign since before Marek Senn came to work here saying that the shop does not take post, and nobody in eleven years has ever put anything through it, and somebody had put three copies of one sheet through it and gone away.

It was one side, in a plain typeface, with no logo and no name on it and no return address, and it said:

> *THE INSTITUTE'S RECORD OF ITS OWN ROOM IS NOT A RECORD OF THE ROOM.*
>
> *On Tuesday of week 14 the department published four items about an assessment in a room it owns. The four items are true. They do not contain: a word spoken from the front of a room that four people followed; four seconds in which a line was not held; a rail; a first-year's forearm; or the fact that a public room was created for two hours by a government office that has never had to create one and does not want to.*
>
> *The Office's own assessor said in the room, out loud, in front of witnesses, that no office in the district should ever have to do that twice. We are holding him to it, and we are not going to let him forget that he said it.*
>
> *IF YOU WERE IN THAT ROOM: any word of yours is now in a record. Records are how people are found. A name inside a finding is a machine and the machine does not care what the name was for. If you want something you said taken out, you may ask for it. There is no form. The only thing you have to give is where to send the answer.*
>
> *A box at the post office on Weaver Street. A box is not a membership. You do not have to join anything and nobody will know that you sent to it.*

He stood and looked at it for a while, and then he did the thing he was going to do, and it took him about nine minutes, which was the least time it had ever taken him.

He laid all three out in a row on the bench and read across them, and he made himself say out loud, in an empty shop, to nobody, what each of them claimed and what each of them was for, because a thing you cannot say in order is a thing you have not understood.

The department's four items were a record of a finding about a person, written by an institution about its own room, built to be held up and copied to a regional office. It would be believed, and in nine years it would be the document, because it would be the only one anybody could produce without a witness. The co-operative's sheet was built for nine hundred addresses, with about two hundred names on it and a blank box in it, by a co-operative that had broken its own rule once in two years and printed the three noes when it did it, at the cost of a woman's standing with a garage. It was the most honest sheet he had ever read and the one that had put the most people in it. The third sheet named nobody, said what the department's document was not, and offered anybody who had been in that room a way out of a record with no form on it.

And not one of the three said the thing that had actually happened, which was that a man had been holding a line with a boy in a room and had gone out of it for four seconds and had come back slower, with less, and had finished; and that a woman on a rail was coming down a set of steps on four because of him; and that a boy of twenty-two had lost six weeks of Saturdays because a foreman said the word college in a doorway; and that about fifty houses in this city had a plate in them, and there was a form with all ninety names on it in a drawer under a battery.

None of the three documents was wrong. That was the thing he had to write down afterwards and could not make sound true to anybody. The department's was true and it had four holes in it. The co-operative's was true and it had nine hundred copies. The third one was true and it had no name on it at all.

And there was no office in this city whose job it was to put the three of them side by side and say which one was the account. The one man in this building who had spent a term asking for exactly that kind of job had four people who could end anything he did in a fortnight, a conditional extension of an award he had not earned, and a woman from a trust who had told him in a corridor on the Thursday precisely what was going to happen in about four days, in a way that made it a choice and not a season.

He squared the three of them. He caught himself doing it and left his hands where they were.

Sera Quill came in at about four on the Friday afternoon and put down a tool bag she had not brought it for, which is how he knew.

"Right," she said. "That's the maintenance work, then. I'm going to say it once and you're not going to thank me for it, so don't."

"Understood."

"I don't think you do. You'll hear it anyway." She took her sleeves down. "The department's demonstration equipment came off the department's list on the Wednesday, and the contractor's obligations on the department's equipment came off with it. Both of them are mine now, for the term, and my name is on both, and I did not ask for either and I have got nothing out of it, and I would like that on a piece of paper somewhere, because in about four months somebody will ask me whether I wanted the work, and I am going to say no, and I am going to have to be believed."

"That includes the plates."

"That includes the plates. Twenty-four of them out of nine crates, six instructor booklets, four card sets, and a job card on a wall that I put up on the Thursday before the term began with a number on it." She said the number. "Seven-four-one-one-two. That was a service round number and it is now a custody number, because the bag out of that rack has it on the bag in my handwriting and the bag is in my cage."

"Where."

"Relay cabinet. Bottom tray, behind the isolators, on a padlock, and the padlock is on because of a thing I am going to tell you about in a minute." She looked at the bench, and at the three sheets on it, and did not come over. "There is a new label on that cabinet. It went on this morning, it is a printed one, I had it done at the shop on the corner, and it says KIT 4-19 — DO NOT REISSUE. It is the only printed thing in this building that says what that stock is, and I have not put a name on it and I have not put a date on it, and it is not coming off until somebody gives me a reason in writing that I can read out loud to somebody."

"The department's hold."

"The department's hold is where the department put it and it has not moved and it is not mine to move and I have not touched it. Same for the Cordage, same for Ninth Street, same for the clinic in the van. Five places in this city have 4-19 plates in them on the Friday of week fourteen and there are four holds, and the fifth place is a repair shop in Lattice Ward, and the hold on that one is a padlock and a woman's word, and that is not good enough and I know that it is not good enough, and I have written it down anyway, because the alternative is that the stock comes off a list and goes back into a van."

"You have taken custody. You have not lifted a hold."

"I have taken custody of a cage," said Sera Quill. "I have lifted nothing. If you write *quarantine* anywhere about this I will read it back to you. And the four are in one tray, one line each on a sheet in a sleeve, with no name and no date on any of them, and that is deliberate, because when somebody comes in here in about four years and asks me why I have got somebody's stock, I am going to be able to say *because they left it, and they did not lift it, and I wrote down the day I took it*. If there is a date on it, then in about four years it is a museum and not a cage."

"And the plate in the bag is in the same cage."

"The plate in the bag is on the third shelf and the bag has my number on it, and it is the only plate in this city that anybody can look at, and it is in a repair shop and not in a department's store, and the day it goes into a department's cage it becomes a fact about a thing that happened instead of a thing that needs mending, and I am not having that." She stopped. "And the other thing in that cage is a brown envelope with a boy's name on it that came off a registrar's desk in week four, and it is in the tray next to the bag, and they have been in adjacent trays since the Tuesday morning. If anybody comes and asks me for either one of them, I am going to hand it over, and I am going to tell them what is in the other tray, because the second of those two objects is going to make somebody extremely interested in the first one, and I would rather it was me that said it than a form."

"Marek."

"Don't." She picked the bag up. "That is the whole of it and there is nothing in it for you, and I have not been asked for anything by anybody, and I would like to be allowed to go and do a job."

She got to the door and turned round.

"One more thing, and it is about the page you are going to write."

"What page."

"The ledger. Trust's first condition. Monday at nine, the department and the trust may read it." She shifted the bag. "I have started you a book. It is the shop's, with the shop's name on the cover and my name on the inside, because I am the named supervisor under condition two and a book with a name on the front is a book somebody can come in and ask to see. And when you fill the first page there will be no room on it, because you write small and you write a great deal, and I am not a woman who writes in a gap in the middle of another man's page, and I am certainly not going to write on the front of it. So I will write it on the back of a form and gum it in. It will be in the margin, and you will find it when you find it, and it is not a favour, and I am not going to be thanked for it, and I am not going to explain it now."

The door shut.

On the Saturday of week fourteen Talia Venn's van was in the lay-by outside the wholesale kitchens at about four in the afternoon with the roller door up and the diesel auxiliary off, and Marek Senn stood at the back of it for about an hour and ten minutes in the light gone, and neither of them sat down.

She had the folder with the two hundred and six pages in it on the seat beside her and she did not open it.

"Two things, and the first one is mine," she said. "The man in Bower Street asked me on the Tuesday for every page I have written since the eighth week, I said no, and it came back on the Thursday as a request with a number on it, which is completely proper, and about two thirds of what he is asking for is other people's, and I am not going to hand a man in an office two thirds of other people's. I will have to say something in about a fortnight in a room with a man who writes things down, and what I am going to say is that I hold two hundred and six pages in a folder in a drawer, and that producing them is how they become somebody else's." She put her hand flat on the folder without opening it. "The reason I am saying no to it is the same reason I made you come down eleven feet off a lane to put your name in pencil on the back of a card in week twelve. It is not distrust. It is that a page in a drawer is mine and a page in a file is theirs, and the difference is about nine seconds long, and it is the whole of what I know."

"That is not my fault."

"No," said Talia Venn. "It is nobody's fault, which is the worst version. If it was your fault I could hand it over and be angry and be finished."

He had been holding it since the Tuesday, for about four days, and it came out of him standing at the back of a van, which is how everything in this case had gone for thirteen weeks.

"There is a room."

She did not move.

"There is a room in a public building on a residential road in Nine Bridges," he said. "A reading room on the first floor, a capacity of sixty, a booking form, fourteen pounds the morning and twenty pounds deposit, and a box on the form that says *name of person responsible*, and a line underneath it about an address and a telephone number in case they cannot let you in, and a line saying the room is not available to any group whose purpose is not stated in writing on the booking. I found it on the Friday of week thirteen and I have not been and I have not rung, and I wrote it down and put the page in the back of the load book. I told Hollis Pratt he was right, that it stays cold, and that I would write it down somewhere and not in that room. It has been in the back of that book for six days and every time I have opened it I have looked at the page."

"Whose name."

"Mine."

"Then it is shut."

"Talia—"

"It is shut, and I am not going to have the argument with you in a vehicle about a form with a box on it, and it is not the fourteen pounds, and I am not going to let it be the fourteen pounds. Because if it is the fourteen pounds then in about four years somebody will find four hundred pounds and open it, and that is how a rule turns into a price. The reason it is shut is that you have spent a term refusing to put a name in a building so that nobody can be found later, and it is the most correct thing you have done since the Thursday of week one, and you did it about nine times, and I have watched you do it and I have never once agreed with you out loud. And a room that costs your name is a room you can have. And in about four years there will be two thousand people sitting in rooms that cost their names, and every one of them will have been told by somebody kind that it was the price of the room."

"Who would take it out for them."

"Nobody," said Talia Venn. "That is the question, and it is the only one, and I have been waiting thirteen weeks for somebody in this case to ask it, and you are going to have to decide what the answer is going to be, and you are not going to be able to decide it by having one room."

The lay-by was emptying out. Two men went past the back of the van with a pallet between them and one of them said something to the other about a Thursday and neither of them looked in.

"Every room you have been in this term has become a room you were in," she said. "I do not mean that unkindly, I mean it the way an engineer means it about a pipe. In that corridor on the Tuesday, in front of nine people, you and I disagreed about a girl's forearm, and neither of us apologised, and neither of us went and stood somewhere else, and that is the best thing that has happened in a public room in this city in thirteen weeks, and I was in it, and I want it understood that I was in it and that I did not have a room. So here is the practical part, and I am giving it to you as a professional, and I would like it written down in a book with a crane on the cover or in a book with the shop's name on the front, and I do not care which.

"This clinic and your shop are four miles apart and they are going to stay four miles apart, and if they ever stop being four miles apart then every person in this city who has a plate on, and every person who has been short of breath at four in the afternoon, and every person who has a wrist that has been done for three years, ends up standing in one room with a nurse and a repairer in it and a door. And the door will be the reason they come. I have been the door for eight years. You have been the door since the Sunday of week thirteen — you put a number on a board in a market and you are a door now, and I have watched a man become a door this term and I have watched what it does to a room. And if I stood in that room with you, then I would not be a nurse in it. I would be the reason the door was open. And the thing being said in that room would be two people who found each other, and every person who came through it would be finding us."

"That is the best argument anybody has made against me and you have made it in four sentences in a car park."

"I made it in about ninety seconds and I have been making it since the Wednesday of week eight, and you have never once asked me for it. And there is a smaller thing, and I am telling you it because you will otherwise do it. I have a first-floor room in Bower Street with a man in it whose title is continuity and returns, and I am in it on Tuesdays until this is finished, and I am not going to put you in that room. Not once. Not to be helpful. Not to be in the room while I answer for a list of ninety names. If you asked me I would say no, and you would not be able to argue with me, and I am telling you now so that you never have to find out what that would have cost you."

"I would not have asked."

"I know. That is why I am telling you."

She got the folder up off the seat, did not open it, and held it against her chest with both arms the way somebody holds a box of tools.

"And the last of it, and I am going to say it quickly and then you are going to go, and I am not going to say one word more about it than the words themselves. Thursdays. Not this one. The one after. And if one of us cannot, it moves, and it moves without anybody writing down why. There is no form. There is nothing in either of our records. I am not going to be the person who puts a day in a diary and calls it a plan. And if anybody in this city ever asks either of us what that is, we say the same sentence, and the same sentence is: we had an hour."

"That is an—"

"It is an hour," said Talia Venn. "And if you make it into anything else I will not come, and I will not be unkind about it, I will simply not come, and you will find out on a Thursday. And I am not going to kiss you, and I want to say why in one sentence, so that you do not spend a fortnight working out whether I have thought about it. I have thought about it. In a van, on a route, at about six in the morning twice this week, with a plate in a bag in a coat. And I am not going to do it in a lay-by outside the wholesale kitchens at four in the afternoon on a Saturday in week fourteen of a term in which a boy of twenty-two lost six weeks of his wages on Wednesday, and I am not going to do it because I would be doing it to end a day, and I have watched somebody do that to somebody else this term and I have watched what it cost, and I am not going to be the second one of us."

"I was not going to."

"I know that as well. You are not going to, which is a completely different thing from you not wanting to, and I have known the difference between those two things about you since about the seventh week and I have never mentioned it." She went past him to the step and got down. "And I keep a copy of every page I write. Two hundred and six, in a folder, in a drawer, and I have never given one away, and you are the second person in eight years who has been near that, and neither of us is going to say what that is either, and I would like to get out of this vehicle now."



She dropped the roller door about halfway and then shouted over it, at the last minute, with her hand on the bar.

"Your fifth room. It is shut, and it is the right decision, and you did not make it for a good reason. The maintenance ledger opened at nine o'clock on the Monday morning of week fifteen on the bench at Civic Spine 7, on its face, with a pencil on a string beside it, and it was a shop's book with the shop's name on the front and the words *maintenance ledger* printed underneath in a typeface from about 1978, and inside the front cover, in the same hand as the rest of the shop's paperwork, a line: *S. Quill, lead repairer.*

Nobody came.

He wrote the first line of the term on the inside cover on the Sunday night, in the small upright hand, and read it back about nine times and did not improve it, which was a thing that had happened four times in a term and had stopped being a virtue:

*Opened Monday of week 15, 09:00, trust's condition one. First reading: nobody came. The department sent nobody and the trust sent nobody, and neither of them telephoned, and Darla Vint asked for a photograph of the first page and said she would come and get it herself, which is not the same as coming. A thing that is open one morning a week to a department and a trust and has one reader in it is not an institution and I am not going to be the one who calls it one in the middle of a term.*

At about ten past ten on that Monday morning, which was about forty minutes after she came off the flatbed at the upper pound, Nkechi Osei came into Civic Spine 7 in her own coat and stood at the bench.

She had not been invited. "You've got a book open," she said.

"It's open. It's a ledger. It opens on Mondays at nine because a trust says it does, and anybody can read it, and that is the whole arrangement. And there's a list on the wall."

She read the list. It took her about twenty seconds and she did it the way she did the steps, which was without hurrying and without any appearance of hurrying.

"No."

"Right." She nodded once. "Good. I did not come to ask you to put it in, and I would have said no, and I have said no to you before and I would have said it better. "I asked you for a thing in a lane in week thirteen and I have not had it and I am not going to have it, and I want to say the reason out loud in this shop, because I have said it in a lane and a lane is not a place anybody writes down.
