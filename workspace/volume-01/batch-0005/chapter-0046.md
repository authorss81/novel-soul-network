# Chapter 46 — Four Seconds

The window opened at forty-two minutes past nine and for about four minutes the room did not know what it was for.

That was not a fault. That was the panel, working as Professor Onyeka had said it would, one thing at a time, in an order nobody had chosen, and the order was: the ones who were not him went first, because the plan said so in item four, and item four was the item that had cost the most to write.

Dessa Kwan did it in about nine seconds with her back to about half the room and her hands on nothing, because there was nothing to have hands on and she had said on the Saturday that she would not know where anybody was standing and had not been told. She did the thing she does four hundred times a week, which is take a weight, and then say out loud what has been on her, and then hand the next decision back, and then go. She said it in eleven words and none of them were the word.

Rafi Pell did his step in the air, badly, the way he said he would, with a courier bag strap over one shoulder that he had brought with him and that he took off halfway through and put down on the floor, and he did not look at anybody, and the panel's note-taker wrote down the words *courier balance step, no fourth beat present* and then, after a moment, put a line through them and wrote *no fourth beat present*, which was a thing that girl of twenty would think about for a long time.

Reuben Daw went to the fire door that does not latch, and put his shoulder against the frame, and said: *Two crates, off a truck, up a run, in about nine seconds, on a Saturday, and I have never been watched doing it and I am not going to be watched doing it on a Tuesday, and there is no run in this room and the panel may write that down and it is true, and the run is about two hundred steps and the last forty of them are the ones that count and I cannot show anybody the last forty of them in a college.*

And then it was the two of them, and there was a lot of room left over, and Professor Onyeka said, "The window is open. The candidate may begin."

---

Tam Arrick put his right hand out palm up at about the height of his own belt, standing up, with nothing in it.

He did not put it out on a chair and he did not put it out on a table and he did not put it out towards Marek. He stood in the middle of the floor in a room with about thirty plastic chairs in it and a whiteboard with a dead term on it and a plywood board with an empty rack, and he held his right hand out into the air, palm up, fingers slightly apart, the hand that has not closed properly since the week before the term began and whose middle finger has not moved at all, and he did not look at it.

Marek Senn stood about a foot and a half away from him, facing him, with his own hand somewhere in the region of Tam's elbow, not touching.

Nobody in that room could have told you what the line was. Professor Onyeka wrote *two-person line, physical, as the candidate and Mr Arrick described it to me in the corridor, not demonstrated and not visible* on her pad, and that was a completely honest line, and it was the sentence that the whole of the last twenty minutes of that morning had been about: that the two things you can do in a room and the one thing you cannot do in a room are the same two things.

"All right," said Marek Senn. "Here it is. It's going to be slow and I have told you beforehand that I have never done it, and there is nothing to look good."

"That is not a thing you need to say," said Tam Arrick.

"It's the only thing in the plan I can say, and it's mine, so I am going to say it, and you cannot stop me saying it, because item five says you can stop the whole thing and not one word of it."

"That's fair," said Tam Arrick, and moved his feet, half an inch, and settled.

And Marek Senn did the first part of a boundary release, in a flat voice, with no shape on it at all, in a room with a panel in it.

The first part is the hardest to invent and the easiest to do, and Talia Venn had shown him once, in a treatment bay, in week five, with a bag of bricks on a table: you say what is on you. Not what is on the plate. What is on *you*. And it is a list, and it is short, and it is a thing you have actually noticed.

"Seventeen seconds past nine last Thursday, a woman's hand, on a wall" — he heard his own voice doing it and it was flat and it was not a performance — "and a cold floor, and a boy's arm, and a room going out of a man in a wet jacket, and about four hours of sleep a night since the Thursday of week one, and the inside of my own front left tooth, which is the one I have been biting on since week eleven and which I did not know I was doing until I said it, and a headache that has been behind my right eye since about a quarter past nine, which is now."

The headache moved while he said it. It went from behind his right eye to behind both eyes and stayed there, and it was not a headache any more, it was a band.

"That's the first part," he said. "Second part is what's off me, and I have got it, and it's four things, and I'm going to say it fast because it's a list and it's not a story." He looked down at his own hands. "The store room's not in this room. The crib in room two's not in this room. There's no word in this room that belongs to anybody, and there's nobody in this room I have to be anything for, and the last one is that the term ends on Friday and I have not told a single person what happens on Monday and I do not know."

"Stop."

And Marek Senn stopped, because the word came from the boy, and the boy had not been asked and had no cue to give and had given one anyway.

Tam Arrick's hand was still out, palm up, in the air.

"Stop," he said again. "You went out of it. I felt you go. You've been out of it for about three seconds and I want you to know I felt it go, because that's the thing you told me you couldn't do and I can do it, and it's the whole reason I agreed to this and I would like it written down that it's the reason."

Marek Senn said, in a room with a panel in it, in a voice with nothing in it at all:

"Don't finish it for me."

The room made a noise that was not a noise.

"I know what that sounds like," he said, to Tam Arrick, and to the panel, and to the eight people behind them, and he did not stop and he did not soften it. "You're standing there with your hand out and you have just said the truest thing anybody has said to me in a term, and I felt it arrive, and it went straight to the exact place in me that was opened, and I am refusing it, in front of everybody, and it is going to cost me this and it is the only correct thing I can do.

"You are not the instructor's voice. You are not my voice. You are a boy of twenty with two tendons cut who has been taught a card since September by a man in that corridor, and I have watched what that does to people, and I did it to a man on a dock in week six, and I am not doing it to you in a room with a panel in it, and if I take two seconds to refuse it then the attempt fails and the attempt is allowed to fail and that is on the sheet and it is item five and it is me who wrote it."

"Also you're not allowed," said Tam Arrick. "Item four. *To say a word that somebody else supplies.*"

Nobody in the room moved.

Marek Senn stood in the middle of a floor in a room that was no longer a public room the moment the panel rose, with a band of pain behind both eyes, and he put his own hand out — palm up, about a foot under Tam Arrick's, not touching, and he could not do it, and the second part of the thing came out of him in a hurry and in about four words and it was the correct words.

"Nothing's on me. That's it. That's the second part. I'm not adding to it and I'm not explaining it." He could hear himself going faster. "Third part is the one I have never done. Third part is I say what's been shared and I say whether anything's still shared, and then I decide what happens next, and it's mine."

"And what's still shared?" said Professor Onyeka.

"Nothing."

"Nobody's?" said Professor Onyeka, and her pen was not moving.

"Nobody's," said Marek Senn. "There is nothing in this room that belongs to another person and I have not taken anything out of it, and the answer is no, and nothing stays, and there is no echo, and I am not carrying anything of yours out of here, and the next decision is mine and the next decision is to stop."

He was going to say the last four words and the line went.

---

It did not go slowly. There was no moment at which he could have said that it was going.

He was *in* it, that is the thing, and he had not understood in advance what that would mean, and the understanding arrived in his body and not in his head about half a second after his attention left him. His attention left him because he looked up.

He looked up because the room did something. Not the room — the rig. The bottom rail of the demonstrator panel, which is a steel bar the fold-down frame swings out from, at knee height, the front edge of the only demonstration equipment in the building, came across about four inches, with a noise like a dropped bar, and hit the wall bracket and stopped dead.

And the return had come back onto the folded plate in the rack behind the perspex on the second shelf, which Sera Quill had said she would not touch until somebody told her in writing that she could, and which was still in a rack on a live board in a room she had cleared a load out of and had not cleared a wall of.

And there was a girl of nineteen on the floor at the front of the room.

She was on the floor because a witness with a notebook sits on the floor at the front of a demonstration when the demonstration is at knee height and does not stay at the back of a room, and Toby Ansell had told her to bring a notebook, and she had been writing since the window opened, in the two minutes of it that she had been able to write anything, and the rail came across at the front of a plywood board and caught the inside of her left forearm from the wrist to about four inches below the elbow, and there was a sound, a small one, and the notebook went out of her hand, and she did not scream, and she did not say anything at all.

Marek Senn saw the whole of it. He saw it the way he saw a plate — not as a picture but as a shape, with the load path in it, and the load path ran from the folded contact through the bracket into the rail and along the rail into about two hundred and thirty millimetres of a girl of nineteen's forearm, and he was standing four feet away and he was the only person in that room who could see the shape of it, and he opened his mouth and he could not make a sound come out of it.

Two people are holding it. One of them is not.

He tried twice. He got a shape out that was not a word on the first one. On the second one he got *move* out and the room did not hear him, because a room that has been given a word by somebody at the front of it takes about a second and a half to hear a different word from a different place, and everybody in that room had been listening to a light and had not been listening to a man.

That was the four seconds. Not the arm. The four seconds were the length of time it took a room full of people to stop listening to a rig and start listening to a man, and in all four of them the rail was against a forearm and he could not get a word into the air in a shape that would move anybody, and he was in a two-person line holding a release and the line was not there.

He felt the boy go.

Not figuratively. He felt the far end of the line lift off, the way you feel a hand let go of a rail in the dark, and the terror came up the line into him — cold, enormous, total, and *twenty years old* — and it was the week-one drill, it was the exact texture of it, a boy in room two with his boot coming off a ladder, and it was not Marek's and it did not go away when the line went, and it sat in his chest with his own headache and it was the worst four seconds of his term by a distance that he had no vocabulary for.

Somebody had not dropped the line.

That was the thing he understood third. The line had gone slack and it had not been dropped, because the far end of it was still there, held, by a person, on purpose, and a boy of twenty was standing in the middle of a floor holding a line open with nothing in his hand because he had been asked to and had decided, on his own, in front of a panel, that he was not going to be the thing that let go.

"You are still—" Marek Senn started, and the boy's voice came along the line and into him, in a flat voice, no shape, nothing on it, and it was not a completion and it was not the instructor and it was not his own and it was a person saying *I am here*, and it went into the exact place in him that was open, and it would have worked.

He took it out of his mouth like a splinter.

"—still holding," he said. "Stop holding. You are not allowed to hold that for me, that's the whole—"

But a chair went over, and Toby Ansell had got there, and Toby Ansell got the hardback notebook out from under the rail with two fingers and pulled Rhoda Ilesanmi's forearm along it, out of the way, in one movement, and Rhoda Ilesanmi said "oh" in a small voice and then said nothing else for the rest of the minute.

And from the door, eleven seconds after the rail went across, the isolator went over with a bang, and the room went dead in a way that is not like a light going out. Somebody was counting. Somebody had been counting since the rail moved, and it was not Marek Senn.

Marek Senn stood in the middle of a room with a dead board behind him and a dead room in his chest and a boy's terror in it that was not his, and the window was open, and the chair's clock was running, and there was no line, and he could not stop the practice, and the practice was still going.

He could not stop it because stopping it was the third beat, and the third beat is *the practitioner returns the next decision to the subject of the brace*, and there is no brace, and there is no practice, and the only thing there was to return the next decision to was himself, and he was not there, he had been on a board four feet away watching a rail go across a girl, and for four seconds the only man in the room had been a man looking at a fault, which is the one thing he is actually good at, which is the thing that has ruined every room he has ever stood in.

So he finished it.

Slower. With less. And he did not accept a voice, and he did not accept the two words, and the pain in his chest did not lift and he did not ask anybody to take any of it off him.

He said: "Nothing's still shared. The answer is no. Nothing stays." And then he stopped, and looked at the whiteboard with a term on it that had ended, and said, "And the next decision is mine, and the next decision is to stop."

"Window closed," said Professor Onyeka.

It had run for a minute and forty-one seconds. She wrote it down. She did not look at anybody.

---

There were four people in the corridor at eleven minutes past ten and one of them was on a chair against the wall with her sleeve pushed up.

Marek Senn got to her first because he was nearest and because he was the one who had caused it, and the two of those are the same thing, and he knew which of them was true and he knew the other one was also true.

Her forearm was about four centimetres of opened skin along the inside, from about two fingers above the wrist up, and it was clean and it was not deep and it was the kind of thing that gets four stitches and a review in nine days, and it was bleeding at a rate that was entirely ordinary and entirely terrifying at the same time.

"Rhoda—"

"It's nothing," said Rhoda Ilesanmi, and did not look at him. "I saw it come. I want to say that before anything else anybody says, and I want to say it in this corridor, because I was on the floor with a notebook and I saw a steel bar come across about four inches and I saw where it was going and I got my arm round about a second after it did, so I have had a look at it and I know exactly what it was and it was not a surprise and I would rather that was in everybody's head before anybody says anything else."

"You have a first aid box," said Sera Quill, from about four feet away, to nobody, to everybody, in a corridor. "There is a first aid box on the wall in the fire lobby of this building and it was put there by me in the autumn and it is got my name on a tape on the side of it. It's got a cold pack in it. Go and get the cold pack, Rhoda, and don't get anything else out of it, and put it on that, and hold it. That's it. That's the whole of what I know and I am not going to be asked for a second opinion by anybody in this corridor, and if anybody comes at me with a second opinion I will say what I said in there, which is that there is a folded return contact in a rack on a live board and the isolator is off and the rail is the front edge of equipment that isn't in the room now and I have put it in the corridor and nobody in this building is going to be having an argument about it with me in a corridor."

Rhoda Ilesanmi went and got the cold pack and came back and sat down on the chair and put it on her arm.

Then she said, "I have to be honest with somebody about something, and I'd like to do it now while it still feels like something, because by Friday it'll just be a thing that happened to a first-year on a Tuesday and I'd like to be a person who said it."

"Go on," said Professor Onyeka.

"From about two minutes past ten" — Rhoda Ilesanmi looked at the floor — "from when the bar went, to when Toby got me, I could hear him. Not his voice, his *counting*. I have never met him before this morning. I was on the floor with my arm against a bar and I could hear somebody in a room counting out loud, and the count was not even, and it stopped about half a second before Toby got me, and I want somebody to write down that the first thing I was aware of was not a bar, it was a man counting, because I'm going to be asked about it in about nine years by somebody who isn't me and I'd like it in the record that I heard a count and not a rail."

Nobody said anything for about two seconds.

"That's a fact," said Professor Onyeka, and wrote it down, and looked up at Marek Senn.

He was standing in a corridor outside room one of his own department with a band of pain behind both eyes and a boy's terror in his chest that had not lifted and was not going to lift for some hours, and he had understood about ninety seconds ago that he was now a man who had hurt a nineteen-year-old in a room in front of a panel, and that the sentence in the ninth week was now not the only thing on the table.

"Senn," said Professor Onyeka. "You are not to leave the building. The panel is going to sit for about forty minutes and then rise, and I am going to hear everybody who saw anything, and I am going to hear you last. I am not going to hear you in a corridor at ten past ten in the morning and I am not going to hear you in front of nine people who have not given evidence yet. Is that clear?"

"Yes, Professor."

"And the line," said Professor Onyeka. "Mr Arrick held a line after the practice had gone out of it. I have written *the practice* and I have not written *the candidate*, and I am going to ask you in forty minutes why, and I am not going to ask you now, and I want you to sit down, because you are grey."

"I'd rather not sit down."

"Sit down," said Professor Onyeka, "and Mr Quill, if the panel requires a witness to say one more word about that isolator before anybody leaves this building, I would rather it was said now than in forty minutes."

Sera Quill did not look at Marek Senn.

"Yes, Professor," she said. "It's off and it's locked off and I have tagged it and the tag says what I told you it says, and there is a folded copper return in a rack on a board in your corridor with a number on it, and that is the only sentence I've got and I'd like the panel to have it now while I can still hear myself say it. Nine seconds. It is not a fault number, because there is no fault. It is a plate that took a load and nobody let it off it, and if that plate had been let off on the Thursday before the term began, as it should have been, then the bag would have come off that frame on the Monday and nobody would have been stood in front of it for a term at all, and I have said that sentence in three buildings this year and in every one of them there was somebody in the room who wanted it to be about a bad batch."
