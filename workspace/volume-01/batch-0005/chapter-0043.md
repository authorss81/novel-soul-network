# Chapter 43 — Ten Past Six

The van was on the pull-off off Fell Road at ten to six in the morning with the flatbed's tailboard down and the board under a tarpaulin, and Corvin Ashby was standing in the headlight with a tea flask and a list on a piece of stiff paper with a rubber band round it, and it was thirty-one degrees and dark and the light had not properly got going in the autumn.

"Nineteen," said Ashby.

"Nineteen."

"You make twenty." He did not look up from the list. "I've got eighteen names on this and one of them's an apology. Twenty-year-old lad, Welsby, had a wedding on and he rang me at four this morning and cried about it on the telephone, which I've had to listen to twice, and I want it noted that he cried and I said it was all right."

"That's the round, then."

"That's the round." Ashby put the flask in his pocket. "And I need to tell you the two things I've got to tell you and then I want to talk about nothing for four hours. One. I took Danny Pryce and young Cunliffe off the steps this morning and put them on the pound, because the pound's a man short and the pound is the worst job on it and I was doing both and doing neither, and which is the exact thing I did in the spring of last year, and on the Monday of week thirteen I told you in a lane with a bar in it that I would not be responsible for a thing I do not understand, and I have been responsible for a bar for nineteen years and I do not understand nine foot of water under a closed gate, and that's the honest position."

"And two."

"Two. Your man is on the pound." Ashby finally looked up. "You're the pound. I put you on it at four o'clock yesterday afternoon when I heard you were coming, and I'm telling you that before you're asked, and I'm not asking, and if you'd rather go on the steps I'll swap you for the lad who cried and I'll take the bar, and don't tell me you can't do the pound, because I watched you carry a man off a chain in December and you carried him like a man carrying a sack, which is the correct way and the only way."

"I'll take the pound."

"Right." He handed over the stiff paper. "That's the order of the day. Cutbank first, the steps on the way down, then the upper pound and back, and we do it on the ebb, and the ebb is ten past six off the tide table, and if the water's not down where I want it we sit on the tailboard and drink the tea and I am not a man who hurries a tide."

Nkechi Osei was down at the Cutbank already, in the dark, with a bar and a coil of orange cord, working the last two steps in a head-torch, and she was alone, because it was ten to six and the men came off the buses at a quarter to six.

Marek Senn stood at the top of the steps for about a minute and did not go down, and did not write anything, because he had learned in week thirteen what watching a woman come down a set of steps for half an hour actually does and he had not done it once since.

She came down twice, counted, and went up twice, and on the third time, coming down, the count in the air above her was still four.

He went down and took the pound.

---

Nkechi Osei came off the steps at ten past six and said, to nobody, "Right," and looked at nineteen men and a bar, and then at Marek Senn, and said, "You."

"Me."

"You've not been on a round."

"No."

"Then go and stand on the pound and don't help anybody for an hour." She shouldered the bar. "That's not me being hard, that's me telling you that a man who helps in the first hour is a man who's still working out what the work is, and I am not doing this job with a man working it out."

He went and stood on the pound. The pound was a stone landing about the size of a kitchen, with about four foot of drop on the upstream side and a handrail on the wall that had been a pipe in a different century, and every eleven minutes somebody came up from the steps and went along it, and Marek Senn's job for four hours was to be the second man when a bar came up, and to hold the top of a thing so that two men could get a wet chain onto a flatbed without anybody's foot going in the gap.

At about twenty past seven, on the second pound, a man of fifty-six called Roy Ancrum put his left leg over the tailboard of the flatbed and his right knee went, in the way that a knee goes, and he was down on the boards on his side with his hand still on the rail and his leg out straight.

Marek Senn got to him in about three seconds. Roy Ancrum said, "That's the knee, that's all, don't lift me," and Marek Senn said, "I'm not lifting you," and Roy Ancrum said, "Right," and lay on a flatbed tailboard in overalls and looked at the sky for about forty minutes.

He got up on his own at two hands, using a rope round the rail, which took him about a minute and a half, and he put the leg down and took the weight and stood in it for a while, and then he walked the length of the flatbed, and came back, and did it again, and said, to Marek Senn, "That's nothing. That's a knee."

"That's a knee," Marek Senn said.

"It's nothing today. It'll be nothing tomorrow. It'll be a thing in nine years." Roy Ancrum wiped his hands on his thighs. "Forty-one year I've been on the water and eleven on this, and I can tell you exactly how it happens, and it's not the steps, and everybody thinks it's the steps. It happens on a flatboard at eight in the morning with the leg out straight in a wet jacket, and you do it about nine thousand times, and about five thousand of them is how you get it."

He went back on the pound and did the rest of it and nobody asked him to sit down again.

At about ten to eight Marek Senn found Corvin Ashby on the flatboard with the stiff paper and a pen, and a district form on a clipboard, and Ashby said, "How's Ancrum."

"He went over his knee on the second pound and got up on his own and did the rest of it."

"Right." Ashby turned the form round. "Then that's the form. Read it."

It was a single side, and it wanted four headings in writing with a date: who wrote it, what it touches, how a person stops it, how many people have been put through it. And under the four headings there was a space for a person's name, and under that a space for a *designated* person, and a note in small print at the bottom saying that a return completed without a name would be recorded by the district as *unidentified*.

"We write *unidentified*," said Ashby, "and it goes in a drawer in a district office about eleven weeks, and that knee is on a flatbed in about nine years instead of in a book now, and that's the whole argument and I've had it with four people in nine years and I have never once won it, because writing his name in on a Monday morning in October gets a report in the spring that says *informal* and the pump money goes."

"Your book."

"My book's got no names in it," said Corvin Ashby, "and it has not had any names in it for nine years and there is a reason for that which I have explained to two men in a fortnight and I am not explaining it to you on a Monday at ten to eight in the morning with a man's knee on my flatbed." He held the pen out. "Do you want to write his name in."

"No."

"Then I have to write *unidentified* and I have to write it now, while I can still remember the shape of it." He wrote it in a small flat hand and signed under it and gave the form to nobody, and put it in the inside pocket of his coat, and it was a piece of paper with three words on it that would be the only record in the city of a knee.

"Right," he said. "Now we're going to talk about nothing for three hours."

---

At about twenty past eight, on the last pound, the bar went.

The steps at the Cutbank are two concrete treads with a lip on the last one and about nine inches of green on them at six in the morning, and the bar is a scaffold tube with a hook on the end of it and it is the only thing that has to be there all the time because if it is not there then the first man down has nothing. Nkechi Osei had it on the second tread and it slipped, and it went sideways off the lip, and she went down on her hip on the wet concrete with the rail in her other hand and about four hundred pounds of tube clattering into nine inches of water.

Eleven men on a round do not stop for a woman who has fallen on the steps. That is not cruelty, it is arithmetic, and everybody on that flatbed knew it, and the eleven of them kept moving and two of them turned round.

Marek Senn was on the pound four feet above the steps and he was the one who could stop, and the round could not, and in about two seconds he was going to have to do something with his mouth to a woman who was on the ground in a head-torch with a tube in the water.

"It isn't me," she said, before he said anything.

"I know."

"I want to say it now, before you have done anything, because I can see you getting it ready." She was up already, sitting on the top step with the rail in her left hand, working her right hip. "The tube went. I did not count. I was on three and I had the bar in the wrong place and I have been on four since the Monday of last week and today I put the bar in the wrong place, and those two things are connected and it is not your fault and I would like both halves of that said at once, in that order, by you, now, in front of these eleven men, because in about a minute one of them is going to say something helpful."

"Correct," said Marek Senn, to eleven men and a woman on a step. "It wasn't you. The tube went and you had it in the wrong place. You're on four and you've been on four since the Monday of last week, and it is not because of him, and nobody is going to be able to take it out of you in this city, and I have not got anything to give you this morning except that sentence and the fact that I am saying it in front of witnesses."

Nkechi Osei looked at him for a second.

"Right," she said. "That's the one thing I wanted and it took you four seconds and you'd have had it in one if I'd asked you for it, and I didn't ask, so write that down too, because it's the second time."

She got the tube out of the water with a hook and stood up on the top step and tested the leg and said, "I'm working."

And that was the entire event, and it lasted about ninety seconds, and eleven men saw it, and the round went on.

And at about half past nine, when the water was down and the last two loads were on the flatbed and the bar was back on the tailboard, Nkechi Osei came up the steps past Marek Senn and stopped, and said, without lowering her voice, because a round is a round and men are on it:

"You asked me a question in a pull-off last Tuesday and I answered it and I've had six days with it and I want to give you the second half. On Monday I came off that step and my first thought was not *I've hurt my hip*. My first thought was *he's going to say something*. And there was nobody standing there. You were four foot above me on a pound and you were working a bar and you couldn't have said anything." She shifted the tube on her shoulder. "That's the thing that's frightened me. On Monday of last week it was a man on a bank. This morning it was a man four feet up with a bar in his hands and it was me waiting for him to talk, in a place where he was busy. That's worse than the bank and it's worse than the Tuesday and it's a Friday of flood season, and it's going to be a Friday of flood season about nine more times before the year is out."

Marek Senn stood on a pound in the dark with his hands on a rail and had nothing.

"Then don't wait for me to talk," he said.

"I know," said Nkechi Osei. "That's the trouble. I know it and it doesn't help, and I have told you the only thing I have and you can have it for your book." She went up the flatbed.

---

He came off the hill at about eleven and Sera Quill's voice was in the messages on the bench, and there were six jobs on the Monday list with his name against all six, and he had told her on the Friday evening that he was going up the hill at ten past six on Monday morning and would be back by eleven, and she had said one sentence, which was *then write it on the list, because in about a month somebody is going to notice and then it will look like you hid it.*

He wrote it on the list at ten past eleven, in her hand and under her line, and put the time on it, and then went and did four of the six, and did them well, and did not do two of them, and did not do them because he was not going to be back in Lattice Ward by two in the afternoon.

He did not write the two on the list. There was nowhere on the list to write two, because the list had one column for jobs and one for names and a line at the bottom for *out of area, agreed*, and the two jobs were in Lattice Ward, which is four miles away, and he was not going to be told they were out of area, because they were not.

He noticed that on the Wednesday, at the bench, at about ten in the morning, and he did nothing about it for about two hours, and then he did something about it, and what he did was write both of them at the bottom of the list with a time, and put his initials against them, and the initials meant *not done* and everybody on that bench in the history of Civic Spine 7 knew what initials at the bottom of a list mean.

Sera Quill read the list at two o'clock, and read the two lines, and read the initials, and put it back on the nail, and said nothing, and that was the fifth time.

---

He was at the bench at about ten past ten that night with the extractor on and the door open and the roll out, and he had done nine hours in a van and a van and a stone landing, and he was not tired in the way that sleep fixes.

The load book has four headings and a *Do not know* at the bottom, and it has been in this flat since the Thursday of week one, and there has been an entry on most nights since, and the entries have been getting longer and not better. He turned to a clean page and wrote three lines and no headings, because he had three things and three things do not need four headings.

*55. Monday of week 14. The round, 06:10 ebb, Cutbank to the upper pound, nineteen men, one bar, and a knee on a flatbed at 07:20 that is in no document anywhere in this city and is written in a district form as *unidentified* in a coat on a hillside. I was the man on the pound. I have now been the second man on the pound in two buildings in four days and I have not been asked to do anything in either and both of them turned on me anyway.*

*56. The bar went at about 08:20 and she went down on the steps and I was four foot above her and the round could not stop, and she said "it isn't me" before I had got anything ready, and she made me say the whole sentence out loud in front of eleven men — not because it helped her, she was up and working inside ninety seconds, but because she wanted it said where people could hear it and she had worked out in six days that she could not say it about herself.*

*57. AND HERE IS THE ONE I HAVE TO WRITE DOWN. I broke rule three and I broke it in a hurry to get to somebody else, which is the only way I have ever broken it. The rule is one person one thing and take the answer. At ten past eight in the morning I had a form in front of me and a pen and a man's knee and Ashby holding the paper out to me and asking me a question, and I said no, and then I asked him a second question in the same breath about a third thing, and I asked him about the pump money because it was the thing I could make a sentence about at eight in the morning with a man's leg out straight on a flatbed, and I did it to get to a comfortable place in a conversation that was about a man I could not help, and I have written in a book in week twelve that the rule is not about information and that I only ever break it in a hurry to get to somebody else, and on the Monday of week fourteen at ten past eight in the morning I did it on a hillside with eleven men in earpieces and a bar in the water.*

Then, at the foot of the page, where the heading says *Do not know*, one line, because there was only one and it had been in the book since week eleven and had got worse rather than better:

*Do not know whether anybody who reads this book after me will use it to work out what I should have done on Tuesday. I keep it for attention debt. That is all it is for. On Tuesday I am going to be the fourth thing in a room one last time and there is no line in this book that will tell me what to do, and I have known that for a fortnight, and the only thing I have done about it is to have written down four hundred words about the fact that I have not done anything about it.*

He put the pen down at about ten past ten and read it once and did not add anything, which was the first time in six weeks he had read a page of that book and not made it better, and he noticed that too, and did not write it down.

Then he went to bed at eleven, which was the last night before the most important morning of his term, and it was the shortest, and he lay in the dark under a laundromat with a very bad right shoulder and listened to a dryer in bay four that had been going since Michaelmas, and he did not sleep much, and at about two in the morning he got up and wrote three things on a fault form and pinned none of them to anything.

The third of them was: *There is a strip light in that room and a whiteboard with the term dates on it and a strip curtain and about thirty plastic chairs that belong to a nursery, and the room will be locked at nine by a caretaker's key, and the thing that makes a room public is not the chairs and it is not the light and it is not me standing in it, and I have until a quarter to nine in the morning to work out what it is.*
