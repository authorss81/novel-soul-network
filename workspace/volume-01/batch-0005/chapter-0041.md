# Chapter 41 — A Key Is A Thing You Are Given

The Friday of week thirteen was the first day out of term that Marek Senn had ever spent entirely in daylight, and he found that he did not know what to do with his hands in a shop that was not open.

Civic Spine 7 ran a day shift through the summer, seven till four, six days, and the Friday list had six jobs on it and five of them inside the district. He did them. A lamp ballast in a stairwell in Nine Bridges, a relay cabinet in a laundrette he did not go into, a door interlock on a cold store in Broadfields, a bell transformer at a school that was shut for the summer, and a fitting on a handrail at a clinic in Crown Terrace that took him forty minutes and would have taken anybody else twenty and that he got right the first time and then sat on a step outside for ten minutes afterwards because being right about it had stopped meaning anything to him anywhere.

At half past twelve he got a sheet of the department's headed paper out of the drawer by the bench and put it on the wood and uncapped a pencil and wrote, at the top, *A public release on Tuesday*, and under it, *What has to be true*, and then stopped, and then wrote one line, and then sat looking at it.

The line was: *Four people, and the four of them have never been in a room together.*

He looked at it for a long time.

It was the whole of the problem and it was not a problem about consent at all, and every version of the last twelve weeks had been a consent problem, and he had got quite good at consent problems, and the difficulty was that nobody who could help had ever been asked for anything, and everybody he wanted to ask had heard his voice already.

That was Asha Reed's sentence, or Tobi Ansell's, and whichever one of the two of them had actually said it, it was on the page in a first-year common room in a cold converted store room, and it was load-bearing now in a way that had nothing to do with teaching. **The room has to be able to hear the important thing from more than one mouth, and he is not one of the mouths.** If four people were all told by him, in four rooms, with him standing up in each of them, then on Tuesday there would be five people in the room who had had the same conversation with the same man, and the panel would be looking at a man with four assistants, and the four assistants would be looking at the man, and any one of them could say afterwards that they had done what they were asked, and it would be true, and it would be a room with a cue in it that was wearing four bodies.

The list of everybody he might ask was six lines long and he had written it out before the week began. Rafi Pell. Dessa Kwan. Tam Arrick. Reuben. Nkechi Osei. Mal Farrow. He read the six names on the heel of his hand where he had written them in biro in a kitchen at seven in the morning three days earlier and he crossed out three of them before he had finished.

He crossed out Nkechi Osei, because using her again, three days after standing on a bank and changing how she comes down a set of steps, was not a plan, it was a second helping. He crossed out Mal Farrow, because a man of sixty-one had said *not yet* and had said the condition himself, and the condition was *when he asks me, and not before*. And he crossed out the fourth name, which was his own, which was not on the list, and which he had not meant to write down.

That left three, and one of the three had told him in a market on a Monday in front of forty people that a procedure with a man in a chair in it was the same problem wearing a lanyard.

---

He went down to the tool roll in the tray at about one o'clock because he wanted a shim for a frame, and the roll is a canvas thing with six pockets and a strap and it lives in the tray in the cage door, and everybody in that shop has known for twelve weeks that the fourth pocket has a grey card in it and that the card is a piece of rubbish and that he uses it.

He did not take the shim. He took the card.

It is a grey transit sequence card, about the length of a hand and a half, with the corners gone soft and a shallow file scratch across the printed beats where a file had gone over it years ago and somebody had decided the card was still worth keeping. There are four printed beats down one side in a type that has not been in use in this city for a very long time. Underneath them, ruled in the same ink and the same hand, there is a fifth line, and there is nothing on it, and there has never been anything on it, and the card is the only object Marek Senn owns that he cannot account for.

He had looked at the fifth line once in seven years, in week nine, at this bench, and had worked out that a father can leave a blank line. He had got nothing else out of it then and he was not going to get anything else out of it now.

What he was thinking about was not the fifth line. What he was thinking about was the four printed beats, which were in a card, on a table, in four different rooms, in the hands of four people who had never met each other and who would be in a room on Tuesday morning in front of a panel.

If everybody had the same card, then their practices were comparable. Not taught to each other — comparable. Two men in a room who have both read the same four lines have something to disagree about, and a disagreement is the only thing in this case that has ever been worth anything, and the card would have made it in about nine seconds and it would have solved the problem that had eaten his whole term.

It would have worked. He turned it over in his hand and looked at the scratch across the printed beats and worked out, in about four seconds, exactly how he would do it: copy it, four to a side, on a machine, at the copy room at the end of the department corridor, in the last week of a term when the department had nothing on the machines and the caretaker's letters had nobody to enforce them. Four cards. Fold one into each of four pockets. Walk into four rooms. *This is the same card I have. Tell me where yours is different from mine.*

It was a key. That was the whole of it. That was what it was for.

He put it down flat on the bench, squared it with two fingers the way he squared everything, and then he stood there with his hand on either side of it and did not pick up the pencil, for a while, and the extractor fan was going and there was a lorry outside.

Then he wrote on the corner of a spare parts tag, in the small upright hand he used on tags, five lines, and tore it off and put it in the fourth pocket on top of the card, which is where tags go and where nobody would ever look at it again.

*1. A key is a thing you are given.*
*2. I would be making one. There is nobody in this city who could check what I put in it.*
*3. It would be the only object in that room that four people could not refuse, and I have spent thirteen weeks on the belief that a thing nobody can refuse is the whole of the case.*
*4. The scratch across the four printed beats is not mine and I do not know who left it and the card is a shim.*
*5. If I fill in the fifth line on the copy, then the fifth line is a thing I have written. It is not a thing anybody has given me and it is not a thing anybody else has written.*

And then, because he had been honest for five lines and he was going to be all afternoon, a sixth:

*6. Four people who have never heard each other is the plan. A card is not four people. A card is me, four times.*

He put the grey card back in the fourth pocket and rolled the strap over it and put the roll in the tray in the cage door and went back out to the van, and it took ninety seconds, and he thought about it the entire time.

---

The Exchange at ten past one on a Friday is a room with the trestle tables pushed together and the shutters half up and about nine people in it and none of them there for him, and Dessa Kwan was doing a delivery in the old kiosk at the back with a clipboard, and she has a way of doing a delivery that makes it look like a fight she is winning.

He stood in the doorway and did not go in and she looked up and knew him in about a second and a half and came out with the clipboard under her arm.

"Ask it," she said. "You've been standing there a minute and a half and you've rehearsed it, and if you rehearse it out loud in my kitchen doorway it stops being a question and starts being a performance, and I have got about nine minutes before this is on the pass."

"I need you to do a thing on Tuesday morning," said Marek Senn, "in a room, in front of a panel, and I need you to say no first and I'll take the no, and then I'll come back with the rest of it."

"No," said Dessa Kwan.

She did not say it unkindly and she did not say it slowly and she did not follow it with anything for about four seconds, and the four seconds were worse than the rest of the day put together, because he had asked her to refuse first so that the refusal would be a thing he could hold, and she had understood the request inside it and had refused without giving him even that.

"Right," he said.

"Not right. You've still got a question in your face and I'm not answering the question in your face because you didn't ask it." She put the clipboard down on the pass. "You want me in a college on a Tuesday morning, and about eleven people come through that door every day asking me whether the thing on the shelf in here is a good one, because there's a nurse with a van who parks a hundred yards up this road and does not advertise it. Nine of them have been in here in the last month asking me a question about a plate, and if the third-year's name is on a piece of paper next to mine then every one of those nine is one conversation away from a department, and I have spent nine years making that door boring. Boring is the only thing I've got and it's the only reason people come in here and tell me things."

"That's fair," said Marek Senn.

"It's not fair, it's mine." She picked the clipboard back up. "And then the other thing, which is the one you actually came for and you're pretending you didn't. You don't want me to be in that room to do a thing. You want four people in that room who have never heard each other say the same thing to each other, and I can see it on you from here, and I want you to know that I know it, because if I say yes to the version where you ask me nicely then in about two years there'll be a woman on a pass somewhere who does the thing you asked me for because a man in a college asked her nicely once."

"I was not going to ask you nicely."

"You were going to ask me properly, which is worse, because properly has a page on it." She looked at him for a second. "And I'm not doing it in a kitchen. I've said that once, in a yard, in front of a man with a stopwatch, and I meant it then and I mean it now, and I don't say it twice for an audience so I'll say it once for you: *I don't do it in a kitchen.*"

"I know."

"You don't, but you'll use it. Everybody uses it." She turned back to the pass. "Go on. Out with it, because I've got eight minutes and one of them's mine."

"There's a version of this where I pick four people and you are one of them and you have never met the other three and nobody tells you what they are doing and nobody tells them what you are doing, and I don't get to see anybody beforehand, and each of the four of you can stop it by word at any point including in the middle, and if you stop it then the whole thing stops and the stopping goes in the record as a stopping and not as a failure."

Dessa Kwan put the clipboard down again.

"That's four," she said. "And you haven't got four."

"I've got three."

"You've got two and a half and one of them is a man who counts to two out loud before he answers you and the other one is twenty-two and on a Saturday rate." She wiped her hands. "And I'll tell you the thing you don't want, and I'm telling you because I have watched about nine men build a room and I have watched what it does to them, and I'd rather be unkind now than polite on Tuesday. You'll find three in a week. You'll be pleased with yourself. And the fourth one, the one you leave till last because you're saving him, will be somebody you've already stood in front of in a room, and it'll be the ninth voice, and by the time you notice it's Tuesday morning and there'll be a panel in the building and no time to unbuild it."

"How do you know what I'm going to do."

"Because you wrote it down on Tuesday night at about two in the morning, and a man who does that at two in the morning has already read the last page and copied it out." She said it without any edge at all. "I don't know a thing about you. I know about four in the morning. Any fool with a pencil knows about four in the morning."

He left the Exchange at ten to two with nothing at all, which was the correct outcome and the first time in thirteen weeks that walking out of a room with nothing had felt like work done rather than work failed.

He worked the last three jobs and then drove the van back and put it in the yard and did not go home, and at ten past four on a Friday afternoon in the autumn he sat in the front room of a flat above a laundrette on Pell Street with the light gone and the dryers going through the floor, and he telephoned the shop downstairs.

"You told me not to ring you at half past nine in the morning," he said, when she picked up. "It's four ten."

"I know what it is," said Enid Sarkis. "Go on."

"You made me put a date at the top of the page and a line at the bottom, and one of the two lines said that if nobody had looked at your wrist by the end of the term then somebody was to ring you and say so in words, and you'd take the reason and not the apology. I wrote it. It's my handwriting. I initialled it in front of you."

"And when's the end of the term."

Marek Senn looked at the window. Below it, in the shop, four dryers were turning and the fifth had been out of order since the summer before last and had not been fixed because he had fixed it for free in week four and had not written it on the day book properly and nobody had chased him.

"It was the Friday of last week," he said. "The twelfth. It was the Friday the term ended. It was the same day as the boy's weight date at the hospital, which is the only reason I remember it."

"Right," said Enid Sarkis, and he could hear her not moving about, which from her was a kind of shouting. "So that's a week, then. And nobody rang."

"No."

"And you're ringing now."

"I'm ringing now," said Marek Senn, "and I'm going to say the reason, and it's the reason, and I want you to have it in one piece so you can be finished with it. The reason is that there is no job. There is no person whose job it is. I have spent thirteen weeks looking for one and there isn't one and there is not a body that appoints them, and the only thing I could have given you on Friday of week twelve was a telephone number on a wall in a market, and that is a number in a crisis and you are not in a crisis, and I would have been handing you a crisis to fill a gap, and I have decided that I would rather ring you on a Friday afternoon with nothing than hand you that in the autumn and let you think a man's got this sorted."

There was a dryer going on the other end of the line that was not one of the four.

"That's it, then," she said.

"That's it."

"No, that's the reason," said Enid Sarkis. "You've still not told me what I asked you to tell me, which was somebody looking at it. So: is there anybody."

"No."

"Anybody at all."

"There's a nurse in a van," said Marek Senn, before he could stop himself, and then heard himself do it, and then had to stand there and listen to it.

"A nurse in a van," Enid Sarkis repeated. "That's the fifth one since Sunday. That's five people in thirteen weeks and none of them a job and all of them something that comes round." Her voice did not change at all. "I have told you four times that you are going to come round on a bus. You have now told me that you won't, four times, and then you told me about a van, which is coming round, except in a lorry, and worse, because a lorry has a shelf on it."

He did not say anything.

"There's a woman on my counter's wrist done four years and there's a woman on my counter's wrist done three," she said. "That's two, and I've not written either of them down, and I've not told anybody, and you didn't ask me that, and I want you to notice that I told you anyway, because I have decided that it makes me feel better and it is the only thing in this conversation that does."

"That was your right and you did not owe it to me."

"No," she said. "I did it for me. Write that down in the book with the crane on it. And don't you dare come up here and look at it. If you look at it you'll be a man who looked, and a man who looked is a man who's seen it, and I'd have to start again with somebody I don't know."

"I won't."

"You'll come round on a bus," said Enid Sarkis, "and I'll be here, and that's the last of it, and put the phone down, the dryer in bay four has been going since Michaelmas."

He put the phone down. He wrote two lines in the notebook, which was not the load book and never had been.

*Ringed Enid Sarkis at 4:10 on Friday of week 13, seven days after the date on the page. She asked whether there was anybody and there is not, and I said the answer first, and she took the reason and did not take the apology because I did not offer one. She told me that there are two women on her counter and she has written down neither and told me anyway, for herself. There is now a third person in this case who has told me something for free. In thirteen weeks. I am the fourth in the room in all of them and I do not think I have noticed it once.*

*She has a plate on her wrist and there is no job. On Tuesday a panel is going to record that a man performed a release in a room and the plate on her wrist is going to be exactly where it is. Nothing I do on Tuesday touches that. I want that written down by me tonight, in a term when I am tired and honest, and not discovered by me in March.*

---

At half past five he looked up the Fell Road branch library on the terminal in the office at the Spine, which is a slow machine with a modem on it, and found the reading room on the second floor of a building on a residential road in Nine Bridges with a capacity of sixty and a booking form.

Fourteen pounds for a morning. A deposit of twenty. A name.

He read the third box twice, because it took him a while to understand that it was a box on a form and not a figure on a website, and the box said *name of person responsible*, and underneath it in small print it said *and address and a telephone number in case we cannot let you in*, and it said *no bookings by telephone*, and it said that the room was not available to any group whose purpose was not stated in writing on the booking.

That is the fifth room. It has been on his list for nine days. It holds sixty people, it is not a department, it is not a panel, and nobody has refused it, and the cost of opening it on four hours' notice is fourteen pounds and a deposit and a name, an address and a telephone number, in ink, in a ledger, on a shelf in a public building on a residential road, where anybody who comes in and asks to see the booking sheet for a Tuesday morning can read it.

He looked at that for a while, and then he understood that the ninety households and the September register and a woman in Nine Bridges with a hip unit fitted in a car park for nothing had all been the same sentence, and that the sentence was *put the name in the building so somebody can find you later*, and that he had spent the last four weeks refusing to write one letter into one postbox for exactly that reason, and that the fifth room was going to cost him the identical thing at fourteen pounds a morning, and that Hollis Pratt had been quite right to tell him to write it down somewhere and not in that room.

He tore the last page off a pad at the Spine and wrote it in the small tag hand.

*Fifth room: Fell Road branch library, reading room, first floor, sixty. £14 the morning, £20 deposit. Booking form wants a name, an address and a telephone number in ink and says the purpose has to be stated in writing. It is not a department and nobody has refused it. Do not name it to anybody in that building. If the assessor says room one is not a public room, this is what it costs, and it is not fourteen pounds, and I have known that since the Wednesday morning and I did not want it, and I am writing it down at the end of a day when I have been told no twice so that I cannot pretend tomorrow that I did not know.*

Then he tore the page out, folded it in four, and put it in the back of the load book, which is where he had put the other three things he did not want to look at in a term.

He got up at half past five because he had to be at the yard at seven in the morning, and he was in the room with the light gone and the dryers going through the floor, and there were four days on the calendar and a flood round on the Monday at ten past six and a boy he had not asked who was not coming, and he had asked one person one thing and been told no in nine words and given a warning he had not paid for, and the only thing in the whole day that had gone right was a refusal he had asked for.
