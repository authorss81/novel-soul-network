# Chapter 45 — What the Room Does

The word went out of Davan Rusk at twenty-one minutes past nine on the Tuesday morning of week fourteen, in the teaching shape, at the pitch the laminated card on the plywood board has got printed on it, with the small fall at the end of it that a room full of frightened people hears as a competent adult.

*Scope.*

Nobody had asked him. Nobody had given him a signal. He was standing at the front left of the room in the place a man stands in a teaching room because he has stood in one for forty years, and the light above the board had gone out, and the sound from behind the perspex was the sound his own board makes when a contact is tired, and his mouth did the thing it has done perhaps four hundred thousand times, and by the time it had finished being a sound it was in twenty-one people's ears.

Marek Senn felt it in his sternum before he understood it, the way you feel a door open in a room you thought you were alone in.

And then he watched the room do the thing that the whole of his term has been about, in about four seconds, with no conspiracy in it at all.

He had been telling people since the Thursday of week one that the fourth beat was a gap. He had been telling them for thirteen weeks and he had been right every time, and he had never once, in any room he had ever been in, seen what an actual room does when a gap gets filled, because a gap is only a gap when there is nothing in it, and there had been a word in it for about a fifth of a second and it had come from the front of the room from a man with a folder under his arm, and the room had a shape again.

Ruslan Petric, twenty-three, Group C, the second candidate on the Tuesday, who had been standing by the window with his hands behind him, took his hands out from behind him.

That was all he did. He took his hands out from behind him and put one of them flat on the back of the nearest plastic chair and set his weight over the back leg of it, knees soft, the way a person sets their weight when they are about to take something and have not yet been told to. He had been in Group C since September. He had been taught the 4-19 card in September by the man who had just spoken, along with a hundred and ten other people.

The two note-takers the department had brought up from the third year, a girl of twenty and a boy of nineteen who had been in Marek's own group, half-turned towards the front. Not moving. Turning. Their hands were still on their folders.

And at the very back, on the end of the third row, a boy of twenty with two tendons cut in his right forearm and a middle finger that had not moved since the week before the term began, turned his head about six inches.

Nobody else in the room went anywhere at all. A woman of twenty-seven in a coat did not move. A man of twenty-one in a clean jacket did not move. A boy of twenty-two in work clothes did not move. A girl of nineteen with an empty hardback notebook did not move, and Toby Ansell next to her did not move, and a woman of fifty with a hand truck and thirty plastic chairs on it did not move, and neither did the panel, and neither did a lead repairer in overalls with a tool bag, and neither did Hollis Pratt by the whiteboard with his pen capped.

Dessa Kwan said, later, in a corridor, that the count of people in that room who followed it was four, and that she had counted them herself in about two seconds while it was happening, and that two of the four had been in her own group at the Institute two years ago, and that this was the first time in her life she had seen a thing move through a room the way a smell moves.

Professor Onyeka did not stand up.

"I am going to rule on that in about two hours," she said, "and not before, and I want everybody in this room to hear the reason, and the reason is this. If I rule on it now, the only person in this room whose position is affected is the candidate. I will not make a determination whose only effect is on one man while the room is still moving."

The room was still moving. Marek Senn could feel it in the plastic chairs.

"So I am going to say three things now and hold all three, and I am going to say them in the order in which I intend to rule on them. One: the word was spoken at twenty-one minutes past nine by a member of the teaching staff of this department, who is not a member of this panel, and who did not address it to the candidate. Two: the panel will determine at the end of this sitting whether it was used, and the panel's test for *used* is the sentence in the requirement, which is *no substituted cue used, followed or accepted*. Three: that determination is mine and not the department's and not the Office's, and it will be recorded with the time and the word and everything that happened in this room in the next four minutes, and I am going to ask everybody who saw anything to keep their own note of it in their own hand, in their own book, in their own time, and I am telling you that now while it is fresh so that nobody can say afterwards that I asked for it."

"And Professor," said Anders Kolt, from the trust, without opening his laptop, "the sentence in the requirement is *used, followed or accepted*, and those are three words, and a person can be followed without it being accepted and accepted without it being followed, and I'd like the panel to be clear that it is ruling on three things."

"Three things," said Professor Onyeka. "Thank you. Noted, and the panel will rule on three."

Sera Quill had the perspex up and had not touched the plate.

"Right," she said. "I'm going to do the thing I said I'd do, and I'm going to do it out loud so that nobody in this room can say afterwards that a repairer moved furniture in the middle of a proceeding, and I'd like the chair to note the time." She looked at her watch, which was a cheap one on a lanyard. "Twenty-three minutes. Right. Ruslan — you, in the window, with your hand on the chair — take your hand off the chair and put it flat on the wall and stand there.

"The rest of you on this side. You, in the blue coat, you're facing the front, turn round and face the door, and you — the boy — step back two paces and stop. I'm not telling you to be anywhere. I'm telling you where you are, because four of you are standing in the shape of a person who has been told a load is coming and there is no load, and I have watched four men get hurt this year by nobody being in a position to say *there is no load*, and I am in a position to say it and I am saying it."

Nobody argued. That was the part that Marek Senn wrote about afterwards, and the part he could not make sound true to anybody: Ruslan Petric took his hand off the chair and put it on the wall without a word, and a girl of twenty turned round and faced the door, and a boy of nineteen stepped back two paces, and a boy of twenty at the back of the third row put his hands in his pockets and left them there.

"Marvellous," said Sera Quill, to nobody. "Now I'm taking the frame off the wall."

"Ms Quill—" said the district woman of fifty.

"It's the department's frame and it's bolted through into the Spine's board and there are four bolts in it that I put in on the Thursday before the term started, and I have a job card with a date and a number on it, and I am not asking anybody's permission, and if the department wants to complain about it they can complain to the trust about my round." She had a driver in her hand. "Right. Two hands, and I'd like the small one on the bottom, and there's a bar in a bucket somewhere and if anybody's got it — thank you."

She took the fold-down frame off the wall in about ninety seconds with one of her crew holding the bottom bracket, and she did it in a room with a panel in it and thirty plastic chairs and a woman with a hand truck, and she carried the frame out into the corridor and set it down flat on the floor, and came back in and unscrewed the bracket plates and put the four bolts in her pocket, and then she went to the canvas bag of practice mass, which was on a low shelf under the board, and she put it on the trolley's third shelf and wheeled the trolley to the door.

"That is the whole of the demonstration equipment in this room," she said, "and it's in a corridor, and I have put it there deliberately and I will leave it there until somebody in a position to write it down says I may put it back."

"Sera," said Marek Senn.

"Don't." She did not turn round. "I know what you're about to say and it's about the sentence in the ninth week and the thing the panel can act on, and I'm a repairer and I've had a board with a folded contact in it twice in eleven years and both times a person was told by a person to have a go at the same frame within a month. So put your hand flat on the wall if you want to. That's all I've got and it is not a technique and it is not a lesson, and you can have it or not."

There was a silence, and then Dessa Kwan, from the middle row, said, "There was never any reason for the bag to be in there."

Nobody spoke.

"I mean it," she said. "I've been in this room four times since September because my kitchen's on the Saltmarket route and the college takes a group down there, and every single time there has been a bag of mass in that frame and a light over it and a man in front of it, and in eleven years in that kitchen I have never had a bag of mass in a frame over my head with a man in front of it, and I have thrown about nine hundred of them across a pass in a service with about six other people in a room, and nobody has ever stood in front of me and confirmed anything, and the load came off and I put it down and I went on."

Dessa Kwan, twenty-seven, who has never been taught anything by anybody, in a room with a panel in it, sat down again.

"You can't be shown that by a light," she said. "I have watched three cohorts of yours come past my kitchen. You can be shown it by a light."

And Ena Truslove, who had been standing by the hand truck with thirty plastic chairs on it for about ten minutes, said:

"Am I in the way?"

Professor Onyeka looked up. "No."

"Right." She moved the truck about two feet along the wall, out of the sightline of the front. "I'll tell you the thing, since I'm here and I can't leave till you've done, and it's not about you. Eleven years I've been in and out of this room. Tuesdays, Thursdays, and every Friday in the summer until they took the second room away. And in eleven years I have never once been asked who was in it, and I've never once been asked to sign anything, and I've never once had anybody tell me there was something happening in here that mattered. There's been a strip light on for eleven years and the only time it's gone off is now, and the difference is that now there's people stood in it going *what was that noise* and not one of them has asked me to move my chairs."

She put her hands in her apron.

"I'd like to stay," she said. "If that's allowed. I've got four little ones coming on Monday and nowhere to put them, so I've got nothing to be anywhere, and I'd rather be stood here than be out in a corridor being told I've made a fuss."

"It is allowed," said Professor Onyeka, "and I am going to say why in a minute and it is the reason this room is a public room, and you are the reason, and I am aware that is a ridiculous thing to say to a woman with a hand truck and I am going to say it anyway because the alternative is that a government office takes a room off a caretaker's key and nobody in the building ever finds out."

---

At twenty-eight minutes past nine, Professor Onyeka said: "The demonstration equipment is in a corridor. Nobody has objected and I am not going to ask. I want to establish one further fact and I want it established before the window, not during it. Ms Quill — is that rig required in order to perform a boundary release?"

"No," said Sera Quill.

"Then this panel notes that it was not required, and the candidate did not ask for it, and it is not in the requirement as read out, and the panel draws no inference from the loss of it." Professor Onyeka put her pen down. "Now the practical part, and I am aware that I am about to be doing a chair's job. M. Senn. The requirement is a working non-hinged boundary release, performed by the person performing it, in a public room, with witnesses, and with no substituted cue used, followed or accepted within the scored window. The rig is in a corridor. Is there anything you need?"

Marek Senn had been standing at the front of a room for about nine minutes with his hand flat against a wall, and he had watched four people put themselves into the shape of a load that did not exist and had watched a repairer take the load away with a driver, and he had been counted.

"No, Professor," he said. "There is nothing I need. There never was a frame in this."

"That is the first thing anybody has said in this room this morning that I am going to believe without a cross-examination," said Professor Onyeka, "and I am going to say why, Professor, and you may minute it. On the Thursday of week fourteen the department's own register for this room contains a demonstration frame and a bag of practice mass. It has been on that wall since the Thursday before the term began. In thirteen weeks, in this building, in a document that a technician can read in four minutes, the required form of the demonstration was a mass, and a mass is a load, and a load is what the fourth beat is for." She looked at the panel. "I am not ruling on that. I am not capable of ruling on that. But I would like it in the minute that the chair noticed it."

---

Sera Quill and her crew took the perspex off the rack and put it in the corridor with the frame, and the rack was empty and the laminated card was still clipped to the top of the board, and the room got colder and quieter and about four feet bigger, and at half past nine Professor Onyeka made the panel find their pens.

"I am going to rule on the timing now, and I am going to do it in a way that is going to be uncomfortable for everybody, and I would like it minuted that I have said I was going to be and then done it.

"The chair's ruling on timing is this. The panel's test is whether a substituted cue was used, followed or accepted inside the scored window. I have held the determination on that since twenty-one minutes past nine because the only person in the room the determination affects is the candidate, and because a room that has just had a word go out of the front of it in the teaching shape is not a room in which a determination about that word should be made in the first minute.

"I am not now ruling on it. I am going to call the scored window, and the word was spoken eleven minutes and more before the window opens, and I am aware that eleven minutes is a distance that the panel has chosen and the panel has chosen it because the distance is a fact and the fact is more defensible than my judgement about what a person does with eleven minutes. And I am going to say this to the room and I want it in the minute, because it is the most important thing I have said all morning: **the word was spoken at twenty-one minutes past nine and the window opens at forty-two minutes past nine, and everything that happened in this room in between is not in the window, and I have not been told by anybody that they intend to use it as if it was.**"

Nobody in the room said anything.

"And the last part of the ruling. I am going to hold the determination until the end of the sitting, after the candidate's attempt, and I am going to hold it in front of the witnesses as well as in front of the panel, and I am going to hear the evidence of every person in this room who saw anything, and I will take the evidence of the department's own third-year note-takers before I take anybody else's, and Mr Kolt has already reminded me that I am ruling on three words and not one, and he is right.

"This is a nine-minute wait, and I am sorry for it, and I am not going to shorten it by pretending I already know."

And Professor Onyeka looked down at a sheet of paper with a line on it.

"Scored window opens at forty-two minutes past nine," she said. "M. Senn, you have stated that you will not see any of the four of what they do before this morning, and that is minuted. You have stated that all five of the people named may stop the attempt by word at any point, including the candidate, and that one stopping ends the whole thing and that the ending is recorded as an ending. Is that still the plan?"

"Yes, Professor."

"Do you want to change any part of it in the last nine minutes?"

"No, Professor."

"Then the panel will now observe," said Professor Onyeka, "and I am telling the room that the panel has a clock, and that the clock started when I finished the sentence, and that it is going to run for the length of a boundary release and not one second longer, whatever happens, including if nothing happens, and the person who stops the clock is me."

She put her pen down.

"Window is open."
