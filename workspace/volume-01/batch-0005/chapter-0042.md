# Chapter 42 — Four Written Things

He wrote the plan on a clean sheet of the department's headed paper at about half past six on the Saturday evening of week thirteen, at the kitchen table in the room above the laundrette, with the dryers going and a pen instead of a pencil because the plan was going to be copied four times.

He had read a great many written plans that term. He had read the four sentences in his own handwriting that Ekow Nartey had initialled and dated, and he had read a four-column sheet on a trestle table in a market with four columns and no fifth, and he had read a page the size of a playing card with seven headings written on the back of a card in ninety seconds by a woman in overalls, and he had read four columns in biro on the back of a kitchen supply order form belonging to a boy who was twenty.

What he wrote was this, and he wrote it in the order it had to be read and not in the order he had thought of it in:

*Things about Tuesday. M. Senn. This is mine and it is not a document and it is not going anywhere.*

*1. What it is. Five people in a room. Four of them do one thing each, the way they already do it, and nobody performs anybody else's, and there is no new practice in it and there is nothing in it that anybody in this city has not already done.*

*2. Who is in it. Four names, written by me, four cards, one name on each, one card each, and I keep none of them. And who is not: everybody. The department is not in it. The company is not in it. The two people who have said no to me twice are not in it and are not asked a third time.*

*3. What each person is asked to do. Their own thing. One sentence, in their own hand, on their own card, and it is not mine and I do not see it until Tuesday.*

*4. What nobody is asked to do. To follow anybody. To time anything to anybody. To be listened to. To be looked at in order to find out whether it was good. To say a word that somebody else supplies. To be brave, and I am writing that one down deliberately, because I have spent thirteen weeks asking four frightened people to be brave and there is no clause about it anywhere and there should be.*

*5. Who can stop it. All five of us, by word, at any point, including in the middle of it, including after it has started, and one stopping ends the whole thing.*

*6. What happens if somebody stops. The run ends. The panel writes down that it ended. I do not get to be disappointed in a room, and if I am, that is mine.*

*7. When it ends and how. After the panel rises on the Tuesday, or by eleven o'clock that night, whichever is first, and the five cards are torn up in front of the five of us and there is no copy and I do not want one.*

*8. The one thing that is mine. I hold a line with one other person while I try the fourth beat. Either of us can put it down, at any point, without saying why. I have never done it. I cannot describe it in advance. It is not a practice and it is not a technique and it is the only part of this I could not write down before the day, and I have written down that I could not write it down, because a man who cannot describe the thing is the thing.*

Then, at the top of the sheet, above the number one, in the biggest hand he had, in capitals, because he had read Asha Reed's face in a cold room and he was not going to get this wrong twice:

**THIS IS NOT A TECHNIQUE. THERE IS NO NEW PRACTICE IN IT.**

And he sat with the pen in his hand for about a minute and then wrote one more line underneath that, and it was the only line on the sheet that was entirely for him:

*9. I am not going to see what any of them does before Tuesday. If I see it, I will know what it is supposed to look like, and then I will be a room with a man at the front of it, and the four of them will be doing a thing for me. I have been that room twice this term and both times it cost somebody their arm or their work.*

He read the whole of it four times, which was a habit and not a virtue, and then he copied it four times on the department's headed paper, which was the department's paper and he was aware of that, and it was out of term and the copy room at the end of the practical corridor had four machines in it and nobody in it after four, and he had used that machine for a set of photocopied hand-sentences in week eleven and had given one to a deputy registrar and been given a dated slip for it.

He copied the sheet four times. He did not write four names on the four sheets. He left item three blank on all of them, one ruled line under a heading, because item three was theirs and not his, and he folded the four sheets into four, and put them in his coat, and got his coat off the back of the door and stood in the middle of the room with it in his hands and understood that he had just made a key and given it to four people, and that it was the correct shape of the thing and that the whole of this chapter's argument about a grey card in a tool roll was about the fact that a key is a thing you are given.

He made the four in the same week, in one hour, on one machine, on his own, and gave all four away.

Rafi's Saturday was the only Saturday he had, and he took it on the Tuesday and lost eleven pounds for it, and he said the figure out loud in the kitchen before he said anything else, which was the first thing about him that had not changed in a term and was now, Marek Senn understood, the only thing about him that was going to survive Volume 01.

"One. Two."

"That is a kitchen, mate."

"It is a kitchen *and* it is a Friday and you have known me three years and I have never once counted in a kitchen, and if I do it in a kitchen I do not get to complain about it anywhere else, and I am going to do it in a kitchen." He put the bag down. "Eleven pounds, by the way, because you are going to ask me to give up a Tuesday and I am telling you what it is now rather than at the end of it, and if you have come here to tell me the figure is going to be a shock then I would rather we both had it at once. Eleven pounds is about half a day's hot food, and I have not had a day off since the spring, and I will have a day off on the Tuesday and I will not be paid for it, and there is nobody in my depot I could ask about that because there is no such thing as a person whose job it is."

"I know," said Marek Senn. "I'm sorry. I can't fix the eleven pounds."

"You keep doing that." Rafi Pell got the kettle. "You keep apologising for things that aren't yours, and it's the single most Senn thing you do, and I want to say — I've watched you do it to four different people this term and it's not generosity, it's a way of keeping the thing yours, because if you're the one who said sorry then you're the one who did the thing and nobody's allowed to be angry about *you*, only about the building."

He put the kettle down and turned round and said, "Go on then, and I'll read it while that boils."

Marek Senn took out one of the four folded sheets and put it on the table between the two mugs and did not say anything about it.

Rafi Pell read it standing up, in a kitchen, in his work jacket, with a courier bag on the floor. He read item four twice. He read item five twice. He got to item eight and stopped for a while, and then went on, and then read the whole thing again from the top without stopping, which took about a minute and a half.

"Right," he said. "Three things and then I'm going to be late, and the first one is a yes and the second one is a condition and the third one is not a question, it's a thing I'm going to do and you don't get a vote in."

"Go."

"A yes. In writing, on that sheet, in my hand, and I'm signing it Rafi Pell, not P. Pell, because somebody in this case has got a record with initials on it and it was ticked in a different ink, and I'd like my name spelled out on a piece of paper somewhere in this city." He did not sit down. "The condition is on item four and it is the whole of it. You write *to be looked at in order to find out whether it was good*, and that's right, and I want one more line under it, and the line is: *nobody watches me to find out whether my step is a step.* Because my step is a courier's balance step and it ends when I stop talking, and it has no fourth beat in it, and the only reason it works is that I put my left shoulder against a wall, and on Tuesday there is no wall, so I'm going to do it in the air, badly, and it is going to look like a man doing nothing. And if anybody in that room — you, a panel, a cook — looks at me to see whether I'm doing it right, then I will do the thing that makes it look right, and that would be the first time in two years I have done anything other than what I actually do, and I am not going to be that man in a college."

"Agreed."

"Don't agree, write it down." He put his thumb on the blank line. "And the third thing, which is not a question. You've written *I am not going to see what any of them does before Tuesday*, and I want you to know that I have read that sentence about nine times tonight and I do not think you mean it, and I'm going to hold you to it anyway, and if I find out you watched somebody, I will stop, and I won't give a reason, and the sheet says I don't have to."

"I meant it."

"You meant it on Saturday," said Rafi Pell. "Nowhere else. That's all I know."

Then he wrote it out, in block capitals, on the sheet, on line three under the heading, in his own hand: *I do what I do with a bag. Nobody times me. Nobody watches me for whether it's good.* And he signed it, and dated it, and put it on the table and pushed it back across with two fingers, and then said, "One, two," again, to nobody, and picked the bag up off the floor, and went.

At the door he stopped.

"Who's the other three."

"Rafi—"

"You've got me, and you had one at the kitchen yesterday, and you had the one you told me about in week nine, and there's somebody at Saltmarket and you have not got him, because I have asked you every day this week and you have not mentioned him once and you are not that careless." He shifted the bag. "And don't tell me. That's the test. If you tell me now I'll start guessing and I'll get it right by Thursday and then I'm in that room for a reason that isn't mine."

The door shut. Marek Senn stood in a kitchen with a sheet of paper with two names' worth of handwriting on it and only one name on it, and the count was four, and he had one.

Tam Arrick came to Pell Street at about ten on the Saturday night with a bus token in his hand and a plan folded inside a jacket that was not his, and he would not sit down.

"Right. Read it out."

"I'm not reading it out, I'm giving you it to read."

"I've read about forty pages of your handwriting this term and I'd rather you read it to me out loud, because I want to know where you hesitate and I have never once in thirteen weeks been able to tell where you hesitate by looking at your face." He sat down at last, on the end of the bath, which he was not supposed to do. "Go on. All of it. And don't skip the bits about stopping, and don't put the kettle on."

Marek Senn read it out. It took about four minutes. When he got to item eight he stopped for a moment and then went on, and the thing that was true in the room was not the thing he had written.

"I have never done it," he said. "That's true. I can't do the fourth beat. I've tried on an empty plate at two in the morning with nobody in the room and my own mouth gets to the word before I do, and that was in week eleven, and I have not tried since, because every time I try it I am doing it to prove something to myself and there is nobody in the room and there is no panel and there is nothing at stake, and that is the most shameful sentence in this flat."

Tam Arrick did not say anything for a while.

Then he said, "Item four."

"Item four."

"Read item four again."

"*To follow anybody. To time anything to anybody. To be listened to. To be looked at in order to find out whether it was good. To say a word that somebody else supplies. To be brave.*"

"Yeah." Tam Arrick put the sheet down on his knee. "Right. Three changes and two of them are going to be yours and one of them is going to be mine, and I want them before I write anything, because I'm writing my own and I'm not writing on your sheet and you're not holding mine."

"Go."

"Change one, and it's yours, and it's the one that matters." He held the sheet up. "Item eight says *I hold a line with one other person.* Which other person."

"You."

"Then item five is wrong." He put it down. "Item five says *all five of us, by word.* There are five of us and one of the five is me and I am the one holding the other end of the thing you can't do, and if I say stop then the thing you can't do stops, and that's a power and you've handed it to the man in the case in writing, and in about a year somebody in a department is going to read this sheet out loud in a room and say *the affected party holds the release*, and by then it will be too late to argue with."

Marek Senn did not say anything for a moment, and then said, "Then it's four of us who can stop it and the fifth is me, and the fifth is me."

"Which is the opposite of what you wrote, and it's worse, and you should have written it."

"It's not worse, it's exactly right, and you have just written the whole of the second dependence in about sixty words on my kitchen table at ten o'clock on a Saturday, and I am not going to thank you for it because you have told me not to be thanked and I have agreed."

"Item four." He did not look up. "Nothing about me is on their equipment. I don't put a hand on a plate of theirs. I don't put a strap on. I don't stand in a frame. There's nothing in that college Tuesday morning that I touch, and I'll do the fourth beat on my own hand, standing up, with nothing on it, and if that isn't a release then the witness says it isn't and the panel writes down that it isn't, and that's the end of it and I don't want it treated as anything else."

"That's the only version there is."

"I know it's the only version there is. I'm telling you that I know."

"Change three," he said. "Yours."

"Item seven. *After the panel rises, or by eleven o'clock that night, whichever is first.* I want the second one to be *or at the door*, because you're going to be slow getting out of that building and there's going to be about nine people in a corridor and I'm not sitting in a car park while you talk to all of them, and the cards go in the bin at the door, and I'll wait, and if you're not out in twenty minutes I'm going home and the sheet's off."

"Right."

"And the last one and it isn't a change, it's mine." He picked the plan up off his knee and folded it in four the way it had been folded, and did not give it back. "I'm not writing on your sheet. I've got a page of my own with four columns on it and there's a space at the bottom and I'm writing this underneath the columns in my own hand and my own ink, and my name goes at the bottom, and the date goes next to it, and the seven headings go on it because that's how I do things and I don't do things on other people's paper."

He stood up.

"And I'm going to be the only one of the four of us who is there as the person it happened to," he said, from the doorway, "because that is a real job and nobody has asked me to do it, and the two of you get to be strangers in a room on a Tuesday morning and I have to be a boy in a room with the person who did it to me, and I have had three minutes to think about that on a bus and I have decided I am going to do it, and you are not going to thank me for it, and if you ring me tonight about the plan I will not pick up."

The door shut. Marek Senn stood in a kitchen with a plan and three-quarters of a Saturday, and he did not know that at about eleven o'clock that night, in a room on Sennet Row, a boy sat down on the end of a bed with a sheet of kitchen supply order paper on his knees, read his own four columns from the top, and then wrote, on the blank side, above the columns, in biro, going over the second line twice because the first one had not taken:

**COLUMN TWO IS WRONG. THE HAND IS GOING TO BE TOLD TO DO THIS, AND I AM THE ONE WHO IS GOING TO LET IT, AND I AM NOT TELLING ANYBODY FOR A FORTNIGHT, AND THE REASON I AM NOT IS THAT I DO NOT YET KNOW WHICH PART OF THE PLAN IT IS, AND BY THE TIME I DO IT WILL BE TOO LATE TO ASK.**

And he did not tell anybody, and he told Marek Senn nothing, and the fortnight started on the Saturday of week thirteen and the volume of his term ended on the Thursday of week fourteen with four days still to run on it.

Dessa Kwan came to Civic Spine 7 at eleven on the Sunday morning and asked for him at the counter, in her own coat, with her hands in her pockets, in a shop with three people in it, and by the time he had got his coat off she had been introduced to the day-book man and the apprentice and told them her name, which she would not have done on Friday.

"You came to my door on Friday," she said. "I said no. I'm not unsaying it. I said no and I was right and I've had two days to think about whether I was right and I was, and I'm still here, so write that down before I start."

"You told me to go and find them and not to bring you to them."

"I did."

"I haven't."

"You haven't," said Dessa Kwan, "and I have checked, and I want you to know I checked, and I checked by asking two people who would have told you in a minute if you'd been up the hill, and neither of them had heard your name, so you did one thing right and I want to mark it before I stop being generous."

She took the second sheet out of his inside pocket without asking, unfolded it, and read the whole of it standing up in the middle of a repair shop with a parts cage and a sink and a wall of handwritten notes behind her, and the apprentice stopped pretending to sweep.

She got to item four and said, "*To be brave.* You put that in a list of things nobody's asked to do."

"Deliberately."

"That's the only line on the page I would not have written and I'm not going to take it off." She folded it back up. "Here's mine and it's got a condition and the condition is not about Tuesday. On Tuesday I will be in a room and I will do a pass, and I will not know where anybody is standing and I will not be told, and I will have my own back to about half of it, and if anybody in that room — a panel, a student, a man in a lanyard, you — comes into the two feet behind me and puts a hand anywhere near me while I am doing it, I will put a pan down and walk out and the run is over and I am not coming back in and nobody gets to ask me why. That is a condition, and it goes on the sheet, and it goes in the record whatever happens, and if it goes in the record and somebody reads it in six months and thinks I was frightened, then that is what I want written."

"That is not what it's for."

"It's exactly what it's for," said Dessa Kwan. "I've had men in this city tell me they needed to stand behind me for two minutes. In this room. About food. Two minutes." She looked at him. "And the other thing, and this is the part that costs me, so I'm going to say it fast. Item five. I want to sign my name."

"You have to. It's the rule of the thing — everything about a person, published by them, is published by name. That's the Exchange's rule and it's a good one."

"It's my rule and I borrowed it off a girl of nineteen on a Wednesday, and she got told no by Marda Szabo for asking for a book, and I've had nine years of being the woman who runs the kitchen and I have never once put my name on anything in this market." She said it flatly and without any performance, and then she wrote it on the sheet in a small upright hand, *Dessa Kwan*, and dated it, and put it on the counter. "That's the cost. Bea's going to hear about it inside a week because Bea hears everything inside a week, and four men work this counter and one of them will say something about a college, and he can say it. So now you know what it is that I asked for, and I have not got it, because you haven't got a room yet."

"I've got a room at nine o'clock on Tuesday morning."

"Then don't tell me what it is," said Dessa Kwan, "and I mean that as an instruction and not as a mystery, because I have found out in thirty-one seconds that I do not want to be in a building where I know a date, and there is a nurse in a van who taught me that about nine years ago and I did not listen to her until about four minutes ago."

She went, and Marek Senn stood in a repair shop with a sheet of paper with three names on it.

The last one was the hardest, and the hardest one was also the easiest, and Marek Senn understood at about two o'clock on the Sunday afternoon that the reason was that Reuben had already refused him once in front of about forty people and had nothing left to lose by refusing him properly.

Reuben was twenty-two. He had been on the Saturday rate for six weeks, which in a yard means it is not a rate and it is not permanent and there is no such thing as a person whose job it is. Eleven men on a Saturday, six of them over fifty, five of them if those six are off, and he was the youngest by four years and the only one of the eleven who had said no to the third-year in a market.

He was stacking empties on a hand truck at the back of the Exchange at half past two on a Sunday and he put the truck down when he saw who it was, which Marek Senn noticed and did not think about until later.

"Fourteen months ago you asked me a question in this yard," said Reuben, "and I answered you and it was a no, and you have not asked me anything since, and I want that said before you ask, because if you have been in here for six months building a thing and I am the last piece of it then I am going to say no and go, and I'd rather say it than be part of it."

Marek Senn held out the fourth sheet.

Reuben did not take it for about four seconds. Then he took it and read it, and he read it twice, and he is one of three people in this case who can read a page twice and understand more the second time.

"No," he said.

"Right."

"No, and here's the two reasons, and the first one is about me and the second one is about the other four." He handed the sheet back, which nobody else had done. "One. On the Monday of week twelve I stood in a room with about forty people in it and I said no to a company man, and I said it because I'm the youngest on that Saturday and if I say yes then the other four have to stand up and decide and eleven men on a Saturday means if five of them are off I've got nothing to carry. I've spent six weeks making myself one of the five they can rely on and I did that by being the one who said no first. And if I say yes to a college in September, then in about four months a foreman is going to say *Reuben's in with the college* and about four men are going to hear it, and one of them is going to think it means I've stopped being hungry, and I will be hungry on the Saturday, and I'll have said something clever in a market instead of loading a truck."

"Two," said Marek Senn.

"Two is worse." He leaned on the truck. "You've written that the four of us have never heard each other say the same thing. Fine. That's good and I like it. But the person asking me is the man a woman on a rail has changed how she comes down steps, and everybody in this city who's heard anything about it has heard it as him, so the second I say yes I'm not a stranger, I'm the fourth thing through a hole in the wall. And I don't know how to not be that, and neither do you, and you'd not put that on a form."

"Then don't," said Marek Senn. "Don't say yes to me. Say yes to a piece of paper that doesn't have my name anywhere on it."

Reuben turned the sheet over and looked at the back, which was blank, and then looked at him.

"You've written *I keep none of them*."

"I've written four sheets and I have handed three of them away and I have not got yours, and if you say yes I will hand you the fourth and there will be four of them and I will not have any of them, and the only name in this city that appears on one is D. Kwan because she decided to put hers there herself, and she made me promise not to say a word about the date to her, and she has been in the shop for ninety seconds and told me that, which is the fastest anybody has ever taken anything off me."

"Which means you've not got a room."

"I haven't got a room. I might have a room at nine o'clock on Tuesday morning and I will not know until I'm in it."

Reuben laughed once, which was not unfriendly.

"Right. Then I'll do it, and I'll do it on three conditions, and you'll write all three on the sheet and you'll give me that sheet in the Exchange, in the front, with the shutters up, and I'll read it out myself, because I've spent six weeks being the fourth thing in a market and I've had enough."

"Go."

"One. Nobody gives my name to the company, in writing or out of a room, and if somebody from a company asks the foreman about me the foreman can say whatever he likes, because I am not working for the company and the plate on a Saturday came off a van, not out of a building." He held up a finger. "Two. If I stop, the round stops, and the round stopping is a thing that happens to nineteen men and a man short on the pound, and I need to be able to stop without thinking about that first. So the stop has to cost me nothing on the day. Which means the thing has to be a Tuesday, and it can't be a Monday at ten past six, and it can't be anything in a shift, and if it turns out to be in a shift then I take my name off and I'll take it off on the floor and I'll say I'm doing it and you can't stop me."

"Agreed. And third."

"Third is the one you'll not like." He put both hands on the truck. "I've got no form. You know I have no form. Nobody who is any good at this has a form — the rail's got a rail and the yard's got a card bent at the corner and the market's got a book with names in it, and my thing is I stack empties on a truck on a Sunday and on a Saturday I carry two crates up a run, and that is the whole of what I am and I do it in about nine seconds and I have never once been asked to be watched. So if I'm on your sheet then somebody's going to have to say what my thing is, and I want to say it myself and I want it to be the only sentence in that room that has got a number in it, and I want it to be wrong on purpose, because if it is right then somebody has been teaching me and it isn't true."

He said the sentence, out loud, in the back of the Exchange on a Sunday afternoon in the autumn, and it took nine seconds, and it was three crates off a truck up a run with a hand truck, and Marek Senn wrote it down in Reuben Daw's own words on line three under the heading, and Reuben signed it, and dated it, and asked for the sheet to be left on the counter in the front with the shutters up.

He did it himself, at ten past four, with a market coming in and about thirty people in the room, and he read his own three conditions out loud in front of them, and the name of the college came out, and Marek Senn's name did not, and nobody in that room knew who had put the boy up to it except the boy, who had done it on purpose and who knew exactly what it had cost him and did not appear to be enjoying it.

Reuben Daw's Saturday rate did not come off. It came off on the Wednesday of week fourteen, on a telephone, in about four seconds, from a foreman who had a form and a company name and no idea at all what any of it was about.

Neither Reuben nor Marek Senn found out until the following week.

Talia Venn rang the bench at about ten past two on the Saturday afternoon, and she had no reason to and neither of them mentioned one.

"The Tuesday," she said. "The Bower Street thing. It's ten o'clock, it's a first-floor room, it's a man whose title is continuity and returns, and I have been twice now and I am going to be on time and I am going to be in a corridor with about eleven other people, three of whom I think work for the district and two of whom I think have been sent to make it look busy."

"What does it cost you."

"It costs me a day off the route and nineteen patients and two of those nineteen are the Thursday ones who cannot be moved and one of those two is a woman with a hip unit from the September open day." She said it like a list of tools. "And it costs me my own record, because the first thing that man will do is ask me for everything I have written since the eighth week, and about two thirds of it is other people's, and I am going to have to say so, and then somebody is going to ask me in what capacity I have been collecting it."

"In what capacity will you say."

"I haven't decided. That's a Tuesday problem and it's a Tuesday problem I have had for eight days." A pause. "Why are you asking me what it costs."

"Because I am trying to work out how much of it is mine," said Marek Senn.

Talia Venn was quiet for about three seconds, which was a length of quiet he had learned to read.

"Marek," she said. "It is not your Tuesday. Whatever it is, it is not yours, and I am not going to ask, and you are not going to tell me, and if you ever want to use my day off as an ingredient in a plan I will find out and I will be extremely unpleasant about it."

"Ten o'clock on the Tuesday," said Marek Senn.

"Yes. Ten o'clock. In Bower Street, on the first floor, with a man who writes things down." And then, in a completely different voice, flat and cold and about a third of an inch from where they had been: "You will be somewhere with your mouth shut for about four hours and there will be a person in the room who wants you to say a sentence."

"Yes."

"Right." She put the phone down.

He stood at the bench with a receiver in his hand for a while. He had set the date on Thursday afternoon in a letter with a reference number on it and the collision had been sitting on the calendar ever since, and he had told himself for three days that it was the department's fault for having a diary, and it was not, and she had just told him that she was going to be one floor above a public room she had no idea was there, and he was not going to tell her, and there was no leak, and there was no way in this city to tell her that was not a leak, and the reason he was not going to was not integrity.

It was that the moment he told her, the Tuesday stopped being a room he had walked into and started being a room she had arranged, and he had spent thirteen weeks learning that the only way he could ever get to the back of a room was to be the last man to arrive at anything.

He wrote that down, in the notebook, in the small tag hand, and did not soften it.

*She has told me the cost of her Tuesday in Bower Street and she has told me not to use it and I have not told her, and the reason I have not told her is that I wanted the room to be a room I walked into, and she would have made it a room she stood in the back of, out of habit, without meaning to, and I would have got there and found her at the back wall doing the thing I have been trying to learn to do since week nine, and I would have been relieved, and it would have cost her a thing she has not agreed to spend.*

*Rule three. One person, one thing, take the answer. I asked her a question I had already worked out the answer to, and I did it in order to hear her say a sentence that let me not tell her something. That is the rule and I broke it inside four seconds and I did not notice for about nine minutes. Write that in the book, in the tag hand, and do not write it in the good hand, and do not make it cleverer than it is.*
