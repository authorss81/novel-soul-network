# Chapter 47 — Somebody Who Was Never Taught the Card

The corridor outside room one of the practical building has a bench along one wall that is there for the purpose of waiting in, and there were eleven chairs and about forty people in the corridor by a quarter to eleven, and Marek Senn sat on the bench and was the fourth thing in every conversation in it and was in none of them.

That was new. He had spent thirteen weeks arranging to be the fourth thing, and he had got very good at it, and it had never once occurred to him that a room would arrive at it by itself, and that it would arrive at it and he would not be able to do anything about it, and that it would be a relief.

Anders Kolt, from the trust, came out of the room at eleven o'clock and stood at the end of the bench.

"You've got about nine minutes before you're called and I want to use eight of them and I'll stop when you tell me to."

"Go on."

"The determination on the word is going to go your way. I've read the requirement nine times since Friday and I made a note of it, which I don't do." He had the laptop shut under his arm. "A cue is not used when it isn't followed and isn't accepted, and four people in that room followed it eleven minutes before your window and one of them has spent the morning since feeling sick about it and one of them is a boy of twenty who will be frightened of a Tuesday for the rest of his life, and neither of those facts is in the window. That's not me being on your side. That's me telling you that the three hardest questions in the next three weeks are not going to be about the word."

"Go on."

"They're going to be about the four seconds and about the girl." He put the laptop down on the bench, which was not his. "I'm going to ask you, on the record, on Thursday, in a room, in front of the department and the trust and a man who is not fond of you: what happened in the four seconds when your line went and your mouth was open and the room did not hear you. And I am going to ask it in that order and not the other way round, and I am telling you the order now so that you cannot say I ambushed you, and I am telling you the order now because in about three years I will be the man who did not ambush you, and that is the only thing I have got to spend."

He picked the laptop up.

"Mr Arrick held your line open after the practice had gone out of it. That is going in the minute as a fact about a boy, and it is going to be read by about four hundred people, and in nine months one of them is going to write a paper about it, and the paper will be titled something about consent, and the boy will be twenty-one and consenting and not in a room." He looked at the floor. "I'm not asking you to agree with me. I'm asking you to think about it on a Sunday, in daylight, before Thursday, and not to tell me what you thought."

"Then I'll tell you what I thought on Thursday."

"That," said Anders Kolt, "is the first sentence anybody has said to me this term that makes me think this building is capable of anything."

Tam Arrick did his own release at eleven minutes past ten, outside the window, with the panel sitting, and he asked to be allowed to and did not explain why and was not asked.

It took about eleven seconds. He stood in the middle of the floor of room one with nothing in either hand, on his own feet, on a floor with a dead plywood board in it, and he said four things out loud in a flat voice with no shape on them at all.

"It was on me. It isn't on me now. Nothing about it stays. The next one is mine."

And then he put his right hand down at his side and he did not look at it and he did not close it, because it does not close, and there is a middle finger in it that has not moved since the week before the term began, and he stood there for the two or three seconds after the fourth thing with his hand open, and then he said, "That's it. It wasn't a technique. It's a thing I can do on my own hand and it's the only version of it I've got, and I'd like the panel to write down that the witness did not use a name, and that the witness said out loud on the day before he came here that it would not be one, and that it was not one."

Nobody in the room used a name.

Professor Onyeka wrote for a while and then looked up.

"The panel will minute that, and I am going to tell you now what the minute is worth, so that nobody in this room wastes the next four days working out what it is worth. It is worth a great deal as an account of an event. It is worth nothing to this panel, and I am going to say the reason out loud and I am going to say it now and not later in a paragraph, and Professor, would you put that in the same breath as the observation and not underneath it."

"Of course," said Professor Onyeka.

"The reason is that this panel has no power in respect of Mr Arrick. He is not a candidate. He holds no licence, he has never been assessed, he is not a student of this department, and he has a plate on a wrist somewhere in this city that this department is not going to be able to explain. The sentence this panel may act upon requires a *working non-hinged boundary release* and that means a practice, and a practice has a shape and a document and a review and somebody responsible for it, and a boy performing a boundary release on his own hand in a public room on a Tuesday morning is not that and cannot be made into it by this panel and would be an abuse of this panel if it tried." She put the pen down. "And I am going to minute that the room was full of witnesses, that the room was a public room, and that the performance was not a practice, and that the panel has no power, and that the panel thought, and I am prepared to be asked by anybody in this building what the panel thought."

The district woman of fifty said, after a moment, "The panel thought the opposite, Professor. I would like it minuted that two members thought the opposite and one of them has not spoken all morning and is not going to now."

"Noted," said Professor Onyeka, "and I am obliged to you, and it goes in the same paragraph."

Sera Quill and her crew took the plate out at twenty past ten.

It took four minutes and it took two of them, and it was done on the floor in a corridor outside a teaching room in a college by a woman of forty-five and a man of thirty in overalls with a bench in the room one, and Marek Senn watched it from the bench and was not allowed to help and was not asked to and did not offer, because there was nothing in that job that he was better at.

There is a puller for a wall plate. It is a flat steel tool about a foot long with a foot at one end and a handle at the other, and you set the foot under the plate and you lean on it, and a folded copper return contact is a thing that has been persuaded, once, with a load, and it will come out of a rack whole because the whole of it has set as one piece, and when it comes out it is the shape of the load. Not roughly. The shape. The line where the weight went through and the line where it stopped, pressed into copper, about four inches long, and thinner than a nail head at one end.

"God," said the district woman of fifty, and looked away, and wrote nothing.

"Right," said Sera Quill, and put it in a polythene bag, and sealed the bag, and wrote on the outside of the bag with a chinagraph: *7-4-112. Folded return. Plate out of rack 2, board 1, room one, college. 20/xx, 10:20. S. Quill.* She held it up and did not read it out.

"I don't read them out," she said, to the panel. "That's a service number and a wall and a shelf and a rack and a room and a time and a name, and if the number's wrong somebody rings the depot and says so, and that's the entire system and there's no part of it that anybody in a panel needs to hear. But the bag goes in a tray in a cage at our shop and it is locked and the number's on the tray, and that is the last time anybody in a college sees it. Not a department's store. Ours. Because a folded return is a maintenance object and the day it goes in a department's cage it becomes a fact about a thing that happened, and I am not having that."

"I have a form for a release of property," said Darla Vint, from a chair she had brought out into the corridor, "and I will need a signature and a reference number, because it is leaving a department's building."

"It's not leaving a department's building, it's leaving a college corridor."

"It is leaving a building the department has a hold on and the hold is in the deputy registrar's name, so yes, it is leaving a department's building, and no, I will not do it without a signature." Darla Vint had the form on her knee. "You will have to put a property reference on it and I will have to write a minute saying why a member of the public relay's staff was in a room at eight o'clock on a Tuesday, and I am going to have to write the minute whether you have a form or not, so you may as well have a form."

"Write the minute," said Sera Quill, "and put in it that a lead repairer and two of her crew attended a room one at eight o'clock at the request of the department to advise on a demonstration rig, and that they were there from eight o'clock and that they were not asked to be and that they were not paid and that they were in overalls, and if anybody in that minute finds that uncomfortable then the minute is correct."

Darla Vint wrote it, and it took four minutes, and Sera Quill read it and initialled it, and then said to Marek Senn, without any preamble at all, in a corridor, at a quarter to eleven:

"Two things in the cage, and I'm telling you because I don't have things on a list that I haven't told somebody about, and it's the third time this week I've told somebody. There's a brown envelope with a boy's name on it that came off a registrar's desk in week four, and there's now a bag with a plate in it off a wall in a college. They are in adjacent trays. If anybody comes and asks me for either of them I am going to hand them over, and I am going to tell them what is in the other tray, because the second one of those two objects is going to make somebody very interested in the first one, and I would rather it was me that said it than a form."

"Right," said Marek Senn.

"That's it. Don't thank me for the list, and don't you dare thank me for a list."

The panel rose at four minutes to twelve.

The determination on the word was nine minutes long and Professor Onyeka read it out in the corridor, in the open, with everybody standing, because the room had been a public room until the moment the panel rose and it was a corridor now, and she had made a point about that on the way in.

"Twenty-one minutes past nine: a member of the teaching staff of this department, who is not a member of this panel, spoke a word in the teaching shape at the front of the room. The panel has the evidence of two third-year note-takers, one of a second candidate, the panel's own observation, and the account of the candidate. The panel finds as to the sentence in the requirement of the ninth week, which is *no substituted cue used, followed or accepted within the scored window*, and it makes three findings and gives the reason for each.

"Used: not used. The candidate performed a boundary release. Nothing said by the member of staff formed any part of it, and the panel finds that the word at twenty-one minutes past nine formed no part of the release at forty-two minutes past nine.

"Followed: not followed within the window. Four people in the room moved into the posture of a person who has been told a load is coming. That movement happened at twenty-one minutes past nine and the panel's finding is that the four persons who moved did so eleven minutes before the window opened, and that the movement is a fact about the front of a room and not a fact about the candidate, and the panel records that one of the four has asked to be named in this determination and has not been named.

"Accepted: not accepted. Two words were offered to the candidate inside the window, by the other person named in the plan, and the candidate refused both of them in the room and in terms, and told the other person not to do it, and the panel finds that the refusal is the clearest evidence in the morning that no substituted cue was accepted, and the panel wishes it recorded that it would have been easier for the candidate, and better for the determination, to have accepted them."

Nobody in the corridor said anything.

"The panel further records," said Professor Onyeka, "four seconds in which the line was not held and in which a member of the public was injured, and records the evidence of the injured person, and records that the candidate's own account of those four seconds has not yet been given and will be taken at the next sitting. And the panel records that the assessment under the requirement of the ninth week was made and completed. And that is the whole of it, and I am going to say one more thing and then I am going to rise, and it is for the department and not for the panel.

"This panel cannot make anybody fit to practise. It is not a board and it is not an examination board and it has no power to grant, withhold or suspend anything, and what it has done this morning is put a record of an event in front of the department and the office, and the department will do whatever the department decides to do and the department's decision will be its own. Mr Kolt has been asking me, on the record and in front of nine people, what happens next, and the answer is that something happens in about four days in a room I am not in, and I have suggested to the deputy registrar that the panel will sit on Thursday of this week, and Professor Onyeka is me, and I have suggested it because the variance cannot sit on a Tuesday and lapse on a Friday, and because if somebody in this building wants to argue with me about the date they can come and argue with me about it now while I am still standing here."

Anneke Roos said, from the wall, "We will be there at ten, Professor, and we will bring the department's part, and the department has not had four days to prepare it and is not going to pretend it has."

"Nor should it," said Professor Onyeka.

Marek Senn had not been able to go into the corridor before half past eleven because a technician from the trust had wanted four photographs of the board and the rail and the rack, and then Hollis Pratt had wanted eleven minutes, and then the district woman of fifty had wanted a statement, and then Darla Vint had wanted him to sign a form about a bag, and then there was the account for the panel, which took nine minutes and was the fourth time he had given the account of the four seconds and the fourth time it had come out a different length.

She came up the stairs at twenty past eleven with her bag over her shoulder and her coat open and a folder, and she had been in Bower Street since ten o'clock, and she went past four people in the corridor without speaking to any of them, and then she stopped about eight feet from Marek Senn, and she looked down the corridor at a girl of nineteen with a cold pack on her forearm.

"That is Rhoda Ilesanmi," she said. "Tell me what happened to her arm, in one sentence, and don't give me the cause yet."
