# Chapter 37 — A Key-holder Without a Certificate

Rafi Pell was eating a slice of toast over the sink at seven in the morning in the room above the laundromat and he had a courier bag on one chair and a sum on the counter and eleven minutes before the van.

"One," he said out loud. "Two."

"You don't have to do that in here."

"I know I don't have to do that in here," said Rafi Pell. "That's the whole of the habit, mate, you don't get an exemption for the kitchen." He turned round. "Go on, then. You've been standing in the middle of that room since Sunday like a man waiting for a bus that isn't coming."

Marek Senn sat down on the end of the bath, which he was not supposed to do, and said it in about ninety words, and did not make it a speech, and did not use the word *hinge* because Rafi had a courier's ear and would have heard it as a name.

Rafi Pell listened with his mouth shut and then held up two fingers to nobody.

"Right," he said. "Two things, and then I've got to go, and I want them both said in this order because the first one's about you and the second one's about me and I have thought about the second one."

"Go."

"One. That is a key-holder. That's the whole of it, and I'm not being kind, and I'd say it to a fella in a market." Rafi put the toast down. "You haven't got a certificate in it. That's not a small thing about you, that's the whole problem, and it's worse than mine and worse than Rusk's and worse than the woman in Nine Bridges, because she's got a room and she's got nineteen years and she's got a reason in writing, and Rusk's got a department and a card in his own name from about 2004. You have got a bank. You've stood on a bank for half an hour and a woman on the steps has changed how she comes down them and there is now no possible act in the world that takes that off you, because there is nothing to be struck off, mate. There's no register. There's no box. If you'd had a licence I could have said, you've lost it."

"And if I'd had one I would have lost it in a hearing and they would have written *exhibited a key-holder's cue in a public field* and there would be a record and I would have had to live in the same city as that record, and this is better," said Marek Senn. "That is the argument for it and it took me a whole day to work out and it is a lie I told myself, and I'm telling you it's a lie because you are going to think it in about a fortnight and I would rather you'd heard me say it."

"Two," said Rafi Pell, and he was at the door with the bag on his shoulder, and he was not going to be late, and he said it in the doorway the way he said everything, which is like putting a coin down. "You can't hand it back."

"There's nothing to hand back."

"Exactly what I said." The door was open. "You keep going on about handing things over — the room, the sheet, the work, the thing on the bank — and you can't hand over this one, because there's no job to resign from. There's no manager. There's nobody who can say *that's been stopped*. So the only thing you've actually got to give away is the work, and the work isn't the thing, and if you give the work away you're just a man who used to stand on a bank. That's worse. That's what I'm saying to you and I want it on the page."

He went.

---

He worked out what he had become forty minutes later, at the bench at Civic Spine 7, at about a quarter to eight in the morning, with a relay in his hand that he had been holding for six minutes without looking at.

He put it down. He read the values on it. He picked it up. He put it down again.

Then he went and did the honest thing, which was to stop working, and to take his notebook out, and to write one line, and the line was:

*I have a certificate in nothing. I have no licence, no assessment, no room, no list, no wage, no employer, and I am not anybody's responsibility, and I have been the fourth thing in three working buildings in a fortnight and I did it by standing on a bank and breathing, and there is no form on which a man can be recorded as having done it, which is the reason it worked.*

And then a second line, which is this:

*Rule three says one person, one thing, and take the answer. I asked one person one thing on Friday, one on Sunday, and one of them at seven o'clock this morning, and I have already worked out that I am going to ask two more before one o'clock this afternoon, and the rule is not about information, and I am writing the intention down rather than the act because I have noticed that I only ever break this one in a hurry to get to somebody else.*

---

He went up the hill at ten past ten and Corvin Ashby was on the tailboard again with a hood up, and the tea flask came out, and Marek Senn said, "I have to tell Nkechi Osei something and I would like you not to be in the room for it," and Corvin Ashby said, "I'll be in the pump house and I'll leave the door shut, and I want you to know that I am doing that because I want to know about it afterwards, and not because I trust you."

Nkechi Osei was on the pound at the far end of the pull-off, coiling the orange cord, thirty-three, eleven years on the round and twenty-nine in the work.

"I'm going to tell you what I did on Monday afternoon and then I'm going to ask you a question and I do not have an answer to it, and I am telling you that I have not got an answer at the start so that you do not spend the next ten minutes being polite to me."

"Say it."

"I came up here at one o'clock because a man rang a number I put on a wall and I could not not come, and I stood on the grass bank above the lock for about half an hour and watched you go down those steps eleven times, and I did not make a sound, and on about the twelfth one you started going down on four."

Nkechi Osei put the cord down in a heap.

"Right," she said.

"You've been on three since about a fortnight before I came to this city."

"A fortnight before you came up the hill," Nkechi Osei said. "Not the city." She looked at him. "And you want to know how to get it back."

"Yes."

"I don't," said Nkechi Osei. "That is the answer you were expecting and it isn't the one I've got. I've had two days with it. What I've got is that I can feel where the four is, and I could have found it, and I am going to have to decide every single time now, on a wet step, in the dark, with a bar in my hands, and that's four hundred more decisions than I used to have." She picked the cord up again. "And I am not frightened of you. I want to say that before anything else, because I can see it. I am frightened of the Tuesday, when you won't be there and the four'll still be in me, and there is nothing anybody can do about the Tuesday."

"I'm sorry."

"Don't be sorry, be useful," said Nkechi Osei. "Somebody in this city has got a way of taking it out. That's what I want. Not today, I can't do it today, I have got a bar and a set of steps on the Monday of week fourteen. Somebody. In a week. In a room, with a bit of paper, and somebody in the room who can say no, because I have not got anybody in this ward who can say no to me and I have not got anybody anywhere who can say no to me, and that is the actual state of my life, and I am not being dramatic about it, I am telling you because you asked me a question."

"I'll find somebody."

"You'll find somebody," said Nkechi Osei, "and you will have to be in the room, because everything you have told me about this for three days says that a room with you in it is a room where you're the thing being said, and if somebody finds somebody and you turn up then it's a bank again, and I'll be on four in a fortnight and you'll be the reason."

She said it without any heat. She said it the way you say a thing you have worked out on your own over two days and have already made your peace with, and there was nothing at all in her face while she said it, and then she went back to coiling the cord and got about four turns of it done before she spoke again.

He took it standing in a pull-off off Fell Road with his bag over his shoulder, and the taking of it was the most expensive four seconds of his term, and he wrote it up in the notebook on the bus down the hill in one line: *She is right and I have no answer and I am not going to make one up today.*

---

He telephoned Asha Reed at one o'clock from the bench and she took it on the second ring, which meant she had been waiting by a telephone, which he decided not to think about.

"You've come to hand me something," said Asha Reed.

"I've come to hand you three things and to be told no to one of them."

"Go."

"One. The case. Eleven first-years in a converted store room on a Monday is not teaching anybody and if you do it again in March I will not come, and I am not the man you need, and Toby is a better man than me for that room and I have written his name down as well as yours and I would like you to know I have written his name down on purpose, because you built a room out of yourself in three days and I have watched that happen twice in a term and both times it was a woman."

"Right. And you have said the thing that I have been frightened of since Monday," said Asha Reed, "and I have got a price for it, and the price is that on the first Wednesday of every month from now until the end of the year, if a first-year wants to know about the fourth beat, I say that the case is Senn's and the technique is nobody's yet, and I say it in that room, and I say it about a person who is not me and has not asked me to and will not be in the room. And in about a year that makes me the woman who says the sentence about you, and I will be twenty and I will be a name in a corridor. I have priced it and I am buying it."

"Two," said Marek Senn. "The Exchange book. Bea Nunn will give anybody the sheet if they ask in the room, but nobody has ever asked, because nobody knows it is there. It is a lined book with four columns and about thirty lines in it and it does not have a name on it and it is the only document anybody in this case has ever made that does not make anybody findable. You go and ask for it in the room, out loud, in front of nine people, and then you get it into a common room so that a first-year knows it exists. Not the lines. The fact that it exists."

"That's cheap and you're right, and I'll do it on Wednesday because market day, and Marda will be at the table and she will say something unkind about it, and she will be right."

"Three. Tam Arrick's plan. He has written four columns on the back of a kitchen order form and column four is who watches and what they are allowed to say, and the first line of it is somebody who was never taught the card, and the second line is *and I want to know they said no*. He will be a first-year on that maintenance track in about four months' time and he will be told nothing and taught everything and he will be given a booklet with no name on it." Marek Senn put his hand over the receiver for a second and then took it off again. "I am not asking you to read it. I am asking you to know that it exists, and I am asking you to let me tell him you know, and I am asking you to decide for yourself what to do with that, and if you do nothing then I will have told you a boy's private plan and you will have to work out what that means."

There was a pause of about four seconds on the line.

"He is a third-year," said Asha Reed.

"He is a third-year and he could not put weight through his hand until a Friday four days ago and he cannot hold anything heavy for three months, and he is the only person in this city who has done a release without a substituted cue in front of a witness, and the witness said in public that it was not a release, and neither of them wants anything to do with a room with me in it."

"Right," said Asha Reed. "I will do nothing, and I will know, and you will tell him I know, and in four months' time when somebody in this building is about to stand in front of a first-year intake and say the fourth beat is a support beat, I will be a voice in a corridor in a cold corridor and it will not be you and it will not be me. That's the job. That's cheaper than a demonstration and much nastier and I am doing it."

"And the fourth one," said Marek Senn.

"What fourth one."

"You know what the fourth one is," he said, "and I have not got a name for her, and that is the one I am actually telephoning about, and I do not know how to do it, and I have been sitting on this bench for twenty minutes about it."

---

He did not sleep much on the Tuesday night of week 13.

He got up at two and wrote three things on a fault form and pinned none of them to anything, and the third of them was:

*There are four. Nkechi Osei, who has a bar and a set of steps and a Tuesday. Corvin Ashby, who has a book nine years old and a letter on a door and now my handwriting in a book in a market. Asha Reed, who has eleven people and a price. And a woman of sixty-eight who works at the counter of the shop I sleep above, who asked me on Sunday to look at her wrist and to write it down, and who has told me that every man who says he is not the man who comes round ends up being the man who comes round, and who is right, four times out of four.*

*I have handed three of them something to hold. The fourth one I have not got a name for and I have not got a person, and I am not going to invent one, and I have been on this bench since ten o'clock trying to work out who in this city it would be correct to give a woman's wrist to, and there is nobody, and there are about four people who would each be a worse answer than me, and the correct answer is a person whose job it is, and there is no such person in this city, and I have known that since the fourth week of term and I have spent nine weeks being angry about it instead of writing it down.*

*And writing a sentence down is the only part of any of this I am actually good at, and I have got very good at it, and it is the same thing as the bank and the forty minutes on a telephone, and I do not think that is a discovery.*
