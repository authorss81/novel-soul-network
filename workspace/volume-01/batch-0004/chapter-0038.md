# Chapter 38 — It Does Not Work

The telephone at Civic Spine 7 rang at five past six on the Wednesday morning of week 13 and Marek Senn answered it before Sera Quill came in, and it was a woman he had never spoken to in his life.

It took nineteen minutes.

She was sixty-one. She was in Nine Bridges, in a first-floor flat over a takeaway, and she had a hip unit that had been fitted on the September open day by a man out of a van, and she had had it since September, and she had read the number on the noticeboard on Monday and had not rung it until Wednesday morning at six because she did not want to ring it in front of her husband.

"Right," she said. "I've read it about six times and I want to say the thing first so you don't have to work round it. I get short of breath. Not a lot. About twice since I had it put on, and both times it was about four in the afternoon and both times it went away when I sat down."

Marek Senn sat down on the bench.

"How long does it take to go away."

"Ten minutes. Maybe a quarter of an hour." She had the noticeboard sheet in front of her and he could hear the paper. "I haven't told anybody. I haven't told my husband, I haven't told my daughter, and I have not been to a doctor, and I want you to understand that I have not rung you because I'm frightened of a plate, I have had this plate since September and it has been wonderful, I can get down my stairs now. I've rung you because there's a number on a wall and it says not a fault and I would like somebody to tell me whether that's a lie."

"It is not a lie about the service bulletin. I know the bulletin. It is a true sheet and it was issued before anybody had anything to claim, and I have got a copy."

"Then what is it."

"I can't tell you," said Marek Senn. "And I'm going to say the reason rather than say it in a way that sounds like a department, and the reason is that I am a third-year with no licence, and everything I have got on the shape of that plate is a piece of paper, and if I tell you about the paper then I have told you a thing you cannot do anything about. And here is the other thing and I would rather you had it. You rang the number and got a company in four minutes and the company was brisk with you. Then somebody in a market told you the man who wrote the number up works in a relay shop on the corner. That is how you got me, and that is how everybody is going to get me, and it is because there is not one name in this city on that piece of paper except the number, and I wrote it that way on purpose."

He heard her breathe out.

"Right," she said. "Then tell me the other thing. Not the bulletin. The other thing."

"You take it off."

"I can't take it off. It took two men to put it on and I live in a first-floor flat." She was not complaining. "That's not me being difficult, that's me telling you what I've got. What I want to know is whether I should be frightened."

And Marek Senn — twenty-two, third year, no licence, night repairer, on a bench in a public relay shop at ten past six in the morning with a supervisor due in at seven — said:

"No. Not from the plate. Not from a plate worn since September, not from a bus, not from going up and down your stairs, and not from twice at four in the afternoon sitting down and it going away in ten minutes. That is the shape of a thing I have seen twice in this case in a fish market and in a clinic, and both of those times it was a hip, and both of those times it was a nurse who was watching, and it went away. What you should be frightened of is being alone with it at four in the afternoon in the middle of a winter, and the answer to that is not a plate, it's a telephone number, and it's a bad one, and it's on a wall in the Exchange, and you ring it, and the woman on that till writes down what she was told and hands it to me on a Wednesday, and I ring you back from a shop."

"And you? Will you come?"

"I can't promise you that," said Marek Senn, "and I want to be careful, because I am not the man who comes round, and I have said that four times this week to people who are right, and the honest position is that I have got no way of promising a thing like that and if I promise it you will wait for me, and waiting for a man is the problem."

She was quiet for a bit.

"Right," she said. "Then that's a no, and I'll take a no, because a no's better than a maybe. My name's Delia. Delia Frost. I live over the takeaway on Carnation Row and I've written it down and I'm going to write it on the sheet in my hand and give that sheet back to the woman at the counter, because that's what the sheet's for, isn't it. That's what she said. Whoever takes the call."

"That's exactly what she said."

"Delia Frost. Hip unit. Eleven weeks. Get short of breath twice, about four in the afternoon, and it goes. And I want it written down that I told somebody and I didn't want to and my husband doesn't know." A pause. "And can I ask you one thing. Are you the one who wrote the number up?"

"Yes."

"Then don't come," said Delia Frost. "Not because I don't want you to. Because in a year's time you're going to be a name and I want to be the one who rang a number off a wall, and those aren't the same, and I can tell which one I am already."

They said goodbye and Marek Senn sat on the bench at a repair shop in Lattice Ward and wrote the time on the day book, 06:24, and put nothing else anywhere, and then did not move for about three minutes.

Then he wrote on a scrap, under the bench, with a pencil:

*Nineteen minutes. I have never done anything better. I have never been more useful. It was on a telephone at six in the morning and it was nineteen minutes and it had nothing to do with a demonstration and it is not a technique and I have not improved anything for anybody and I would do it again at six tomorrow.*

*And Delia Frost has just become the reason I cannot hand this over, because a man cannot hand over the job of being the person a street rings. You cannot delegate a telephone. And the only way it stops is for a person whose job it is to exist in this city and say the thing I said, and there is not one, and I said the sentence about there not being one on Monday in a corridor to a first-year and he wrote my name down as a reason.*

*Rule three. I asked one person one thing at six in the morning. That's still one. It is going to stay one. That is not a rule any more, that is a man who cannot help himself, and the difference between a rule and that is that I could break the rule.*

---

He was at the Saltmarket Exchange at twenty past eleven on the Wednesday of week 13, at the back, on market day, with eleven people on the floor and nine of them at the Choir's trestle table, and Asha Reed asked Bea Nunn for the book in a room.

She did not look at him once, and he was nine feet away, and that was correct.

"Right," said Asha Reed. "I'm asking for the book. The one on the noticeboard, the four columns. I want it on this table, on a Wednesday, and I want anybody in this room who has ever rung a number off a wall to know that it is a real thing with real lines in it and it isn't a rumour."

"Ask properly," said Bea Nunn. "In front of everybody, and say who you are and what you're doing it for, and if you say something about a case I'll say no."

"I'm not asking for a case. I'm asking for a sheet of paper." Asha Reed did not raise her voice. "I'm a first-year and I'm a member of the market clean-up rota and I'm asking on my own account and not for anybody, and if it turns into a case somebody can take it off me, and I'd rather that happened today than in six months."

Marda Szabo said, from the table, without looking up from a lineage sheet, "No."

She did not say it unkindly. She said it the way you say a thing you have thought about properly, and then she did say the reason, which is what the Choir does, and the reason took about forty seconds and it was the best forty seconds of argument in the batch.

"There is a rule in this co-operative that everything about a person, published by them, is published by name," said Marda Szabo. "There are two hundred and twelve of us and nine of us are at this table and every one of us has been named in that book at some point because we put ourselves in it. That is the trade. You write your name, you get the corrections, you get the corrections read out to nine people in a market on a Wednesday, including the ones that are against you." She put the sheet down. "And that book has thirty-odd lines in it and most of them are one line and a name and a number, and you want it on our table, in a room, where nine of us and a market of about two hundred people walk past. And there is not one of those names in that book that has not got a plate on, or had, or is waiting for somebody to come. You are asking us to stand in front of the only document in this city that does not make anybody findable, and to say *look, everybody, here it is*, and in about four months a district office or a company or a man in a very good coat is going to ask us who told us to do it, and I will be the one who has to answer, and I am fifty-one and I do not need to be the one who answers that."

"Nobody said my name."

"You didn't say it," said Marda Szabo. "You said *a woman I know*. You are one year in a college and you have been at this market since you were a child, and every one of us can see it is somebody, and Marda Szabo is not a fool and neither is Ekow." Ekow Nartey, who had the request book, said, "I'll enter it as a request if you want, and the request goes in full, and the ground is public-safety or it is five pounds out of a tin with four pounds in it, and if it's public-safety then the request gets read out and your name is on it and you get named in the quarterly if it's upheld. That's the rule and it's the rule in both directions."

"Then it's a no," said Asha Reed, after about four seconds.

"It's a no to the table," said Marda Szabo. "It's a yes to the table from me in about a month, when it is printed and it is out of anybody's hands, and then it is a fact and not a favour, and it will have got about forty more names in it by then, and it will be a page of a quarterly and anybody with nine pounds can buy the quarterly, and I have been waiting two years to be able to say that sentence to a person of nineteen."

Somebody at the table said, "That's a yes, then."

"It's a no today," said Marda Szabo, "and I have taught her what a no costs, which is the only thing this table has ever been for."

Corvin Ashby refused it at the pump house at half past two on the Wednesday afternoon, and his refusal was better than anybody else's in the week, and it cost him the round.

"No," he said.

"You've not heard it."

"I've heard it. You stood on a bank on Monday and my best pair of hands changed how she comes down two steps and now you want to give it to me." He did not put the tea flask down. "I'm not having it. Here's the three reasons and I'll do them in order because the second one is the one that matters."

"Go on."

"One. I don't know what it is. You've told me what a hinge is in about nine sentences over four days and I've understood about six of them and the six of them are enough for me to know that a man in a room who is the fourth thing is a bad thing to have in a ward of nineteen men. If you hand me a job about it, then I've got a job about it, and a job about it is a thing I'm accountable for, and on the Monday morning of a flood round I have got nineteen men and a bar and I cannot be accountable for a thing I do not understand."

"Two."

"Two." Corvin Ashby looked at the floor of the pull-off. "I've been a key-holder in this ward for nine years and I've known it the whole time and I've never once been asked about it, and the reason nobody's asked me is that nobody ever comes up here. You are the first person in nine years to come up here, and you have been on this hill six days running, and you have already had a woman on my round change how she comes down steps." He put the flask back in his pocket. "So if you give me this, then in six months the men will come to me about *the thing the Weir man knows*, and I will be able to do it, because I will have been given it, and I will be exactly what the woman in Nine Bridges worked out she was over a cup of tea, and I have watched her work that out and I thought she was being dramatic about it, and she was not."

Marek Senn stood in a pull-off and found that the argument was not a no he had to take but a no that improved his own position, and that he was not going to be able to say so out loud without sounding like a man enjoying it.

"That's two," said Corvin Ashby. "Three, and three's the one I'll not move on. You've asked me to hand the work to three people and a laundrette, and two of the three are better at it than you and one of them has a set of steps in five days. Fine. Fine. But every one of you is going to be doing it because a man in Lattice said so, and the second the man in Lattice stops — and he will stop, because he's got a panel on Thursday and a term's over and a job to go back to — then it's the four of you and a rail. And a rail on its own is what she had before, and she'll be on four and there'll be nobody to say no to her in this ward, and she'll have a poster about it in a first-year common room and a page in a quarterly and no person in it."

"And if I keep going up the hill."

"Then she'll be on four because you're a bank," said Corvin Ashby, "and in a year you'll be on the ninth ward, and there's eleven of them, and you'll have a car, and in about four years you will have a card of your own with a reference number on it, and there'll be a horn."

The last bus down the hill went at six and he was on it.

---

He went to the laundrette at twenty past seven on the Wednesday evening and Enid Sarkis was on the till with a cardigan on and a hotplate and the dryers going.

"I've come to tell you that I can't do the fourth one."

"You've come to tell me you can't do the fourth one at twenty past seven on a Wednesday, in person, rather than put it in a letter, and I'll say that's a decent way to do it." She turned the plate. "So who is it. Who would I ring about my wrist if I couldn't ring you."

"That's the whole of it. There's nobody. That's not a way of putting it, that's the fact."

"Right," said Enid Sarkis. "Then you'll have to keep coming, won't you."

Marek Senn did not answer that, because there was no answer to it that was not either a lie or a resignation.

"Nineteen months," she said. "Four men. You know what the difference is between you and the four, love? The four came round in a van. You're going to come round on a bus, and it isn't going to be for the company, and it isn't going to be for the form, and you are going to be a man who is difficult to be angry at, and in about two years that is going to be the most powerful thing about you and you are not going to have noticed it happening."

He got his washing and went up the stairs.

---

He got to the Weir at about nine and the pump house light was on, and Nkechi Osei was on the steps, alone, working the last two in the dark with a head-torch, and he stood on the bank and did not go down and watched eleven of them.

She was still on four.
