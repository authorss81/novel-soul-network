# Chapter 31 — Four Minutes

The monthly meeting of the Saltmarket Exchange began at ten with a piece of paper, and the piece of paper was about ice.

There were about thirty people in the back room, which was a former ferry waiting room with the shelving taken out and eleven trestle tables borrowed from the front, and the roof leaked over the fourth one, and there was a tea urn on a table by the door that had been on since quarter to. The chair was Bea Nunn, who was sixty-one and sold ice, and who had chaired this meeting for nineteen years and had never once been late for it and had never once let it run past eleven.

Marek Senn was at the back. He had no vote and no office and had not asked for either. He had been in the room since half past nine, standing by the door with his hands in his pockets because there was nowhere to sit that was not somebody's table.

The ice went for eleven minutes. The rate for the crushed went up a halfpence and came back down. The roof was discussed. A man from the west side wanted to know about the fish line and was told, kindly, that the fish line was not the Exchange's business, and he said it was his business, and there were about ninety seconds of that, and then Bea Nunn moved on.

At nine minutes, Bea Nunn read out an item off a card.

"Motion to suspend standing orders." She had a hand on the card. "Anybody. Card in the tin by eight o'clock. Name on it, anybody, doesn't have to be a member."

Nobody moved for a while.

Then Marek Senn put his hand up, and thirty people looked at him, and he thought: *right, so that's the whole of it, then.*

He got four minutes. He counted them, because he was that sort of person now.

"I've got four minutes because I said this is important," he said, "and I want to start by saying I know what that is worth in this room and I'm not going to pretend it isn't. I've got no vote and no office and I'm not a member. My name is Marek Senn, I'm a third-year, I haven't got a licence, and there's a sheet on the noticeboard out there with a service bulletin number on it and nothing else on it."

Somebody said, "We know, that's the cat's got a number on it."

"It's not the cat's," said Marek Senn. "It's a service number. It came off a bundle label. It's not a fault, it's not a recall, and it's not a claim, and a company will tell you what it means if you ask them, and they don't have to. Two people have rung it since Tuesday morning. That's all I wanted to say, and I want to say one thing and then I'll sit down."

He had about two minutes and forty seconds left and he used all of them, which he thought about afterwards.

"Two of those calls were people who did not know anybody here and did not know me. That's the only reason the number's on the wall instead of a name. If somebody in here rings that number and the person on the other end is brisk with you, that's the number working. If somebody tells you it's fine and there's nothing to worry about, write down who told you that. Not to catch anybody. Because in about a fortnight somebody's going to want to know who was told what, and the only person who can answer that is whoever wrote it down at the time, and that person is going to be a fishmonger, not me."

He sat down.

The chair said, "Noted," in the tone of a woman noting a change in the weather, and looked at the fourth trestle table, and asked whether anybody had anything else.

Nobody had anything else. The meeting moved to the floor.

Marek Senn sat at the back with his hands in his pockets and listened to two minutes and fifty seconds of nothing, and it was, as far as he could tell afterwards, the correct use of four minutes he had ever made.

---

He got up to leave at about ten twenty and the room did not let him, the way a room does not let anybody.

That was the thing he had not planned and could not have planned. The meeting ended at eleven and then eleven became half past and then half past became a quarter to twelve, and people stayed, and the ice table became a tea table, and there were about fifteen people in four conversations and Marek Senn stood in the middle of a former ferry waiting room with nowhere to be, and for the first time in eleven weeks he was not the reason anything was happening, and he could hear all of it.

The argument by the tea urn was about a wrist.

"— got it off a table," said a woman he did not know, dark-haired, forty-ish, with a bag of fish scales on the floor by her feet. "In a car park. In September. With a form."

"Errol's not daft," said another one, older, in a cardigan that had been mended at both cuffs. "Errol's got a bad wrist and it's the only thing that's ever helped it. What are you going to do, take it off him and let him do the round with nothing?"

"He shouldn't be on the round at all."

"He's been on the round every year for eleven years."

"That's not what I said and you know it isn't."

"I do know it isn't. I'm saying he's got a plate with no name on it and no doctor on it and whoever made it has put a line on the label and not a check underneath it, and that's the whole of my point, and it came from your side of the water—"

"My side of the water is not—" The older woman put her cup down. "Whose side of the water is anything."

"That's Errol Nkemdi," said a third voice, a man with a ledger under his arm. "Errol Nkemdi, out of the Ferry Road terraces. Sixty-one? Fifty-eight?"

"Fifty-eight," said the dark-haired woman.

"And he got a wrist unit off that open day in September, the one where they had the tables out in the car park by the Institute, and everybody I know from the terraces went, and it was free, and it was that on the form—" she waved. "A dash. Where your name goes. He showed it to me and he said, that's what that is, that's what it means."

"And who came round with the tables?"

"Young men with a van." She said it flatly. "Same as the man who came round with the cat."

The man with the ledger laughed, and the older woman did not, and Marek Senn was in the middle of the floor of the Saltmarket Exchange at about twenty past eleven on the Wednesday morning of week 12 with an entire case in his coat pocket and no way to say one word of it that would be of any use whatsoever to anybody standing within four feet of him.

Then the dark-haired woman said the other thing, and she said it to the tea urn.

"And if you do want to talk to anybody about the round, go up the hill. Corvin's got it. Corvin's had it nine years and he's never once written a name in that book, and he's the only reason anybody up there can do anything at all, and the district has been at him since the autumn—" she turned to the man with the ledger. "When did that letter come."

"Twice. Once in the autumn and once round the new year."

"And what's in it."

"Written practice." The man with the ledger put the ledger down on the table, which people in that room did not do. "It wants four things off anybody who's running a shared load release in the flood season. Who wrote it, what it touches, how a person stops it, and how many people you've put through it. In writing. On their form. With a date."

"By when."

"End of the third week of the flood season."

Marek Senn knew, without moving his head, that the flood season's first round was the Monday of week 14, and that the tide table fixed it, and that it did not move.

"Corvin can't write any of that," said the older woman. "Corvin could not write a shopping list. Corvin's book is nine years of things nobody can read and if he wrote his name on a form that's the end of the pump money for the Low Wards and the next one is a photograph in a district magazine with our name under it."

"Corvin's refusing the district, isn't he."

"Corvin's refusing everybody, dear, it's the same hand he writes with."

---

Nobody knew who he was. He found that out by standing there for another ten minutes.

He was not a repairer from Lattice. He was not a man from a college. He was a person standing near the tea urn at the back of a room, and four separate conversations went past him and not through him, and the most useful thing that happened to him in a term happened in those four minutes and the ten after them, and not one word of it came out of his mouth.

He did not arrange any of it. He had not asked a single person a single question. He had been ignored for four minutes, the room had gone back to its own business, and its own business was the case, which is the only way anything has ever come to him that he was able to keep.

---

The tin by the door was for motions, and cards went in it before eight, and there was a second card in it that morning from a person who was not a member and had put her age on the front of it, which is not required and which got a laugh.

Asha Reed was nineteen. She was in her first year at the Institute, on a maintenance-track course that ran two days a week in a building she did not like, and she had been coming to the Saltmarket Exchange on and off since she was fifteen because her mother's business had a standing arrangement with two of the trestle tables and because it was the cheapest warm room in Nine Bridges that would have a person of her age in it.

She had put her card in the tin on the Monday night, in her own kitchen, with the table cleared, because her mother's business opens at six and the tin is at the Saltmarket and she was not going to walk it down there in the dark with her name on the front of it.

"Motion," she said, when Bea Nunn read the card out. "That the Exchange keeps a sheet. For the number. Whoever rings it, what's on the card, what they were told, and who took the call. And that the sheet stays in this building and it is not the man who wrote the number on the board."

"That's four things and a place," said Bea Nunn.

"It's four things and a place," said Asha Reed. "And I'm not asking for a book and I'm not asking for a name in a ledger and I'm not asking anybody to do anything to anybody. I'm asking for a piece of paper with four lines on it, and if the sheet's in this room then nobody can take it out of this room, and that's the only argument I've got and it's a good one, because I've watched a document go out of a room this term and I wasn't in the room when it went."

Nobody asked her what document. That was the part that stayed with Marek Senn for the rest of the day and most of the night.

Bea Nunn looked at the card for a while.

"We're not a registry," she said.

"I know," said Asha Reed. "That's why I'm asking the room instead of asking a department."

"Is anybody against."

Nobody was against. It went in about ninety seconds, which was faster than the ice had gone.

Then Bea Nunn took a sheet of the Exchange's own headed paper out of a drawer and wrote the four column headings on it, and pinned it to the noticeboard a month out of date to the right of his six lines, and the first entry on it was made at ten to twelve by the woman chairing the meeting, out of a call she had taken at the counter on the way in from a woman of sixty-eight who had come in through the front, and who had come in through the front on purpose.

Marek Senn was not close enough to see what she wrote and did not go to look, which was the only discipline he performed that day and the reason he slept.

Nobody had written Marek Senn's name anywhere in it. He checked twice, in two places, and then felt the whole shape of the thing arrive in his chest with a weight he had not been ready for: he had wanted to be the first name on a list for eleven weeks, and a fishmonger had taken a piece of paper and a rubber band and had done in ninety seconds the thing he had been unable to arrange in a term, and had put her own name at the top of the first column, and had made herself the first name in the sequence.

---

He read it on the Friday morning, at about eleven, standing at the noticeboard in a former ferry waiting room with his bag over his shoulder and a bus in four minutes, and the first line was in a hand that leaned forwards and said:

*Wed. Wrist, 3 yrs. No name given. Said she'd had it since before the lad upstairs moved in. Told her: the number is a service number, it is not a fault, and she can ask them what it means and they needn't answer. Nobody to follow up. — B. N.*

And the second line was in a small engineer's hand and was dated the Friday, and it was the second line because the woman had rung at about a quarter to twelve on the Wednesday on her way in from the front, and he had rung at about a quarter to eleven on the Friday and had not thought about it either:

*Fri. Telephoned. I am the service engineer on that bulletin in this district. My name is P. Vance and I have got a service record and it is correct. What I am ringing about is the wall, and I am ringing because I said on the phone to a man on Tuesday what the wall costs, and I want it on your paper that I know the cost and I am not asking you to take it down. Tell whoever put the number up that a number is a reference until somebody with a sequence is looking for it. — P. Vance*

Petar Vance had put his own name on it, in his own hand, on a Friday, at a counter in a former ferry waiting room, in a column he had not made and had not asked to be part of.

That was the most useful thing that had happened to him in a term, and it had happened to somebody else.
