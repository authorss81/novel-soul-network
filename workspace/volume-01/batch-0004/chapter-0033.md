# Chapter 33 — Ninety Seconds

Tam Arrick's weight date was on the Friday morning of week 12, which was the last Friday of the term, and he went to it on his own.

He got the bus out to Nine Bridges at ten past eight and sat in a corridor off the Ninth Street for about fifty minutes with a magazine he had already read and a plastic bag with a clean shirt in it that he had brought for no reason he was ever going to explain, and at about nine, without hurrying him, a woman of about sixty called his name off a list.

Mrs Ollerton had a collar on, because it was still cold in that corridor in the last week of the term, and she spelled her name on the wall chart since 1988 and had a bad hand herself, and she did not say the word *normal* about the middle finger and did not write anything on it.

"Right," she said. "Two kilos. Off the floor, on to the counter, with the other hand underneath if you want it under there. And then you put it down again however you want to put it down, and I'm not going to tell you how."

Tam Arrick got a two-kilo box of writing paper off the floor of an office at the end of a corridor off the Ninth Street, and he carried it eleven feet, and he put it on a counter, and his right forearm came up under it the way a forearm does when the hand has not been asked anything, and nothing at all happened.

He stood there.

"All right," said Mrs Ollerton. "Now the elbow."

"It did that by itself."

"I know it did that by itself," she said. "That is not the same as it happening on purpose and it is not the same as it happening every time, and I am going to write down that it happened, and I am not going to write down that you can do it, because you cannot do it. You did it once, this morning, in front of me, and I am going to tell you what I am allowed to tell you, which is what the registrar told me on the Friday, which is that two of those tendons are holding and one of them is not, and the other two fingers are promised to you and the middle one is not, and I will know more in a fortnight than anybody knows now and I will not pretend to know more than I know."

"How long."

"Six weeks," said Tam Arrick. "That is what I have written. That is my figure and I put it down in week eight and I have not changed it since and I am not going to change it now because you are about to tell me something different."

She looked at him.

"Three months before anything heavy," she said, "from the Friday you were in theatre, and not one day less, and that is the registrar's, and the registrar's is the one with the name on it. And you are not going to hold a kettle and you are not going to hold a pan and you are not going to hold a wagon. You'll talk me out of it and I will not be talked, and I have got a card here with a telephone number on it and you are going to lose it and I am going to lose it and one of us is going to ring the other in about nine weeks because the number is on the card and not in your head."

She gave him the card.

He put the card in the left pocket of his jacket, and then he went and sat on a bench in a corridor off the Ninth Street for a while, and then he took the supply order form out of his rucksack — the back of a kitchen supply order form, four ruled columns, dates in the margins — and he turned it over to the blank side, and at the top of the blank side, in the gap above the columns, he wrote four lines.

*Friday, last week of term. The weight date.*

*Nobody told me to come today. Nobody is going to tell me what to do with it today. I have written that down because column one says I do not have to.*

*It took a two-kilo box eleven feet and put it on a counter and my arm came up under it by itself and I did not ask it to. That is not a technique. That is one Friday and I do not think it is going to be two.*

*Finger: no. Same as Monday. I have not written a different number.*

What the morning had not given back was the two things it was never going to give back. Deb's kitchen on Sennet Row had had him off every board since the week he went in, and there was a September coming that he had been told to be standing at that pass for, and he had not been near that pass since the week before, and he did not know yet what his hand was going to be by it. And the brown strap envelope was still in the parts cage at the Spine under a note with his name on it, and nobody in that corridor knew it was there and nobody was going to ask.

He put the page back in the rucksack, and the bus down from Nine Bridges was late, and he got an hour and a half of the day that had cost him a bad morning and had bought one hand eleven feet of carrying, and he wrote nothing else about it, because there was nothing else about it, and because a thing that has only one thing in it does not need a second heading.

---

The call came to Civic Spine 7 at ten to twelve on the Friday morning of week 12, and it came through the switchboard, and the switchboard put it through to the bench, and Marek Senn was on the bench with a relay out of a housing in front of him.

"Senn. It's Bea Nunn at the Exchange."

"Morning."

"It's Corvin Ashby. The man up the hill at the Weir, whose name you have, and I am not going to pretend I have never heard it because it is on a piece of paper in this building and I have read it, and I have had this telephone for about a year and it does not ring."

"What's happened."

"There's a man down at the upper pound. Fifty-eight, a plate on his wrist off your open day, a chain block and a gate and his hand's on the chain, and he can't get it off, and Ashby says he can't unweight it. He says somebody rang the ambulance at twenty to twelve and they're saying forty minutes. He's rung me since because he's got a card in his book with somebody's mobile on it and the somebody's not answering." Bea Nunn's voice did not go up at all. "And there's a nurse on Fell Road and I don't know how I know there's a nurse on Fell Road, and I am telling you because you wrote a number on my noticeboard and this is the first time that number has been any use to anybody. Do you want the address or have you got it?"

"I've got it."

"He says she came off a chain at about twenty-five past ten and has been on the ground with him since," said Bea Nunn. "Get yourself up there."

He was on Fell Road at two minutes past twelve.

---

There were nine people in a hundred yards of lane and Marek Senn counted them from the top because counting them was the only thing in his head that was any use, and a woman in a green coat was on her knees on a wet concrete apron beside a chain block with her hand flat on a man's forearm, and the man was fifty-eight and broad and he was sitting on the ground with his back against a pump housing and his right arm up, and a chain went from his wrist unit up and over a bracket and down to about a hundred and forty kilos of cast iron counterweight, and a nineteen-year-old with a bar in both hands was holding the bar and had been holding it for some time.

The man in the green coat had a card in her left hand with her knee holding it down against the concrete.

Talia Venn looked up. She had a pen between her teeth and she took it out.

"You've come from the Exchange," she said.

"Yes."

"Then you are ninety-seven minutes late and you are going to stand at the top of that lane and I am going to tell you what you are for, and if you do not like it you can walk down the hill, and I want that said now, in front of these people, because you have a habit of arriving in a room and it is the wrong day for it."

"Understood."

"Say it back to me."

"Stand at the top of the lane and do what you say I'm for."

"Good." She put the pen in her pocket. "Two things and then we're doing it. He came off that chain at about twenty-five past ten, which makes you ninety-seven minutes late, and I am not going to be inaccurate about that in front of nine people. Second thing." She did not get up off her knees. "That is a wrist unit off the September open day, fitted by nobody, checked by nobody, and the contact inside it has taken his whole weight coming off a chain handle and it has set in the shape of it, and a set contact does not let go, it holds, and it is holding about a hundred and forty kilos on a man's hand at the end of a chain. And I cannot get the cuff open, because the contact is what holds the shell shut, so I cannot get to it until the weight comes off the hand, and the weight comes off the hand when he lets go of the chain, and if he lets go of the chain the counterweight goes into the water and it is a cast iron block from about 1911 and there is about nine feet of water under it."

"And the time," said Marek Senn. "You have been on this lane since half past ten and he came off that chain at twenty-five past and nobody rang an ambulance for an hour and a quarter."

"That is the second page of the file before it is a page of the file," said Talia Venn, and wrote while she said it, "and it is in my own hand, and my name goes under it, so write it down as well. He was awake and he was in no pain and when I asked him what he wanted me to do he said sit with him. I decided at about half past ten, when I got off the bus, not to ring anybody, and I did not ring them until twenty to twelve, and that is most of an hour and a quarter that was mine to take and I took it. A crew rung at half past ten would have been on this lane by a quarter to eleven, an hour and a half before the one that is actually coming, and they would have told him the thing I have told him, and then it would have been two of us asking a man to let go of a chain with a hundred and forty kilos at the end of it, and he does not need two. I have had the best part of two hours to be wrong about it and I am not certain I am not."

"Would you make it again."

"I would make the same decision again at half past ten," said Talia Venn. "I would not sit on it for an hour and a quarter again."

"Nineteen-year-old with the bar," said Marek Senn.

"Matteo. I sent him to get the bar out of the pump house about four minutes ago and he is doing it very well and I do not want him moved." She was writing. "I am writing this now and it is going to take ninety seconds and I am going to read it back to him and not to you, and I want you to listen to it, because you are the only person in this city who keeps saying that a rescue needs a written something and I am not going to pretend you are wrong. Here it is."

She wrote fast, on the back of a card, in a small flat hand.

*Purpose: his hand comes off this chain. Nothing else, and not today.*
*Duration: until it is off, or I say stop, or he says stop, whichever is first. On the clock and not on judgement.*
*What stays: nothing. Not his practice, not yours, not what I know about him, not what he says to me while it is happening.*
*Who may stop it: him, me, you. By word. No reason asked, and none recorded.*
*Who it is for: him.*
*When it ends: the day a person whose job that is looks at that arm, and not one day before. If there is no such person by the Friday of week fourteen, it ends on the Friday of week fourteen, and it ends whichever of those two happens first, and it does not get extended by anybody wanting it to go on.*
*How it ends: torn in half in front of him, and neither half is kept by anybody, and this page is the only one I have ever written that has an end written on it.*

She turned the card round and read it to Errol Nkemdi, who had been watching a stranger write on a card for ninety seconds with his arm up over his head, and she read it in the voice of somebody reading a bus timetable, and when she got to *who it is for* he said, "That's me," and she said, "That's you," and she gave him the card and he held it in his left hand because his right hand was on a chain, and he read the whole thing himself, twice, the second time slowly.

"That's all of it?" he said.

"That's all of it," said Talia Venn. "I have got a pen in my pocket and if you want a word changed you point at it and I change it and I initial it, and that is a real thing and not a decoration."

"Leave it," said Errol Nkemdi.

Talia Venn turned the card round again and put the three of them on the back of it — herself in ink, him in his own careful capitals because she asked him to, and the third in pencil, which was Marek Senn's, and she made him come down the lane out of the place she had told him to stand, because a line on a card that gives a man the power to stop something has his name on it and not hers.

"The Friday of week fourteen," said Errol Nkemdi, reading it upside down. "That's the week your Monday round is, that is."

"That is the week the round is," said Talia Venn.

He handed it back, and Talia Venn put the card inside her coat, and turned round and looked at Marek Senn eleven feet away, up the lane, and said:

"Now I am going to ask you for one thing, and I want the asking out loud, and I want the answer out loud, and I want it in front of nine people, because you have told me in a stairwell in week five that silence is not a yes and I am holding you to it in the correct direction. This is not a share of mine and it is not a share of his and it is not going in any book."

"Agreed."

"I am going to do this with my hands and I am going to be the one who opens that cuff, and I cannot do it with my hands alone, and I do not want a care-link, and I do not want a technique, and I do not want him to say one word about how he works." She held up a hand. "What I am asking for is *use*. Use of what I am doing, for as long as the chain is under load, on that plate, and nothing stays, and I stop it if I say stop and he stops it if he says stop and you stop it if you say stop, and at the end of it there is nothing in your head about that man's arm that you did not have before you came up this hill, and if there is, you tell me and I will deal with you about it later and not today."

At the top of a lane in the Lower Wards, with nine people in a hundred yards of it and his hands in his pockets, Marek Senn found that the honest answer, when it finally arrived, was not the one he had prepared.

"Yes," he said. "And you have to say what you want me to *do*, because I have spent eleven weeks watching people do this and the only thing I have ever been able to do is read a plate, and I am not a nurse and I am not a repairer on a chain and if you want me up here saying words then I am no use to you and you should say so now."

Talia Venn looked at him for about a second and a half.

"The contact," she said. "It has set in the shape of his weight. Look at the outside of that cuff. I cannot see it, I cannot undo it, and I cannot tell him what is going to happen, and that is the whole of the question he is about to be asked and it is the only question in this lane. He is going to have to let go of that chain on a number and I have not got the number. Can you see it from there."

He came down the lane, which he had been told not to do, and got on one knee on the wet concrete nine inches from a stranger's elbow, and looked at a wrist unit on a fifty-eight-year-old's forearm for about four seconds with the eyes he used on a bench at eleven at night.

He could see it. The shell had a lip on the ulnar side where the cuff overlaps, and the lip had been pushed up and back about a millimetre, which meant the copper under it had taken a load and folded and set, and the fold had run diagonally, and the diagonal told him which way the man's weight had gone when it went.

"It will not hold you," said Marek Senn. "It is not going to hold you. That is the number. When you let go, your hand is off that chain before the counterweight's fallen four inches, because the contact is not gripping, it is jammed, and jammed means it is holding the shape of the load and the load is gone." He looked up at the man. "Mr Nkemdi, I'm going to say one thing and then I'm going to go back up the hill, because I'm not a nurse and this is not my room. That plate is not going to take your weight and if anybody has told you different then they have not looked at it. It came off a table in a car park and nobody checked it and the copper in it has already given up once this morning."

Errol Nkemdi said, "Right."

He said one other thing, to Talia Venn, quietly, and Marek Senn heard it and did not report it afterwards to anybody, including in a book.

"That's the one I've been asking for," he said. "Since September. Somebody to tell me what it's actually doing."

Talia Venn said, "Now," and put her hand flat on the front of the cuff, and said into a man's arm, in a lane, in the open air, to nobody in particular, in a voice with nothing in it: "I can feel the weight of it and it is on your hand and it is not on your wrist anymore. It is on your hand. Get it off yourself or leave it — it's your call, I've got you."

And Errol Nkemdi took his right hand off the chain.

---

The counterweight went into nine feet of water about a quarter of a second later and made a noise that three people in the lane described afterwards as the same as a door closing in another building, and Matteo let the bar go and sat down on the wet concrete, and the chain came down slack, and the man sat there against the pump housing with his arm out and his forearm white from the cuff to the elbow and his hand open, and he said, "Well," once.

Talia Venn did the cuff in nine seconds, and the copper came away in her hand in a shape like a leaf, and she put it in a bag in her own pocket and did not give it to anybody, and then she sat on an upturned bucket opposite him for six minutes and asked him his name, and his age, and whether he had ever had a plate before September, and how many there were in the house, and whether his sister-in-law had one, and she wrote all of it down, and Marek Senn was not in the lane for any of it.

He stood at the top of Fell Road for eleven minutes with a bag over his shoulder in the sun on a Friday afternoon, and did not go down, and did not go away, and was not needed, and this was the correct thing and it was the single most expensive thing he did in the term.

At the bottom of the lane the radio belonging to Matteo's brother was going, and somebody had said that the ambulance was nine minutes out, and Marek Senn took out the little spiral notebook that was not the load book and wrote four words on a page he was going to tear out and did not tear out:

*Use only. Nothing stays. It closed.*

---

The ambulance came at twenty past twelve and Mr Nkemdi went in it, sitting up, having refused to lie down, and the crew did not ask what the plate had been, and the plate was in a bag in a green coat.

Talia Venn stood on the wet concrete and looked up the lane at him.

"The card goes in the file," she said, "and the file is mine, and I am going to have to write a second page, and the second page is going to say that I was on Fell Road from about half past ten on a Friday in my own time taking two statements about a list of September receipts, and that I was not on route, and that I was not on route because of that list, and that I was not there for him." She put her hands in her pockets. "I have been a nurse for eight years and I have never once had to write that sentence, and the district is going to read it, and the district office that put that list together is the same district office that has put a letter on a door up this hill, and I have not got a single thing I can do about any of it."

"Is that the cost."

"That is the cost," said Talia Venn. "The other cost is that you were ninety-seven minutes late and I would like you to keep that, because in about a fortnight I am going to be asked how long the thing took and the true answer is that it took four minutes and that four of the people who were in that lane are not going to be in the next one."

She got into the back of the clinic car and shut the door.

And the plate came off, and it did not go back on, and there was no other cover in the world for that wrist, and the first round of the flood season was the Monday of week 14, ten days off, and nineteen men were going to be on it and two of them could not be.
