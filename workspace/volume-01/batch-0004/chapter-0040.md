# Chapter 40 — Five Days

The Institute was between terms and running two summer courses and a porter's office, and the corridors had the particular silence of a building where everybody in it is a visitor, and the conference room on the second floor had been booked out of term by a caretaker's letter and had two strip lights, a folding table, four radiators that were turned off, and no daylight and no holiday on the calendar at all.

Hollis Pratt came in at twenty past nine with a hardback file and a coat over his arm and put the coat over the back of a chair, which Marek Senn understood to be the gesture of a man who had decided he was going to be in the room a long time.

There were five at the table and one at the back.

Anneke Roos, Deputy Registrar, practical department, fifty-four, nineteen years in the building, in a jacket she had worn to a term panel in week eleven.

Bo Halloran, fifty-one, the Cordage at Saltmarket, dead right hand in an eleven-year sling, who had come because he had been asked and because he had not been able to think of anything else to do about a letter.

Ines Sarr, forty-five, the Ninth Street rooms, Nine Bridges, who had brought a folder of her own and put it on the table face down.

Talia Venn, twenty-four, resonance nurse on the Saltmarket route, who had come in her own coat and who had a plastic folder and nothing else, and who had put the second page of Friday's record face up on the table and not in front of him, and who had not looked at him once in two hours in a way he could feel across a room.

And Marek Senn, third year, no licence, no standing of any kind, at the back wall on a chair that had been in a store room since about 2011, with nothing in his hands because he had deliberately not brought anything, not the four sentences, not the photocopy, not the notebook, and nothing on the table with his name on it anywhere in the room.

Hollis Pratt said, "For the record, in order, before anybody speaks. This is a formal review by this office of four limited local holds placed on a single assembly lot inside five days of each other in the eighth week of term. Each site acted on its own authority. None of them carried a finding of variance. None of them sought indemnity and this office advised against indemnity. I am here to complete a review that this office opened by letter on the Saturday of the eighth week and closed in outline in the week after, and I am going to read out what each hold was, and then I am going to read out what my office's position is, and then the four of you are going to answer, and I want it understood that nothing you say binds this office and nothing this office says binds you."

"Understood," said Anneke Roos.

"Institute. Practical department. The twenty-four plates and the frames out of nine crates, released on the Thursday of week eight at ten past four by the deputy registrar, on a card drafted from the words of a third-year student with one word changed to put a name in place of a department. Reversible in a minute by a person with a key and a reason in writing. Named responsible person: Deputy Registrar. No finding of variance."

"That's right."

"Cordage, Saltmarket. Fifteen plates into a bucket on a high shelf, placed by the person who runs the evening classes, on the Friday of week eight at half past eight in the morning, before the day class. No document. No invoice. Named responsible person: B. Halloran. No finding of variance."

"That's right," said Bo Halloran, "and I'd like it noted that there is no document, and I've been asked twice in eight weeks by people from a company to put a document there, and I have said no twice, and the reason is that a name in a document travels through a rope works in an afternoon."

"Noted," said Hollis Pratt, and wrote it, and meant it.

"Ninth Street rooms, Nine Bridges. Stock withdrawn from a community hall store and from a classroom, placed by the person who runs the rooms. Named responsible person: I. Sarr. No finding of variance."

"That's right," said Ines Sarr.

"Mobile clinic on the Saltmarket route. Twenty-six wrist units and eight hip variants, off a shelf and off six people, one of whom asked for it back and was refused. Placed on the Friday of week eight at half past eleven. Named responsible person: T. Venn. No finding of variance."

"That is correct," said Talia Venn.

Hollis Pratt closed the file halfway.

"Findings," he said. "Four holds, and I am going to be exact, because I have done this nineteen years and the exactness is the only thing I have got. Each of the four holds was properly made, on that site's own authority, by a named person who could have been reversed in a minute and knew it. None of them was a quarantine. None of them was a finding. None of them was an accusation against anybody, and I want that on the record, because in about a year somebody is going to write a paragraph about the eighth week and that paragraph is going to be wrong, and the reason it is going to be wrong is that it will be easier."

Nobody spoke.

"Each of the four holds is also, as of today, no longer justified on any ground I am able to put in a document." He said it without any drama at all. "Not on equipment grounds. Not on safety grounds. Not on a finding of variance, because there is none and there never was. Not on the grounds of an untraced licensor, because this office is not going to trace it and has said so in writing and will say so again. The only ground on which any of the four holds survives today is the absence of a working, non-hinged boundary release, performed by the person performing it, in a public room, with witnesses, and with no substituted cue used, followed or accepted inside the scored window. That is the sentence my office gave this case in the ninth week with a reference number on it, and it is the only sentence anybody in this city can put in front of a panel, and it is the only sentence I am able to write down today."

He let that sit.

"Recommendation of this office: that the four local holds be lifted, if and only if a working non-hinged release is demonstrated at a witnessed assessment convened by the practical department. That is the whole of it, and I want three things said about it out loud, in this room, because my recommendation is otherwise going to be read in about six weeks as a piece of bureaucratic hedging.

"One. The recommendation is conditional on an event that has not happened. I am recommending the lifting of four holds on the strength of a demonstration that does not exist, at a date that has not been set, by a person who has not been named, in a room I have not seen. If that reads to you like an office dodging, you are entitled to that reading and I would rather have it than have it said afterwards.

"Two. This office cannot require a demonstration, cannot convene one, and cannot set a date. There is no such power. The panel is the department's, the date is the department's, and the criterion is the department's, subject only to the sentence my office gave in the ninth week. If the department sets no date, then these four holds stand in their present form indefinitely, with no review, and I want that understood by the four people in this room, because it is the outcome this office expects and it is the one that has been running since the eighth week.

"Three. Each of you may refuse this recommendation and keep your hold on your own authority, today, in this room, without giving a reason, and no office in this city can make you lift it. I am telling you that you can refuse because three of you are going to, and I would like the record to show that you were told."

He put his pen down.

"There is one further item and it is short, and it is not about the holds." He turned a page round and pushed it two inches along the table. "The network practice incident notification submitted in the sixth week carries a category tick in category two — technique variance attributable to the trainee — in a hand other than the candidate's, and in ink of a different batch from the candidate's entry. That is on a file in my office and I have recorded it, and I am putting it in front of the deputy registrar because she is in the room and because it should be said once by a government office in front of four people rather than by a trade paper in six months."

Anneke Roos looked at the page for about two seconds.

"Recorded," she said.

"Thank you," said Hollis Pratt. "That is all of it from me. I have forty minutes before the room goes. In your own time, in your own order, and no one will comment on anybody else."

---

Bo Halloran went first, and he took about twenty seconds.

"Noted."

"That is a decision?"

"That is a decision for today," said Bo Halloran. "I've had a bucket on a shelf for five weeks because four people in four buildings got frightened, and I did not think one of them was wrong, and I'd like a reason with a date on it before I take that bucket down and put fifteen plates back in a rope works where I cannot chase anybody." He looked at the table. "And a company man has asked me twice this term to put a document by that bucket and I've said no twice. When there's a date, send me the date. That's all. I'm not being rude, I'm being a rope works."

"Recorded," said Hollis Pratt, and it cost Bo Halloran nothing to say and everything to have to have said it, because he had set a condition and had known before he set it that nothing on that table was going to meet it.

Ines Sarr opened the folder and did not take anything out of it.

"No," she said. "And I'll give you the reason and then I'll stop, and the reason is not that I distrust you, Mr Pratt, and it is not that I think the plate is no good, and I would like that on your file in those words."

"Noted."

"I have eleven people in a Thursday class and a Saturday clinic and about forty in a week through that door, and not one of them has an employer who insures them for anything, and I am the only person in this room who has ever been the word in a room, and I worked that out on a Friday over a cup of tea in the eighth week, and I had shut a door on a man from a college the week before that, and I did not put the two things together for most of a term, and I have said in a market on a Monday that I would use a procedure against a nineteen-year-old I had known for four months and would not feel anything about it." She put her hand flat on the folder. "So if I lift this hold, and the demonstration fails, then I am the only thing standing between a room in Nine Bridges and a forearm. And I would rather be an old woman with a bucket of somebody's plates in her hall than be right about a date."

"Recorded," said Hollis Pratt, "and I want to say to you, Mrs Sarr, and I say this as an assessor and not as a man: your reason is the second strongest in this room and it is stronger than the one the Institute is about to give, and I am not going to pretend to you that I do not know which is the second strongest, because I have been doing this for nineteen years and I can hear it."

---

Talia Venn did not open anything.

"I'm refusing," she said, "and I am refusing in writing by the end of today, and I am refusing because I have got a better reason than anybody in this room and I am going to give it in the order I actually think it in, and the order is not flattering to me."

Nobody said anything.

"Friday of week twelve. About half past ten in the morning, off Fell Road, not on my route, on my own time, because of a piece of paper in a market that told me about a form in September." She put both hands flat on the folder. "A man of fifty-eight came off a chain with a wrist unit on that was fitted by a young man out of a van, and the contact in it had set in the shape of his own weight, and it was in a bag in my coat at four o'clock that afternoon and it is in the second drawer of a trolley in a vehicle on the Saltmarket route this morning, and it is going back on nobody. Not on him. Not on anybody. And that is a thing I have decided on my own authority as a nurse and not as a person with a hold, and I would like the panel to understand that if the clinic's hold is lifted, that plate is still coming out, because I am the one who is holding it and I am not letting go of it.

"And here is the reason, and it is the reason I am refusing and not noting, and it is this. Three years ago a woman in my care had a hip unit fitted in a room with a doctor in it and a form with her name on it, and it did not do what the woman had been told it would do, and nobody in this city has ever found out why, and I have wanted to know why for three years. And the honest answer, since the eighth week of this term, is that the reason is that nobody is responsible for a fitting that a young man out of a van does in a car park for nothing, and the only way anybody in this city finds out about those is a piece of paper, and the only piece of paper there is about ninety households long and it is in a district office and I have an appointment about it on Tuesday of next week in a first-floor room in Bower Street with a man whose title is continuity and returns."

She looked up.

"So I am going to be asked, in about three weeks, by a district office, whether I lifted stock out of a mobile clinic on the Saltmarket route into a car park on the strength of a date in a room I have not been inside, for a man with no licence who has not performed the thing. And my answer is going to be that I did not, and my answer is going to be correct, and I am going to be the last person in this city holding somebody else's stock, and I would like it minuted that I said that, and I would like it minuted that I know what it costs, because the clinic's hold is in my name and I am the only person in this room whose hold has a person in it who has been hurt by it."

Hollis Pratt wrote for about fifteen seconds and then stopped and put his pen down.

"That is the strongest reason in the room," he said, "and I am recording it as the strongest reason, and I am also recording that it is a reason about a person and not about an office, and that is the distinction this whole case has not been able to make in twelve weeks, and I would like somebody to write it on a wall."

---

That left Anneke Roos, and she did not take any papers out, and she sat with her hands flat on the table.

"The department accepts the recommendation," she said. "In principle, and conditional on the same condition, and I am going to be entirely straightforward with this office about why, because you have given three of the people at this table the courtesy of saying out loud what their answer really is, and I am not going to insult you by pretending to be a fourth kind of person."

"Go on," said Hollis Pratt.

"Because a recommendation from this office is the only sentence in this case that my department cannot dismiss as a grievance by a third-year, and I have forty-one people in a hall at the start of next term and I have to stand in front of them and explain four holds that a department placed on its own authority with no indemnity and no finding, and if those four holds have been lifted on the strength of an office recommendation then I have a document, and if they have not then I have a bucket in four buildings and a student's name on all of it." She did not raise her voice. "That is the reason and it is a departmental reason and it is not a good enough reason and I have known that for about six weeks, and I have been waiting for the end of term to be able to say it in a room instead of a corridor."

"Mrs Roos," said Hollis Pratt. "May I put a question to you that is not part of the review."

"Ask it."

"If the department sets no date, do you know what your position is on the Friday of week thirteen."

"I know exactly what it is," said Anneke Roos. "The variance lapsed with the term on Friday, there is no panel sitting until the spring, the transcript line stands as written, the student goes quiet, and I stand in front of forty-one people and I have never had a worse fortnight in nineteen years and I will do it."

"Then set a date."

"I will," said Anneke Roos. "And I would like it on the record that I am setting it for the department's reasons and not for his, and I would like it on the record that I said so before I knew it would."

Marek Senn had been in the room for two hours and twenty-five minutes and had not said a word, and he had the number in his own book from week nine — fifty minutes in a conference room, fifty-four at a panel — and had never once been able to make it mean anything, and here it was.

Anneke Roos turned round.

"Senn. I have to ask you, because it is on the script and because I have to be able to say I asked. Is there anything before this panel today?"

"No," said Marek Senn. "I have not got a document, I have not been asked for one, and I have not been given a hearing, and there is nothing before this panel and there has not been anything before a panel since the Friday of week eleven."

"Thank you," said Anneke Roos, and started to turn back.

"I'd like one minute," he said, "for something that isn't before you, and I'll give it up the moment you say you've got a room."

Hollis Pratt looked at him for the first time in two hours.

"Go on, then."

"The sentence this office gave in the ninth week says a public room." He got up off a chair that had been in a store room since 2011 and did not move from the wall. "The department is between terms. Everything in that building with a floor and chairs and a door is booked to a summer course or a porter's office or a caretaker's letter, and the examination hall is contracted to the faculty and was booked by this department in September and shaded by the faculty's calendar in October by a different hand. The room the department can put a panel in on a Tuesday morning is room one, and room one is a teaching room, and I have been in it, and it has a strip light and a strip curtain and a whiteboard with the term dates on it and about thirty plastic chairs that belong to a nursery."

"Would it satisfy the requirement?"

"That's not my finding to make," said Marek Senn. "It is not the Institute's finding to make either, and if the Institute says room one is a public room then a department has decided the question that the only office in this city with the standing to decide it has left open, and that is how we got here. It's the Office's assessor who decides whether the room is a public room, on the day, and that is the correct answer, and the correct answer is five days away, and in five days this building has one room."

He stopped.

"And the other thing, and then I'll sit down. There is a list. Every public room in this district you could hold forty people in belongs to somebody, and I have been to four of them in a fortnight and been told no by all four, and there is a rope works, and there are nine rooms above a community hall, and there is a kitchen in an old kiosk in a former ferry waiting room, and there is a yard office off the Meridian with a kettle in it and a door that's open from six." He said it without any weight at all. "I'm not asking anybody for anything. I'm telling the room what is on the list, so that nobody at the start of next term is surprised. And there is a fifth one on the list that I have not been to and I am not going to name in this room, because the minute I name it, somebody in this building will write to them this afternoon, and I would rather that door stayed cold for another five days than have it opened by a man in a conference room with a file."

Hollis Pratt put his pen down.

"Then it stays cold," he said. "For the record, and because it costs me nothing: if the assessor appointed by the Office rules on Tuesday morning that room one is not a public room, this office will have nowhere to put anybody, and that is a fact about this city and not about any of you. Write the fifth room down somewhere, Senn. Not here."

---

Davan Rusk was standing outside the conference room door at about a quarter to twelve with his hands in his pockets and a teaching folder under his arm, and he was not on the panel, and he had asked to be and had been refused.

"You're not on it," said Marek Senn.

"I am not on it because I disclosed a conflict of interest in week twelve that I created myself and they have it in writing," said Davan Rusk. "I have said the words *dependent on external cue* out loud in a room and one of the four on that panel read them in the week-one drill record, and I am not permitted to sit in a chair and judge a release. That is the correct decision and I would have made it myself in the autumn and I did not, and it has taken somebody else's chair to do it." He shifted the folder. "I'll be in the building. There'll be no teaching in it out of term except two summer courses and neither of them touches that corridor, and I will be on the ground floor because that is where my office is."

"You'll be in the corridor."

"I may be in the corridor before you and I may be in the corridor after and I am telling you now so that you can decide what to do with it," said Davan Rusk. "There is a thing you are going to need on Tuesday and I am not going to be the one who gives it to you, because if I give it to you it is a cue and it is the fourth thing and it is the last thing in the world you need. I am going to say one thing and then I am going to go downstairs."

"What."

"Room one is not a public room," said Davan Rusk. "I taught in it for eleven years and there is a strip curtain and a whiteboard and about thirty chairs that belong to a nursery, and I know what a room is, and that is the only reason they let me in here at all and I have not worked out yet whether it was a mistake." He put his hand on the door. "Do not walk in there and look at it and decide it is fine. Make them look at it."

Then he went down the stairs, and Marek Senn stayed in a corridor in his own department for about a minute.

---

He wrote the letter in her office at about twenty past two on the Thursday afternoon of week 13, and she dictated nothing and offered nothing and gave him the pen when he had read it twice.

*Practical department, Nacre Institute of Applied Practice.*
*Reference: PD/13/156. Out of term, under the ordinary progression provisions. Notified to the term practice-variance panel this day, being the Thursday of week thirteen, which is the last day on which this panel may be notified of a date.*
*The practical department convenes a witnessed assessment. Tuesday of week fourteen, 09:00. Room one of the practical building.*
*The requirement is unchanged and is not the department's to alter: a working non-hinged boundary release, performed by the person performing it, in a public room, with witnesses, and with no substituted cue used, followed or accepted within the scored window.*
*The department records that room one is a teaching room. The department does not find that a teaching room is or is not a public room. The assessor appointed by the Continuity Office may determine the matter on the day, and the department will comply with that determination.*

*Signed, A. ROOS, Deputy Registrar.*

Marek Senn read it four times and then said, "The weight date was the Friday of week twelve. That was six days ago."

"That is a fact about a boy's hand and it has nothing to do with a letter I have just written, and I am aware of the two things being in the same conversation." She put the letter down flat. "I was in the building when the strap came off and I have read the registrar's letter, and I know that the middle finger has not moved, and I know that the only person in this case who has ever done what that sentence in my letter asks for cannot close his right hand and cannot hold anything heavy until well into next term. He is twenty. He is not coming. He has not asked to and I have not asked him. And I have written a letter that requires the thing he did and he will not be in the room, and I have done it anyway, and I would like you to notice that I have not asked you for anything."

"You have refused me every time I have asked you for something."

"I have refused you every time you asked me for something that was not mine to give, and I am not counting it," said Anneke Roos, "and if you are going to stand in that room on Tuesday with nothing in your hands and no licence, then I am going to spend the rest of the afternoon making sure that the room is warm and the chairs are straight and that the person who sends you in does not say anything at all, because the moment somebody in that corridor says the word *scope* at you, the whole of your case is finished in a way that no panel can undo."

She took the letter back and put it in a tray.

"Five days," she said. "And you should know what you are walking into on Tuesday, because nobody has told you and I am going to. You have no licence, so nothing you do in that room is a practice. You have no technique, so there is nothing to demonstrate. You have no observation anybody will accept, so nobody can describe what you do. And there is a boy who can now take a load with a hand that could not take a load six days ago, and that is the only thing in this case that has changed since the eighth week, and it is not a technique and it does not count towards anything, and it is also the only good thing in it."

Arithmetic was the only thing he had left that nobody could take off him, and he did it standing in her office with his hands in his coat pockets.

Five days to Tuesday of week fourteen. The first round of the flood season was the Monday of week fourteen, at ten past six, nineteen men, one bar, a set of steps at the Cutbank, and Corvin Ashby short on the pound, and it was the day before and there was no way in the world to move it.

Thirty-two lines in the Exchange book, and a market of about two hundred people a day walking past a noticeboard, and a number on a wall that a man in a district's office is going to find out about from a trade, from a paper, or from one of us. A records request in a drawer under a battery, with ninety households on the form number and Darla Vint's name on the procedure, not posted and not going in a postbox. Forty-one faces in a hall at the start of next term, and a woman who has to stand in front of them and who has already told them why in advance.

And a room he has been in twice and will have to walk into as though he had not been, in a building he has been in every day since the Thursday of week one, where the only person in this city who can decide whether it is a public room will arrive on the morning and will decide it in about four minutes. He will be standing on a floor in a strip curtain with a strip light over his head and no technique, in front of a panel that has already adjourned once with no matter before it and has not yet been told who is sitting on it, and a score sheet borrowed from the exam office with its wooden tray marked *incomplete* set out on the table, and a boy who is not coming and will not be asked to come, in a corridor in which nobody will be permitted to say a word.

He said none of that out loud. He had learned in week seven that the fourth thing in a room is usually the man who explains himself.

"I'd like to be useful on Tuesday," he said.

"You will be," said Anneke Roos, without looking up from the tray. "You will be the most useful thing in that room, and there is not one thing you can do about that, and I am sorry for it, and I have not got time."
