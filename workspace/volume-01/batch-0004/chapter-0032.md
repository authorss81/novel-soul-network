# Chapter 32 — The Door

The end-of-term practice-variance panel of the Nacre Institute of Applied Practice sat for the third time in a term on the Thursday morning of week 12, at ten, in a seminar room on the second floor with the blinds down, and it adjourned at twenty past ten with no matter before it.

There were the same four members. Professor Onyeka chaired it and her field was assessment and she had no interest in Marek Senn, which by week 12 had become the single most reassuring thing about his own case. The man of thirty-eight from the trust was new and had a headache. The woman of fifty from the district's practice standards office had a folder. Davan Rusk came in at two minutes to ten and put his hands flat on the table in a way that had become a habit that he was not aware of.

Darla Vint sat at the back with a folder and did not speak at all.

Marek had a copy of the notice that had summoned him, which said *you may attend and may bring a person*, and he had brought nobody, because the only person he could have brought was Talia Venn and she had refused to hear about it and he had not asked her twice.

"Three things," said Professor Onyeka, before anybody had sat down properly. "Then I am going to adjourn us, because there is nothing in front of me and we have wasted three mornings in a term."

Nobody spoke.

"First. The department has tabled a variance. The department does not have a recommendation. The department has a held position, and the held position is that this is a hardware matter with a vendor enquiry running, and the vendor enquiry will produce a lot number, and I have read the vendor's holding reply and it is true, and I have nothing to hold anybody to. Second." She turned to the woman from the district. "Is there anything from your office."

"There is a flood-season return," said the woman of fifty, in a completely level voice. "It goes out in the first week of every flood season to anybody in my area running a shared practice that involves a load. Four headings, on the form, in writing, with a date. It is not an inquiry. It is not about anybody. It is a list so that if a man goes down on a round the district can say who was in the room with him."

"How many returns are in."

"Two hundred and six in the district, and I have about forty of them, and about nine of the forty are not a school, a clinic, a college, a landlord or a company, and they are the nine I would like to fill in." She closed the folder. "And I am not going to spend a term panel on it."

"You are not," said Professor Onyeka, "because you are right, and because it is not a practice variance and it is not a boundary release and there is nothing in front of me."

"Third," said Professor Onyeka. "And this is the only one that matters this morning, so I am going to say it slowly."

Marek Senn had both hands on the table in front of him.

"This panel will sit on a witnessed assessment. It said so on the Friday of the ninth week, when Mr Senn read it out to you in this room, and it will say so again. It will not invent one. There is no event and there is nothing to score, and every time somebody has put a document in front of this panel I have had to say the same sentence, and I have now said it three times in a term and I am going to give the department a date instead."

"Which is—"

"The end of term is tomorrow. The department may convene a witnessed assessment out of term under the ordinary progression provisions, and if it does, this panel will sit on it, and it will do so within a sitting day of the date being fixed." She looked at Anneke Roos, who had not been invited and was sitting at the back by the door and had not moved. "Deputy Registrar. If the practical department intends to set such a date, this panel must be notified on or before the Thursday of week thirteen, and that is the last day on which it can be notified of anything. If it is not, then this panel does not sit again before the spring, the variance lapses with the term, the transcript line stands as written, and nothing in this city will ever put a release in front of a panel."

The room was extremely quiet.

"And that is not a threat," said Professor Onyeka. "It is a diary. I have a diary and two hundred and six returns in my colleague's district and a roof that leaks in a building I do not control, and I am telling you the truth about the shape of my own authority and I would like it minuted that I did."

It was minuted.

---

Deputy Roos caught him at the foot of the stairs at eleven, and she had not come out of the room in front of anybody, and she did not slow down.

"You heard the date," she said.

"Yes."

"The Thursday of week thirteen. That is seven days and it is the last thing I am permitted to do in this term and I have to write a letter with a reason in it that I can stand in front of forty-one people and defend, and I have had nine hours to think about what that reason is and there is one and it is not a document." She went up two steps and turned round. "It is the Office. A recommendation from the Office on the four holds is the only sentence anybody in that building cannot dismiss as a student with a grievance, and the Office has had my notification since the eighth week and their preliminary review is a preliminary review, and they complete reviews on a timetable, and their timetable has never once been moved by a student telephoning."

"When do they complete it."

"Thursday of week thirteen," said Anneke Roos. "I know because I rang Harbridge on Monday and asked him to put me in the diary, and he did, and he did it without asking me a single question, which is the only kindness that office has ever done for me."

She went up the stairs.

Marek Senn stood in a stairwell in Lattice Ward and worked out that a term of his life had a shape, and that the shape was: one morning, and then seven days of nothing, and then one morning again. And that the seven days of nothing were not a gap. They were the case.

---

He went up the hill at twenty past two on the Thursday afternoon of week 12.

The Lower Wards were a name and not a district boundary and everything in them was low and old and built by people who had wanted the river. Fell Road came off the Bower at a junction with a bus shelter that had a hand-lettered sign for a lost dog on it that had been there so long the dog's name had worn off, and from there the ground went up about forty feet in a mile and every house on the left had a door that was a little lower than the house it belonged to.

The Weir was at the bottom of it, which sounds wrong and is not, because it was the low ground and the weir was what held the water. There was a lock-keeper's hut at the sluice with a hand-painted sign on the door that said, in three colours, most of which had gone:

**SLUICE & PUMP — CUTBANK WARDS**
**NO ENTRY WITHOUT A REASON**
**HAND TO A PERSON WHO KNOWS**

The sign had been painted by somebody who had not been told to paint it, and it was better than anything the Institute had on the wall of room two.

There was a van in the pull-off with one wheel on the grass. There were two men at the gate. There was a smell of silt and diesel and cold water and, underneath it, the specific smell of a copper contact that has been under load and has not been let go.

Marek Senn had a repair bag over his shoulder that he had not decided to bring, and he had told himself on the bus that he was bringing it because the Spine was shut and he had to carry something somewhere, and he knew that was not true, and he went anyway, because a man in a car park in September had put his thumb on a wax seal and said *I don't remember* twice, and he came here to be asked.

He got about eight feet into the pull-off.

The man who came out of the hut was forty-four and had a book under his arm, and it was a school exercise book with a rubber band round it, and he had a pen behind his ear, and he stopped at the van.

"Can I help you."

"I'm not here about the pump."

"No," said the man. "Then I can't help you, and if you're here about the pump you're in the wrong place, because the pump house is a hundred and forty years old and it belongs to a trust and I'm not the trust."

Marek Senn stood in a pull-off off Fell Road with his hands in his pockets and got to the fourth place in his own head about two seconds before his mouth did, and stopped, and took the sentence he had built and put it down on the ground like a man setting down a tool he has been asked to put down.

"What's your name," he said instead.

"Corvin Ashby."

"Mr Ashby, I'm not from the district and I'm not from the college and I'm not selling anything. I want to ask you one question and then I'll go."

"No."

Nineteen seconds. He counted them afterwards, twice, in two different places.

"I've not asked it."

"You've had your face on for nineteen seconds," said Corvin Ashby, "and you know what you look like. You look like a man who has been somewhere useful and is going to be useful somewhere else, and I've had men who look like that in this pull-off twice in nine years, and both times it was somebody from the district with a form, and both times we spent the next eighteen months being a list in a magazine instead of a set of hands on a gate." He did not raise his voice once. "That's the whole of my answer. You can have it for free. You've not asked me anything and I'm giving you a no anyway, because if I wait for the question I might say yes, and I've noticed about myself that I will."

Marek Senn took the no.

He said, "Thank you," which was not what he wanted to say, and then said the true thing, which cost him something.

"There's a district return on your door," he said. "Four headings. I've seen it from here. I'm not going to tell you what's on it because you've read it and you'll have your own words for it, and I'm not going to stand in a pull-off and explain a letter to a man who's had it six months."

Corvin Ashby looked at him for the first time properly, and the looking lasted about a second and a half, and it was not unfriendly.

"That's the first true thing anybody's said to me about that letter," he said. "The rest of it is off a template. I could tell you the reference number from here. Two letters, a slash, two letters, a slash, and a four."

"DPS/FS/4," said Marek Senn.

"That's it," said Corvin Ashby. "DPS/FS/4. And you'll know what I looked like at on the morning it came, same as everybody down here, and there's four of us who could have filled that in in about an hour between us and not one of us is going to, and the reason is not pride. It's that the last time anybody wrote down who does what on the Weir, we got a report in the autumn and the word *informal* came out of it, and after the word *informal* came a letter about the pump money, and the pump money went in the spring, and we've had no pump money since and we've had a hand on that gate every year since out of our own pockets and about four hours' notice from a man in a van." He shifted the book. "You can write that down. I don't want it written down, but you can."

"I'm not going to write it down."

"Why not."

"Because you didn't want it written down and because in about two years somebody in a district office is going to want a paragraph about why a ward has no records, and I'd rather that paragraph got written by somebody who'd been there." He got his bag off his shoulder and set it on the tarmac and stood up straight. "Mr Ashby, when's your round?"

"That's the second question."

"That's me being helpful. It's not the same question."

Corvin Ashby looked at the van, and then at the book under his arm, and then he said:

"Monday. First Monday of the flood season. Tide table says the ebb's at ten past six and the tide table doesn't move, so it's Monday, and it's eleven days, and it's about nineteen men, and we start at the Cutbank and finish at the upper pound." He said it in the voice of a man reading a tide table. "There's no cover on it. There's never been any cover on it. Nobody on that round is licensed, nobody on it is insured by anybody, and the ward's got no legal person, and if a man goes down on it at ten past six on the Monday morning then the thing that happens is that eleven other men stop what they're doing, because that is what they do, and then somebody rings the ambulance, and then afterwards there's a form at the district and the form wants a name and there's not one on the round to put in it, and they write *unidentified* and that's how the ward's name gets on a list."

"Who's the first man on it."

"Errol Nkemdi's not on it. Errol's got a bad wrist and we don't put him on gates." He stopped, and the stopping was the whole of the afternoon. "Errol's got a plate. Off that open day. In the car park. September. On a form with a dash where the name goes. He's not on the round and he comes down here anyway and stands at the gate on a Saturday and watches us lay it out, because he can't sit at home, and he stands there with a wrist unit on that nobody fitted and nobody signed for and nobody has ever looked at, and he takes a gate pole in that hand to show you where he used to take it, and if that contact goes under load and sets then the hand is on a pole with a lot of weight on the other end of it."

Marek Senn looked down the lane towards the gate, and the man standing at the gate was a broad man of about sixty in a good coat with his left hand on a steel pole and his right hand hanging, and there was nothing at all wrong with him that you could see from thirty feet.

"Mr Ashby," Marek said. "That is the only thing I came up here for and I did not know it when I got on the bus. I'm sorry about the nineteen seconds."

"Don't be," said Corvin Ashby. "Be on your way down the hill before the light goes, because the last bus down goes at six and the one after that doesn't go at all."

---

He took the last bus down at six, which was the one Ashby had told him about, and wrote three lines in the load book at the table in the room above the laundromat at half past nine that night, which is the shortest entry in the book and is the only one all term that is nothing but a fact.

*Week 12, Thursday, 21:30, above the laundromat.*

*Nineteen seconds at the gate of a pull-off off Fell Road. He refused me before I asked. He is right and I want it recorded that he told me the reason for free, which is a thing I have offered four people this month and had it refused three times.*

*Monday of week 14, ten past six, the ebb, eleven days, nineteen men, no cover, no legal person, and a man of fifty-eight at the gate with a wrist unit off a car park in September and a pole with a lot of weight on the other end of it.*

And that was all, and he did not write down what he had wanted to write down, which was a paragraph about a document with a reference number on it and no name on it and a good reason in it, because he had spent eleven weeks learning that a document with a good reason in it and no name on it is the most dangerous object in a city, and he had four sentences in his own handwriting that proved it, and it had taken him a whole term to learn and it took him eleven minutes of a bus ride to forget and to pick up again.
