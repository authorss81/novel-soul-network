# Chapter 36 — The Back Wall

Asha Reed had put the case to eleven first-years over three days without telling him, and the first thing she said to him on the Monday morning of week 13 was that, and the second thing she said was that she had done it deliberately and would do it again, and the third thing she said was *don't*.

She got him into the first-year common room at half past nine, which is a converted store room off the corridor by the sports hall with a filing cabinet, a kettle with the element furred, one table, eleven chairs, and a window that looks at the back of the sports hall's brick.

The heating was off, because the term was over and the building was running two summer courses and a porter's office, and eleven people in a cold room with their coats on is not a room anybody wants to be in, and Asha Reed had got eleven people into it anyway.

"Right," she said. "Sit at the back. You sit at the back and don't come in until I ask you, and when I do, don't explain anything for the first minute. If you explain, they'll listen to you and not to me, and I'm the one who has to stand here in March."

"You've told them about the case."

"I've told them about the card," said Asha Reed. "I've told them the card is printed against a practice and the fourth beat is not in it and the booklet that comes in the box says the fourth beat is a support beat and not a step and has got no name and no date on it, and I've told them four people put a hold on their stock and nobody's been in any trouble for it, and I've told them about the number on the wall in the Exchange, because the number is on the wall in the Exchange and eleven of us walked past it and four of us read it, and that's not research, that's a noticeboard."

She did not look at him.

"I've also told them that you're the third-year's problem and not their problem, and that if any of them comes to you about it, they should come to me first, and I would like that to hold, and I'd like you to tell them it holds."

"And if they come to me anyway."

"Then it's my fault and not yours, and you'll be able to say so, and I'll be able to say so." She pulled a chair round. "Do you want to know what my argument is before I make it, so you can decide whether to fight me on it."

"Yes."

"It's this." She put both hands flat on the table. "There is a third-year intake in about four weeks' time and every one of them will be taught from revision 4-19 by somebody in this building, and the person standing at the front will be a person out of a course, and a first-year, or a second-year, or somebody like me who has never held a plate in her life. And that person will say whatever the booklet says, because that is the job and because there is nothing else to say, and the booklet says the fourth beat is a support beat and not a step. And nobody in this building has ever said out loud, in a room, that a thing is missing. Not once. Not to a first-year. Not to a candidate. Not in a corridor, and I checked the corridor, because I read the board outside the practical department office every morning for a fortnight and there has been a term panel notice up there since before the term began and none of them says one word about any of it, and the notices go up in September and come down at the end of term and I photographed all of them."

She sat down.

"So it gets taught. It gets taught quietly and correctly and it gets taught to a hundred and eleven people a year, and every single one of them is a person who will be standing at the front of a room in about four years, and the fourth beat will get further and further into this building and nobody will ever have to lie about anything, because nobody has to lie — nobody has to lie, that's the part I cannot get past. The card is not lying. The card is silent. And you cannot fix a silence by being right in a corridor at half past ten at night about a plate."

"Go on," said Marek Senn, from the back wall.

"Go on," said Toby Ansell, who was eighteen and had a first-year's way of sitting that suggested he had opinions about all of it, and who had been in the room for both of the previous days. "She's been at this for three days. Let him say the other thing."

"The other thing is this," said Toby Ansell. "You've told eleven people that the fourth beat is the important one. You've done it on purpose. And in about three months the second-years will do exactly what you've done, and in two years they'll be standing in front of a first-year intake saying the thing you said, and they'll say it in your voice. And then there's a man at the front who says the important word, and everybody does it, and they wait for him, and it isn't because anybody chose it. It's because that's what a room does."

The room went quiet in the way rooms go quiet.

"Toby," said Asha Reed.

"No, that's the bit, that's the whole bit." Toby Ansell turned round in his chair. "Senn, in your own words — the man in the room is the thing being said. I read your file in the library, the four-page one, because it's on the circulation shelf and nobody takes it out. And you've been in this building for twelve weeks doing the version where you are the only person in the room who has seen the thing, and everybody who has ever liked you has got a version of you in their head, and it is a man who finds the missing part. And if you walk in here and say it to us, then we are eleven more people with a man in our heads who finds the missing part, and in four years it is a hundred and eleven."

"Yes," said Asha Reed. "That is the argument against, and it is the best one anybody has given me in three days, and I have heard it from two of the others and I have got an answer to it, and it is one line, and you had it at the front of your own head before I did."

"Go on, then."

"More than one of us teaches it, and none of us teaches the fourth thing alone." She did not raise her voice. "That is the whole of it. It is not me or nobody. It is that the thing that happened in this building in September is not that one man put a word in. It is that one man put a word in and about a hundred people heard it and not one of us said it out loud afterwards, and the silence is the load-bearing part. I have sat in a common room for three days and worked out that the way you don't end up being the only key-holder in it is not by refusing to speak. It is by making sure that the room can hear it from more than one mouth."

"And what happens in four years when there are forty of you."

"Then there are forty of them," said Asha Reed, "and that is a completely different institution altogether and I would like to be in it, and I am not going to decide that on my own at nineteen, and neither are you."

---

Somebody said, "Are you the third-year's problem, then," which was Rhoda Ilesanmi, nineteen, from the third chair, and she was not being cruel, she was asking, and it was the question in the room.

"I'm the year below," said Asha Reed. "That's what I am, and it's the only credential I've got, and I have used it, and I'm not going to pretend it's a bigger one. You can be the little one. I don't mind. I am the little one and I'm the one who has to do it, and if somebody else does it then I'm not doing anything."

Marek Senn had been sitting at the back wall for about nine minutes and had not said a word, and the thing he was noticing, and the thing he wrote in his own book that night, is that the back of a room is the most expensive place in it to sit, and that nobody in that room could have told him why he had gone to it.

"Right," he said. "I'll take the case, and I'll say the four sentences, and I'll say the dates on them, and I'll say who initialled them, and I'll say that four sites put a hold on their own stock and none of them has been in trouble for it and one of them is a rope works with a dead right hand and one of them is a clinic with a nurse who has written the truth in her own chart about six things. And I'll say all of that in this room, on a Monday, in ten minutes, and then you can do with it what you like."

"And the demonstration?" said Asha Reed.

"No."

"Because?"

"Because I've been in this building for twelve weeks and the thing I actually am, and the only thing I have ever been good at, is noticing a beat that isn't there," said Marek Senn. "And you can put a person in a room at the front of it and that room is done. I did it to a woman of forty-five in a corridor in the seventh week without meaning to, and ten days after that she worked out, on a Friday over a cup of tea, that she had been the word in her own room for four months, and she told a market on a Monday morning that she would use a procedure against a nineteen-year-old she had known for four months, and she was right, and I was in the room."

He turned round in his chair.

"So no. And I want to say why that costs me something, because it does, and because Asha has just told eleven people that the case is worth teaching and I am going to sit here and tell them the case is not the thing that fixes it. The thing that fixes it is a release, done by the person, in a room, with witnesses, and there is not one, and I have not performed one, and I am not going to stand up in a cold room in a converted store room on a Monday morning and do a thing on a table to eleven people in coats. If I do that, then in four years there is a first-year intake who say *we saw him do it* and it is worse than a booklet, because a booklet can be audited."

"So what do we do," said Toby Ansell. "Because we're back in here on the Thursday, and then it's the new term after that, and the next lot of people through that door are going to be told something off a card set, and not one of us is going to be able to say no to it."

"You do the drill," said Marek Senn, "and you say the fourth beat is a support beat, because the booklet says it and you have not got anything else, and I am not going to pretend there is anything else. And Toby is right that eleven of you in a room is not a solution, and Asha is right that one of you in a room is a worse thing, and both of those are correct and I do not have a third thing."

"Then you've wasted our morning," said Rhoda Ilesanmi, not unkindly.

"I've told you what the card does and what the booklet does and I've told you four sentences with a date on them, and on Thursday of this week somebody is either going to set a date or not," said Marek Senn, "and neither of those things is in this room, and I would rather be a man at the back of it than a man at the front of it, and Asha was right to say no to me, and I want it minuted, and I am not going to say it again."

Asha Reed said, "Then that's the morning," and got up, and put her chair back under the table, which nobody else in that room had done, and Marek Senn noticed it and it told him more about her than three days of argument had.

At the door she said, without turning round: "You didn't fight me on the case."

"No."

"You wanted to. You've been wanting to for ten minutes and I could hear it."

"Yes," said Marek Senn. "I wanted to say the case and I didn't, and it cost me something I wanted, and I've been writing that sentence down in a book since the Thursday of week nine and it is the third time and it will be the last, because I have said it out loud in a room instead of in a book, and it is better out loud and it is worse."

---

He read the Exchange's book at twenty past twelve and there were thirty-one lines in it, and line twenty-eight had been written on the Sunday afternoon in a hand that wanted to spell things out.

*Sunday. 14 yrs. Nothing on me. Ask if the number is about the steps at the Cutbank. Told him: the number is a service number, it is not a fault, I cannot see the steps and I cannot go and look at the steps. She asked who she was writing it under and I gave her my name and I did not think about it, and I do not know today why I did not think about it. — C. Ashby*

Thirty-one lines, and a man who had not rung the bulletin number in nine years had rung it on a Sunday because he could not ask a district, and had given a name to a fishmonger because the woman who chairs the meeting asked who she was writing it under, and had not thought about it.

That is the price of a number on a wall, and it is not the one Petar Vance priced. It is not a custodian and it is not a company. It is a man in a lane who needs one thing and finds out that the number reaches a person, and that person is me, and I am the one who comes round, and I said the exact opposite of that to a woman at a laundrette on Sunday morning, and she has been right four times out of four, and it is now twenty past twelve on Monday of week thirteen and I have got a job.

He got the bus up the hill at one o'clock because he could not not, and Corvin Ashby was not in the pull-off, and Nkechi Osei was on the steps at the Cutbank with a bar and a rope, alone, working through the last two with an orange cord round a rail post.

He stood on the grass bank above the lock and watched for about half an hour and did not go down, and she counted out loud on the way down, one two three, with her hand flat on the rail, and every time, and the first four of them he wrote nothing about and the fifth one he wrote in his own notebook because he could not help it, and by the eleventh he had stopped writing and was just standing on a bank in the cold watching a woman go down two steps about ten times.

At the twelfth one she was still going when she hit the bottom, and she turned round and started the next one, and her hand was on the rail before her feet were, and she was already counting when she got there.

And then the whole of it was on a count that was in the air from the top of the bank, and Marek Senn understood that it was not three.

It was four.

He had not counted. He had not made a sound. He had been standing on a bank above a lock for half an hour breathing.

Somebody else had.

Corvin Ashby was sitting on the tailboard of a flatbed with a hood up, thirty feet away, and he had a tea flask, and he had been there the whole time.

He did not say anything on the walk down, and he did not say anything for about four minutes, and then he said, without any heat in it, in the flattest voice Marek Senn had ever heard from him:

"She came off the last two about a fortnight before you got here. She was on three. She's on four."

He was in a lane off Fell Road with a bag over his shoulder and had come down off the bank for the first time in an hour.

"I didn't do anything."

"No," said Corvin Ashby. "You did nothing. That's how it works. I've been in that lane nine years and I've watched about forty men learn how to do this job off somebody else being in the room, and I have never once in nine years seen a man come up here and do it to a woman in four days by standing on a bank and breathing, and that's the part I want you to hear, because in about a year one of us is going to want to explain it to her and I want there to be a witness who can say that's how it happened, and I would rather the witness was you than me, because you are the one who did it."

"I did not do anything at all," said Marek Senn, and it was the first time in twelve weeks that he had said a sentence he entirely believed and had no defence of.

"You stood there for half an hour," said Corvin Ashby, and got off the tailboard, and put the tea flask in his pocket. "That's the whole of it. That's the entire mechanism. I've got to ring two men tonight about the pound."
