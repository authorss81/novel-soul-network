# Chapter 12 — The Term Calendar

The old notice was gone off the board by eight o'clock on the Tuesday morning of week 7 and there was a new one in the same plastic sleeve, and the new one was typed, and it had a reference number on it, and it was courteous.

**PRACTICAL DEPARTMENT — NOTICE OF WITHDRAWAL**
**Reference PD/7/114. Tuesday of week 7.**
*The provisional witnessed assessment provisionally scheduled for Thursday of week 7 at 09:00 in the examination hall is withdrawn.*
*1. The examination hall is contracted to the faculty for the whole of week 7 for a consolidated practice block. The booking is made a term in advance by the faculty office and is not the department's to move.*
*2. The board observer allocated to third-year candidates for that week has withdrawn. The licensing board is unable to convene a provisional board on less than fourteen days' notice to a member resident outside Nacre.*
*3. A provisional assessment held outside the term curriculum calendar requires a faculty resolution, which is taken in the week following the term review and not before.*
*The department expects to be able to offer a replacement date after the term review, which opens Friday of week 11. This notice does not alter your registration, your transcript, or the terms of your scholarship, which remain suspended pending a future assessment.*

Marek read it four times standing up, in a corridor, at ten to nine in the morning, with people going past him to rooms, and the fourth time was because the first three had been him looking for the sentence that would make it a different kind of notice.

There wasn't one. That was the thing about it. There was no *unfortunately*, there was no *regret*, there was no *this decision has been made in light of* and no name and no signature other than a departmental one typed at the bottom in a smaller font. It was the most competent piece of communication the Institute had produced all term, and it was four paragraphs long, and every one of the three reasons was true, and he had spent an hour on Monday night trying to think of a fourth reason and had not managed it.

---

Darla Vint was at the counter with a folder she had clearly prepared on Monday.

"Sit down."

"I'm not sitting down."

"Sit down, because I am going to hand you two sheets and I would like you to have read both before you say the thing you are about to say, which is that this is a Thursday." She put the first sheet down. It was the withdrawal, identical, with the reference number on it. The second sheet was a letter, and it was signed, and it said the same three things in nine lines and then said:

*The department is not able to offer you an alternative assessment route in the remainder of this term. The department recognises that this is a hardship. It has notified the scholarship office that the suspension will be recorded as arising from an institutional cause rather than from non-attendance, which does not restore the scholarship and does not affect the review date.*

"She wrote that last paragraph herself," Marek said.

"I did. It took me forty minutes and I have fought for every word of it and I will fight for it again in November when somebody asks me to soften it." Vint sat down on the other side of the counter, which she had never done. "And I want to say the rest of it, because you are going to go and find out anyway and I would rather you had it from the person who typed it. The department is not doing this to you. I have been in this building eleven years and I have watched a department withdraw a date four times and three of them were done by a person who enjoyed it. This one is a room and a calendar and a man in Crown Terrace who has a dentist's appointment on the Wednesday, and there is not one person in this building who wants it, and that is not a comfort, Senn, it is just the fact."

"What is the comfort?"

"The comfort," Vint said, "is that there is no reason in it for anybody to have a file on you. The reason is a room. Nobody is going to have to write about Thursday of week 7 because Thursday of week 7 is not going to happen."

He took both sheets. He put them in the inside pocket of his jacket in the order they had been given to him, in the left one, because that was a habit he had formed in a walkway in week 4 and had never examined.

"PD/7/114," he said.

"Yes."

"That's a Friday number."

"It's a Tuesday number," Darla Vint said. "114 is a Tuesday. They went past a hundred on Friday and the numbering restarts on the first of the week. You looked at that because you are looking for a hand in it. There isn't one. I have been in this building eleven years and I can tell you the difference between a decision and a diary, and that is a diary."

---

He got to Deputy Roos's office at half past nine and she had the door open, which she never did, and she had the window open, which she never did either, and there was nothing on the desk.

"Sit."

"Ma'am, I want to say—"

"Sit down and I will tell you the plan," said Anneke Roos, "because you will work it out tonight and you will work it out worse, and I have watched a lot of people in this building work things out and I have never once seen anybody come up with a plan that was better than the one that was actually going to happen."

He sat down.

"The plan," she said, "is that you fail quietly and go. That is the whole of it. I am going to say it in four sentences and then I am going to let you be angry for a minute, and then I am going to let you ask questions, and then I am going to go to a meeting I have already been in twice today."

"That's four."

"It is four. One: you are a third-year with two failed practicals and a transcript line, and you are not readmitted to any assessment route before the term review. Two: the line stands, it is accurate, and it will be reprinted in March for anybody who asks after you, and in the ordinary way there is no mechanism in this department to have a line amended at all without an assessment, and this would not be the ordinary way. Three: the scholarship review opens at the end of term in the ordinary way. Four: the file is closed to the department as a hardware matter, which is true, and the department will raise a vendor enquiry about the return contact, which will take six to nine months and will produce a new lot number and a letter thanking us for our patience and nothing whatever that can be counted."

She put her hands flat on the desk.

"That is the plan. It is not a threat. I have never threatened you and I am not going to start, and I am telling you it in the open because if I do not, you will spend nine days building something out of it, and I have read what you write, because I read everything in that file, and last week there were two pages in there that were not about a return contact at all."

"Ma'am—"

"Four sentences," she said. "Now be angry."

"I am not going to be angry."

"Then be something. Everybody in this building says that and then it comes out forty minutes later in a corridor to somebody who did not ask." She sat back. "There is no event, Senn. That is what I want you to take away. There is no board and no panel and no hearing and no letter with your name on it. In March there will be a piece of paper with a line on it and you will be twenty-three and it will still be there, and the reason it will still be there is not that anybody has done anything to you. It is that no event has occurred."

He sat there and looked at the window, which was the window of an office in a school he was failing, and the light came off the yard.

"Can I ask you something that is not about the plan."

"You can ask me one."

"The hall's booked for the whole of week 7."

"Yes."

"Who asked for it?"

"The faculty office, at the end of the summer before this one, as part of the consolidated practice block for all three years, which has run every year since I came here and which the department has wanted to cancel for four terms and has never once got the votes." She looked at him. "And the department asked for that week in September, Senn. For you. I did the booking myself on the second of September because the provisional assessment is my office's to book and I wanted a good date, and I got a good date, and there were four other candidates on it by the end of the week."

"You booked it."

"I booked it in September and it was a good date and it was a Wednesday week with nothing in it until Rusk put the term board in it in the middle of October, and I have looked at the calendar myself this morning because I did not believe it either, and there it is, in a different hand, in October, in the faculty's own script." Anneke Roos put two fingers on a piece of paper and pushed it round so he could see it. It was a term calendar, printed, with a block of a week shaded. "That is what has actually happened this week. Not a decision. A shading."

He looked at it for a long time.

"You said there's no file and no hearing," he said. "That's what I'm actually asking. Because you wrote *four pages* on Wednesday and I know it's four pages, and if there's no event, then what's the four pages for?"

Roos did not answer for a moment, and then she said, in a completely different voice, the way a person does when they have decided to say a thing they had intended to keep:

"Because there is an event. There is not one before the term review, and there is not one this term, and I did not put you in front of a board to be cruel to you, and the term review panel is not a board." She turned her chair a quarter turn. "It is a standing panel that looks at practice variances at the end of each term. I am the officer of this department and I am allowed to table an item, and I am going to table the 4-19 variance, and I have to do it by Friday or the term closes without it, and I am going to do it on the Friday — which is three days after the room you were booked into went out from under us — and I have thought about that for six hours and I have decided to do it anyway."

"Why Friday?"

"Because the item has to be in before the review opens, and because if it is not in before the review opens then there is no panel, and if there is no panel then in November there is a piece of paper with your name on it and no room to say anything in it, and that is a worse thing to hand a person than a room where they are allowed to be told no." She put her hand flat on the desk. "So: the panel has the file, which is four pages, you are named in it, and on Friday I will table it, and in November you will be told there is an item, and you will be allowed to attend it, and you will be allowed to bring somebody."

"Who is on the panel?"

"That is in the file and you will get it in November," she said, "and I will tell you one thing about it for free, because you would work it out and work it out wrong. One of the four is a Threadline-certified instructor who has taught the card since 2011, and he did not put himself on it, and he did not ask to be on it, and he cannot get off it, because the other three have been marking the same scripts for fourteen years and the department needs somebody who has taught the thing on the card to be on the panel that looks at the card."

Marek put both hands flat on his knees.

"Rusk."

"I am not going to say a name in my office," Anneke Roos said, "and I did not come here to say a name, and I have said more in ninety seconds than I said in the whole of week 6 and I would like you to go and be angry now because I have a meeting."

"Ma'am. One more thing and then I'll go."

"Quickly."

"The department issues spare stock." He had thought about it on the tram and it had seemed like a stupid question and he had asked it anyway, which was a thing he had started doing. "Spare training stock to households, for the parents' evening and the open day. One per household, signed out in September."

Roos went absolutely still.

"That is not a scheme I know about."

"It's on the stores issue sheet. It's under *community and family use*, and it's an old line and it goes back years, and it says *not for assessment, not for private practice*, which is a sentence somebody wrote once for a reason." He heard himself. "I'm not saying anybody's done anything. I'm saying the September issue went out to about ninety households and about forty came back, and the ones that didn't are in fifty houses in this city right now with a plate and a card in them, and nobody has tracked it since, because tracking it would mean a list, and a list would mean a question."

Anneke Roos picked up a pen and looked at the desk for a long moment without writing anything on it.

"I have never asked to see that sheet," she said. "It is a line under a line on a stores form that has been in the same drawer since before I came here, and nobody has ever asked to see it, and I am now going to have to think about it, and I would like you to understand that you have just handed me something I cannot put down and did not want at ten in the morning on the day my Thursday has been cancelled by a calendar." She put the pen down. "Go on. I have a meeting at eleven and I am going to it having been made to feel extremely small by a third-year with a transit card."

---

Rusk was in the corridor outside room two with a box of return-path markers and a paint pen, on a Wednesday, doing the same thing he always did on a Wednesday, which is why it was safe to say it to him.

"I know," Rusk said, before Marek had got the first word out.

"Say you've read it?"

"I read it at eight and I have been standing here since half seven because I did not know what I was going to say and there is nowhere in this building to say it." He put the marker down. "First, the thing you are about to accuse me of, and I would like it out of you, because I'd rather be shouted at by you in a corridor than be right in my own head for another hour."

"You're on the panel."

"I am on the panel." Rusk looked at the wall. "I have been since the middle of last month and I did not put myself there and I cannot get off it, and I want you to know what that is going to be like in November, and it is going to be me reading a file with your name in it and then asking you questions, and I am going to do that, and I am not going to do it kindly, and it is not going to be a trial and I am not going to be on your side when I do it. If you file anything before then, it comes to me, and I would rather you knew it from me than found out in a corridor."

"Fine."

"And second." He turned round. "I do not think I can get you anything before the term review, and I want you to hear that from me, out loud, from a man who is telling you no, instead of working it out from a form on a board and being angry at a piece of A4 for a fortnight. If there is a date before the end of term, I do not have the power to make it, the hall is out, the observer has gone, and the only two people in this city who could convene anything are the faculty office and the Continuity Office, and I have a telephone number for the faculty office and I have used it."

"Used it when?"

"Tuesday, at half past four, from this corridor, and I was put on hold for eleven minutes and then a man called Harbridge told me the calendar is a faculty matter and to raise it with my head of department." He said it without self-pity, which made it worse. "That is the whole of what I did this week. I would have done it on Monday if I had not spent Monday in a room with a man from a hospital."

"Was the plate welded?"

"It was set." Rusk's voice changed and then came back. "Copper takes a shape when you put a load through it that the return contact is not meant to take, and if there is no release, it stays in that shape. I have seen it twice. Once in a workshop in Lattice in my first year and once on a plate out of a fish crate in 2019, and both of those are in a file in the department stores room that nobody has ever asked for and that I have never read." He stopped. "I am not going to tell you that in November, Senn. I am telling you now because you have earned it and because I would like to have said it before it mattered."

---

He went to Nine Bridges that night because his mother's shift started at nine and there was a version of himself that still went, and it was his mother's flat three streets from his own front door on Pell Street, and there were plates in the press and a radio in the kitchen and Nell at the table with a headset round her neck and a worksheet in front of her that she was not doing.

"You've not eaten."

"I've eaten."

"You've eaten a bun, I can see the paper." Lena Senn put a plate in front of him without ceremony, and there were two of everything, and it was Wednesday, and she was on nights again, and she had a cloth bag over the chair with things in it. "How's your arm."

"Fine."

"Nine Bridges wants you to be fine." She sat down with her tea. "He's out of surgery, then."

"How do you know about Tam?"

"He rang the flat on Sunday, out of nowhere, and asked whether I was on nights, and I said yes on Sundays, and then he told me about his arm, and then he asked me whether I knew anybody on a board." She said it in exactly the voice of a woman reporting the weather. "So I know about Tam. And I have not told anybody on any board, and I would like that written down somewhere, because he is twenty and he has asked a woman who works nights the wrong question, and I am not going to be the reason a boy of twenty gets a worse answer than he deserves."

Marek looked at his plate.

"Two tendons," he said. "Right forearm. He's out. He's got six weeks before it carries anything and three months before he can hold a kettle. They're doing the exercises and he's writing a plan."

"And are you in the plan?"

"No."

"Was there anything in the plan that had your name in it, and was it put there by you?" Lena Senn said, and did not look up from her tea, and it was the exact same question she had asked him in four different ways since he was eight years old and it had never once been the question she appeared to be asking.

"He's writing it himself," Marek said. "He took it off the department. He doesn't want me in it."

"Good." She drank her tea. "Then there's nothing you can tell me about it."

That was the whole of it. She did not ask him a single question about the notice on the board, and she did not mention the term, and she did not mention the assessment, and she took two plates to the sink and washed them while Nell said something about a history project and put her headphones on.

And Marek Senn sat in his mother's kitchen with a full plate he had not been hungry for, in a flat three streets from his own front door, and did not tell them that on Friday of last week a boy had gone into theatre at eight in the morning with a dorsal splint and a piece of marker tape with a time written on it, and that the reason the boy was in that bed was that a man of twenty-two had said a word in a man's voice on a wet dock, and that on Tuesday of this week the whole of his remaining life had been taken off a board in a plastic sleeve with a reference number on it, and that in March there would be a piece of paper with a word on it and they would all still be living three streets apart in a city of two million.

"Nell."

"Mm."

"Sunday."

She took one earphone off. "What about it."

"Nothing. Somebody asked me what I was doing on Sunday." He had not been asked. "I said I didn't know."

"Well." Nell Senn put the earphone back in. "That's the most honest thing you've said to me in about six months, so."

Lena Senn washed the last plate and dried her hands and picked up her cloth bag and said, at the door, on her way, in the voice she used for last things:

"Whatever it is you're not telling us. Bring it to the flat. Don't carry it round on your own, because you always have, and because the last person in this family who carried something round on his own sat down one night and never got up."

Then she stopped in the doorway with her coat on and said, "And a man of seventy-two came in on Tuesday night, in for a hip, off a gate at work. He was up and dressed and waiting by the door at four in the morning with his coat on, and the sister on nights was delighted with him and told the whole bay, and I said, don't you dare tell that ward round he did it well, because he has done it four times and one of them is going to be the last one. And she said, well, he hasn't met me yet, and I said, he will, and then I got the bus." She got the door open. "And I thought on the bus: that's Evan. That is exactly how it was, your father, the middle of the night in a corridor and nobody saw. And then I sat there and worked out that I had just described him twice in one evening and meant two different men, and that is how it works, and that is how it has always worked, and I have not told your sister and I am not going to tonight."

She went out. The door shut. Nell had her music on. The plates were in the cupboard.

He had told nobody. Not his mother, who worked nights beside people who got better. Not his sister, who had stopped asking him for advice. Not the department, which knew everything.

*Do not know, and this is the one that is not about a plate:* whether there is a version of this where telling somebody would have helped him, and I have spent a month making a very good case that being useful to people without telling them anything is the same as not lying to them, and I have just sat in my mother's kitchen and eaten a plate I did not earn from two people who would have carried it for me, so I know the answer to the question, and the answer is no.

He walked home the three streets in the cold and did the day.

*Week 7, Wednesday.*
*Thursday of week 7 is off the board. PD/7/114. Three reasons, all true, all somebody else's diary. The only route I was ever given is withdrawn and there is no other one this term.*
*The department's plan, in four sentences, given to me in a room by the officer of the department: I fail quietly and go. No event. No panel until November. No hearing. The transcript line stands to March.*
*Two things I did not expect and am not going to forget:*
*— the hall was booked in September by Roos herself, for me, on purpose, and it got shaded in October in a different hand. So the last room I had was taken by a calendar and the person who got me it is the person who has to live with it.*
*— the 4-19 variance is on the term panel and Rusk is on the panel and neither of them put themselves there. I am named in a four-page file. In November I am allowed to attend and allowed to bring somebody and I have not got anybody, because everybody who would come has a reason not to and every one of their reasons is better than my wanting.*

*New thing, and I do not like it, and I am writing it down so I cannot pretend I did not know it:*
*The stores form has a line for spare training stock issued to households for community and family use, ninety out in September, forty back, fifty unaccounted, and nobody has tracked it since because tracking it would mean a list. Fifty houses. And Rusk told me in a corridor today, without being asked, that a plate comes out of the return contact set after a load and nobody took it out, and that he has seen it twice and once out of a fish crate in 2019, and that he has never read the file. So the shape of it is in three buildings in this city and in fifty houses and in a file in a stores room, and I cannot get at one single one of them, and I have no standing, and I have four Thursday evenings of paid invigilation in front of me and no date at the end of them.*

*Do not know:*
*Who shaded the week in October and whether that is a person or a diary entry. Vint says diary. I believe her. I do not like that I believe her.*
*Whose fifty houses.*
*What Rusk knows from 2019 and does not know that he knows.*

*And the last of the day, which is the one I am going to keep.*
*Vint told me the number 114 is a Tuesday number, and that the numbering restarts on the first of the week, and that 100 was on a Friday. I checked it on the tram, because I have no life. So the thread is the routing, not the person. Nobody has to be a villain. The person who rolled 114 is a woman doing a job on a Tuesday. The person who shaded week 7 is a department. Between the two of them they have taken the only route I was ever given, and neither of them will ever know my name, and there is no form anywhere in this city that asks them to.*

He got in at twenty past eleven and Rafi was on the floor with his back against the couch and a courier bag over his legs, eating bread and dripping, watching something with the sound off.

"You've been out," Rafi said.

"No."

"You've been out for fifteen hours on a day you don't work." Rafi did not look round. "Thursday's off, then."

"How do you know about Thursday?"

"It's been on the department group. Somebody's photographed the board and put it up." Rafi turned the volume up one notch, which was how he was kind. "You'll not be having a bad night about it, then."

"No," Marek said. "I'll not be having a bad night about it."

He lay down on his own bed in the dark with his gloves under the pillow and did not do any of the eleven things he had planned to do, and at some point in the small hours he understood that the thing which had been keeping him upright for six weeks — the certainty that the problem was large and true and his — had quietly come out of his hands, and that what was in its place was a worse thing and a smaller one, which was that he had been *waiting* to be given something, and there was nothing coming, and there was one day left in the week and then there was no day at all, and somewhere in that city a fifth plate was coming off a shelf onto a hip.
