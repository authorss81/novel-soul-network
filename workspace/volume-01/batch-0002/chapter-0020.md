# Chapter 20 — The Speed of a Yes

Rafi Pell was not speaking to him on the Saturday morning of week 9, and it turned out that not speaking to Marek Senn took a considerable amount of talking.

It started at about ten with the toaster, and it went on through the door, and by the time Marek had his boots on it had become a short lecture delivered entirely to a third of a slice of bread.

"— and I'm not saying I was right to walk out, because you wrote it on the paper and the paper said I could, and I did. I'm saying I knew what I was doing when I did it, and that's the part I've been sat here for three days with."

"You knew," Marek said.

"Aye."

"Right."

"Don't do that." Rafi turned round with the bread in his hand. "Don't go *right*, because if you go *right* then I'm in the wrong and I'll be in the wrong for a month. Say the thing. Say what I did."

"You walked out of a room that had made you into a data point, at the exact point where you noticed it, and you gave a reason that was about you and not about the technique, and you didn't give the reason, and the paper said you didn't have to." Marek put his second boot on. "That was correct. It was the most correct thing anybody has done since the Thursday of week one and it cost you something and you did it anyway."

Rafi bit the bread.

"Two things," he said. "One, I wasn't going to come and find you first, and you noticed that on Wednesday night and you did nothing about it, and that was the right thing as well, so — two things." He chewed. "Two, you never said we were doing something."

"We're not doing anything. I haven't got a room. I haven't got a—"

"You had a room on Wednesday night and it had my leg in it." Rafi put the bread down. "That's what I'm on about, mate, and I want to say it right because if I say it wrong you'll do a thing with it. You never announced it. There was no point where you said *right, we're going to do something now, and here's what it is, and you can say no*. You said *I'd like you to do the step* and I did the step, and that was a thing with an end to it, and then somewhere in the middle of the evening I was in a comparison with a woman of forty-five and a docker and a third-year, and my courier step was in it, and my shoulder was in it, and the wall was in it, and I was a data point, and I did not know that for about forty minutes, and if somebody had asked me at nine o'clock on Wednesday night which of those two things I had agreed to, I would have said the step."

"Yes," Marek said. "That's exactly right."

"I know it's right, I've had three days." Rafi sat down on the arm of the couch. "Because I'm the fella who says yes at the top. That's my whole thing. Everyone's known it since week one. So when you want something you don't need to persuade me, you need to *tell* me, and you never tell me, because you're polite, and being polite to me is the same as not telling me. You've been doing the thing where you don't ask and then I fall in. And I'm not walking away from you over it. I want that on the record at about ten o'clock in the morning before either of us gets hungry."

"You're not walking away."

"No. I'm not." He picked the bread up again. "And here's the second thing, and that's the actual thing, and it's not about you at all. I'm going to start saying no at the top."

---

"The what?"

"Before I know. That's the whole of it. There's a two-count." He held up two fingers. "I use it on the ramp, forty, fifty times a day, it's the thing that makes me the best on the ring: you come off a kerb, you count *one* on the front foot, you count *two* when the weight's over, and you don't commit to the turn until two's done. It's not technique, it's just a count, and it's the only reason I've not put myself in a hospital, and I've been doing it since I was sixteen."

"Ruf, you were saying—"

"Let me get round to it, I'm building a thing." He put the bread down and got up, and stood in the middle of the room with the rug, which was the worst piece of floor in the building, and did the two-count out loud going nowhere, in one fluid sequence, in about a second and a half.

"There," he said. "That. And on Wednesday night I was in a room and I did not do it. I went straight in. And if I'd done it — two seconds, that's all, two seconds of counting, nobody'd have noticed except you — you'd have had two seconds to say *we're doing something now* before I was in it."

"You want me to time you."

"No. I want to do it *visibly*." Rafi grinned, and it was the old grin, and it was the most alarming thing he had said all morning. "I'm going to do it slow. On anybody. Everywhere. I'm going to count out loud, I'm going to do it at the top of a ramp where two couriers can see me, and I am going to be *embarrassing* about it, and I am going to do it to you every single time you ask me something for the rest of the time we live in this flat, and I want you to know in advance that I am not going to be embarrassed about it later when you've decided I'm being difficult."

Marek looked at him.

"You'll do that to me on purpose."

"Course I'll do it to you on purpose," said Rafi Pell. "You said yes to four things in week one and I only knew about one. So here's the deal and it's not a deal, it's just a fact about me now. Any time you want something off me, you're going to have to stand there while I count to two, and if I'm still going when I get to two, you can have it, and if I'm not, you haven't got it and I'm not going to tell you why. And if you get bored and walk off while I'm counting, then that's the answer and you can have that one for free."

"That's a system."

"It's a man counting out loud in his own front room," said Rafi. "It's not a system. It's the only thing I've got and it's about two years late."

He sat back down and picked up the bread and ate the rest of it, and then said, through it, in a completely different voice: "And you'll not get me back with it, by the way, in case you've been hoping. I said I'd stay and I am staying. I just got tired of being a yes."

Marek Senn stood in a room above a laundromat on Pell Street at ten past ten on the Saturday morning of week 9 with his work gloves under his arm and understood that he had just been given, free, by a man with a courier bag on the floor, the most useful correction anybody had handed him since a woman in a box on wheels had stopped him in the rain in week 5 — and that it was better than hers, because hers had been about a practice and this one was about a habit, and about him, and about the difference between the two, which is that her correction had taken a plate off him and this one had made him slower.

---

Civic Spine 7 was a day shift and there were nine jobs on the board on the Saturday of week 9 and Marek was there from eleven to seven.

He did a resonance lamp, a stair relay, a door buzzer, four plates, and a set of bathroom heaters in a building on the canal nobody could name, and he did them in the order that made sense, and the work went the way it always went, which was that other people watched him do it and gradually stopped hovering.

At about half past two a woman of about fifty with a bad hand came in with a bag of parts and asked whether the same person who did the lamp could look at a thing on the third floor, and Marek said he was the same person, and she said everybody in the building said that and none of them had ever been the same person twice, and he went up and did it and came down and she gave him a coffee, and that was the whole of his professional life up to that point and it was a life he was good at and it did not help anybody's release and he had made his peace with that in the middle of the term without ever writing it down.

At twenty past four Petar Vance came through with a service case and a folded envelope and put the envelope on the bench.

"Not mine to open and not mine to have carried," he said. "Guy left it at the depot counter for Senn of the Institute on Tuesday and asked me to bring it in if I came. Twenty-eighth, remember. It's the twenty-eighth on Wednesday." He put the case down. "The Ninth Street rig's making a noise, by the way. Nothing serious. She'll ring you."

He went. Marek looked at the envelope for a while and then put it in his jacket, unopened, because a man's name on a thing is not permission and he had learned that from a woman with a clipboard and it was the only thing anybody had ever taught him for free.

At half past five he had a plate frame off a wall relay that had dropped out of square, and he needed to shim it, and he did what he had done since he was sixteen and reached into the roll and got out a grey transit sequence card with soft corners and a shallow file scratch across the printed beats, and slid it under the frame to hold it while he marked the wall.

Four printed beats. A fifth line ruled beneath them and left blank. He had used it to shim plate frames two hundred times and had never once looked at the blank, because it was a line about half an inch long and it was not a line anything had been printed on, and it had never been anything at all.

He looked at it at half past five on the Saturday of week 9 with a marker in his hand and a wall relay waiting.

He had had it since he was fifteen. It had come in a tin with a transit timetable and a photograph and his mother's handwriting on the back, and it had been in a tool roll in a hand and a jacket and a toolbag and a drawer in Six dormitories for seven years, and it had never once occurred to him that a father could leave a blank line.

He put the shim where it was and finished the job and wrote the fault on the back of the form in his own hand and signed it, and then he stood at the bench in a repair shop in Lattice Ward with a marker cap in his teeth and did not move for about a minute and a half, and what arrived was not a revelation.

It was recognition. It was a thing he had been standing next to for seven years and had finally looked at, and it arrived in the middle of an ordinary job, at a bench, at twenty to six in the evening, on a Saturday, which is how every true thing about a person arrives, in the middle of something else, and never on the day you go looking.

It arrived with the whole of the rest of it.

He had a page in his notebook with two columns on it and fifteen numbered lines. He had four sites. He had a nurse who had changed the terms of her own scope out loud on a step and put his name across the line from hers. He had a woman of forty-five who had held a meeting on a Friday night because he had said two words in a corridor. He had a man with a dead hand who had put fifteen plates on a high shelf and told him, to his face, that this does not get into the schoolchildren, it gets into the ones with the mark already. He had a docker who had taken a bucket of stock off a wall before the day class started and would not give it to him. He had a deputy registrar who had signed her own name to a cage and had said *nobody made me do it and I checked*. He had a boy of twenty who had walked eleven minutes across Nine Bridges at midnight to hand over a copy of a voice he had built out of Marek's, on purpose, so that nobody else would have it. He had a field assessor who had put a sentence in a file that belonged to somebody else. He had a book clerk who had never been asked for a line and had given him one anyway.

Every single one of them had done it for themselves, on their own authority, for their own reason, and had not been asked, and had not been arranged, and had not been managed.

And he had spent nine weeks being the reason.

Not the cause. He had not put a single one of those plates anywhere. He had put a kind of word in a girl's mouth, a plate count in a notebook, a number in a laundromat, and a boy's word in his own, and then he had gone to a fish market and a community hall and a rope works and a department office and a laundromat above a shop, and in every single room he had been the person who knew things, and every single room had done what he was for, and he had walked out of all of them thinking it was the best thing that had ever happened to him.

He had been doing, for nine weeks, without ever once saying so, the job of the person who is responsible for everybody's consent.

That was a job. It had a shape. It was the shape of a key-holder, exactly, not a metaphor for it, not a rhyme — the person every room turns to at the fourth place, the person who decides whether a room is going to be told something or not, the person whose face three strangers check in the same half second because he is standing there. He had built the whole of it out of care. He had built it because he could not stand in a room and leave a question in it, and because a question in a room unsettled him more than anything else in the world, and because he was twenty-two and alone and had a hole in his chest and eleven hours to fill it, and he had gone round a city of two million people and found somebody to fill it with.

And the terrible part, and the part that made him put both hands flat on the bench, was that it had worked. It had worked for nine weeks. It had worked on Tam and on Farrow and on a courier with a wall in his shoulder. The only things it had actually accomplished, in nine weeks, were a boy's arm and a man's ribs and a woman's registration and a boy's eleven seconds in an empty room.

He sat down on a stool at the bench at twenty to six in the evening on the Saturday of week 9 and said a sentence out loud in an empty repair shop, which he had not done since he was a child and which nobody heard.

"I have been the word in the room."

Then he wrote it in the load book, and the writing was not steady, and it took him eleven minutes and he crossed out four lines.

*Week 9, Saturday, 17:40, Spine 7 bench, on my own.*
*I have been doing a job. Not my job. Somebody else's. The person who is in every room and decides whether the room is told anything, and who is looked at at the fourth place, and who everybody believes is the one who knows.*
*Nobody asked me to do it and I did not notice doing it, and I have been doing it since the Thursday of week one, and on Monday a boy of twenty told me the reason for the paper on the card, and on Wednesday a woman of twenty-four told me the reason for all of it, in a vehicle, in one sentence, and I wrote it in the book two days later as if it were about a clinic and a boy, and it was not.*

*Evidence, in order, and I am putting it down because this is the only entry I have ever written where the evidence is against me:*
*1. Nothing I have done since September has been asked for by the people it was done to. Not one. I have gone to a market and to a hall and to a rope works and to an office and I have been useful in all of them, and being useful is not consent, and I have known that since the Thursday of week five and I have not once applied it to myself.*
*2. R. P. says the paper said he could leave and he did, and that I never announced there was a thing to leave. He is right. There was no announcement. There was a room that had quietly started to need a man in it, which is a thing I have been criticising in a product for nine weeks, and I have been building one, out of nothing but good manners.*
*3. T. A. wrote, on a Monday: you do not get to arrange anybody. He is twenty. He has read more of the inside of this than I have.*
*4. T. V., on the Thursday of week nine, in a vehicle, in one sentence: everything you have done since the Thursday of week one has been arranging.*
*5. A. R. said, on the Saturday: nobody made me do it and I checked.*
*Six people, five rooms, four weeks, and not one of them needed me.*

*And the last of it, and this is the bit that is actually new and I did not have it on Friday:*
*I have been doing it because I cannot stand a question being left in a room. That is the whole of the reason. Not wanting to help. Not courage. Not the badness in my chest. I cannot bear to be in a room where nobody has said what is happening, and so I have spent nine weeks going into rooms and making sure that a question gets answered, and a question that gets answered by me is a question that has been taken off the table.*
*That is the whole product. That is the entire thing and it is not a company and it is not a card and it is not a man with a good voice. It is one frightened man, walking round a city, being competent at things, so that no room is ever left with an open question in it.*
*And there is not one person in this city who has ever printed it on a label, because nobody has ever had to buy it.*

He read it back. Then he did the thing that made it a decision rather than a piece of writing, which was to write four lines underneath it in the ordinary handwriting, in a book, in a room on his own, on a Saturday.

*What I do about it, and I am writing it now while it is still frightening, which is the only time I write anything true.*
*1. I stop arranging. From today. No room gets built out of people who have not been asked. If a person is useful to me, they find out from me what it is for, and then they are asked, in a sentence, with a way of saying no that costs them nothing.*
*2. I do not chase. I have a queue of noes the length of my arm and I am going to let it be a queue. F. said no once, in a yard, about a report, and he was right, and he has said yes twice since, both times on his own terms, and neither of those is a retraction. The correct response to a no is not a better question.*
*3. I ask one person one thing at a time, and I take the answer, and I do not go and find four other people because the first one was difficult.*
*4. I stop believing that being right is the same as being believed.*

He looked at number 4 for a while and then he did something he had not done to a load book in nine weeks, which was to add one word, at the end, in the same pen.

*4. I stop believing that being right is the same as being believed. I have known that since the Thursday of week one and I have been going round the city trying to be believed, and every single thing that has happened in nine weeks has been me trying to be believed. And a boy of twenty has spent a fortnight telling me the fourth beat, which is a question asked out loud, and I have taken his asking and used it as a thing to prove, which is the exact opposite of asking one, and he knew that, and he wrote it on the card, and I am the last man in Nacre who should be saying the word to anybody.*

---

The phone went at twenty past six while he was shutting the bay down.

"Senn." Talia. "Two minutes, I'm between."

"You've had the Office's answer."

"I've read the letter. I wasn't in the room and D. copied me the file, which she is entitled to do and which I want noted as the only reason I know anything at all." A door closed on her end. "Right. Two things and then I have a patient. First: the Choir of Practice. It is a co-operative of nurses and repairers and teachers who publish lineages in plain language, and the only complete record in this city of who wrote a technique, when, and what they changed is in their quarterly, and a copy of every one of them lies on the table at the Saltmarket Exchange for anybody who walks in off the street. Are you writing that down? I don't care if you are not. I am going to say it and then I have got a hip to do. They are not an authority and they will not do anything for you, and one of them will tell you to your face that you are wrong, in front of nine other people. Go. Read a quarterly. Do not tell anybody you went, and do not ask me what to do when you get there."

"That's not help, that's—"

"That's information," said Talia Venn. "Information is not help. Help is what you have just spent nine weeks arranging out of people who had no obligation to you, and I am not going to do any more of it, and I would like it noticed that I have said no to you four times now and every single time you came back with a better version of the same question, and this is me saying no to a fifth one, and the no is the same no."

"Talia—"

"And the second thing." She did not soften it, because she never softened anything, and because the last time she had softened a thing it had cost a boy two tendons. "I am not going to help you find a fourth person. That is your work. It has been your work since Wednesday and you have been doing everybody else's work instead, and the reason I am telling you this now, in this tone, on your Saturday, is that I would rather be unpleasant to you on the telephone than be right in a room in three weeks. There is no fourth person. You will have to go and find out that for yourself, the hard way, on your own, with a queue of noes, and I am not going to walk you to the door. And Senn — the first no is going to cost you more than the first yes did, because the first yes was free. If you cannot stand in a room and be told no by a stranger, then you have not stopped arranging. You have only changed the sentence."

"Who do I ask?"

"Thirteen people who do a thing you cannot do, in a room, and say no to you, one at a time, in words, out loud, with the option to stop you." A pause. "That is the whole method, Senn. It has been the whole method since the Compact and every practitioner in this city who is any good at it is sick of hearing it. You are twenty-two and you have just discovered it. Everybody does. It is very stupid and it works and there is no shortcut, and I have watched four men this year try to find one and three of them are still trying."

He stood in a darkening repair shop in Lattice Ward with a dead receiver feeling like a man who had just been handed the entire remaining project in one sentence by somebody who had refused, in the same breath, to help him with any part of it.

"Two minutes," said Talia. "That was two minutes. I have a hip."

---

He got in at half past nine and Rafi was on the floor with the courier bag over his legs and the sound off again, and there was a sheet of paper on the table with a sum on it that had been wrong and had been corrected twice.

"Senn."

"Ruf."

"Two." Rafi held up two fingers without looking up. "Just so you know, in case you were going to ask me something."

"I wasn't."

"You were thinking about it," said Rafi Pell, "which I can see, because you do a face, and that's a yes at the top and I haven't got to two yet, so you'll have to come back after." He put his hand down. "And for the record, you came back and you didn't ask, and that's the first time in about three years you've done that, and I noticed it, and I'm not going to make a thing of it because you'd find it unbearable."

Marek sat down at the table with his coat still on and told him the other thing, because he had decided on the tram that he was going to tell him the other thing.

"The Thursday class at Nine Bridges," he said. "The one I sat in the back of and didn't write anything down. I was invited back, and the woman who runs it has told me she'll take me out by the arm if I take a note about one of the eleven people who come to it, and she isn't joking, and she has done it before to a police sergeant."

"Are you going?"

"Thursday. After that." He put his hands flat on the table. "And I'm telling you before I go, not on the way, which is a thing I learned off a boy with a sling on Thursday, and I'm not going to sit in that room and be in a frame. I don't know what the frame is yet. That's the part I don't like."

Rafi looked up for the first time.

"You'll not last twenty minutes," he said, "and I say that as a man who can't last twenty minutes in a room where anybody's watching. You'll want something off one of them by half past and you'll not take it, and by eight o'clock you'll be sat there furious with yourself and calling it discipline. And if that's what happens, come home and tell me about it, because that's allowed, and it isn't the same as the other thing."

"Probably."

"Good," said Rafi Pell, and went back to his paper. "Then you'll fit right in, because that is exactly what that corridor is full of, and it took me two years and a bad hand to work out it was a room, and you have had nine weeks and a bad record. Mind how you go."

Marek opened the load book and turned to a clean page and ruled a line across the top of it, and wrote the date, and then stopped, because for nine weeks every entry on that page had been a count of something he was owed or a thing he could not do, and he had run out of both, and for the first time in the term he did not know what the week was going to be.

He wrote:

*Week 10.*

*Where the case is, as of the close of week 9, on one page: four points, a list of what I do not know, and four sentences of what I am going to do. It is not a plan. It is the first page of this book that has got a name on it that is not mine.*
*1. Thursday of week 7 is off the board. PD/7/114. There is no replacement date and there will not be one before the term review, and the department has told me in writing that a date will be considered when the review has concluded, which is a fortnight away, and the scholarship goes at the opening of it.*
*2. The term panel on the Friday of week 11 has one item on it and I am named in the file, and the variance is dated the fifteenth of week 6 and was tabled on the Friday of week 7, four days after the room I was booked into went out from under us, and my officer did that on purpose and told me so after. A document, a suspicion and an absence: the panel cannot act on any of the three. Four panel members. One of them taught the card. One building, one card, one voice, nineteen years and one term, and he cannot get off it, and he told me so in a corridor before he had to.*
*3. The Office cannot trace a blank field, and has said so in writing, with a reference number, and has told me the only thing a panel can act on is a demonstrated non-hinged release in a public room by a named practitioner with witnesses and no substituted cue used, followed or accepted in the window. That is hanging over me. That is the whole of what I have. It is a requirement, not a permission, and there is no date, and no licence, and no technique, and the requirement is exactly the one thing I have never once been able to do.*
*4. Four sites have stopped. I did not do that, and the first of them was a nurse who forwarded the government's own letter to three strangers before she had spoken to one of them, and a woman of forty-five in a corridor above a community hall who worked out on a Friday lunchtime, on her own, that she had been the word in her own room for four months. It is the only part of this that works, and it works without me, and on Saturday I found out why, and the reason is that I have been doing a job that is not mine for nine weeks, and the job was the word in the room. Ines Sarr has told me she will take me out of her classroom by the arm if I take one note. I am going on Thursday. I am telling Rafi tonight, out loud, before I go, and I am not going to sit in that room and be in a frame.*

*Do not know, and this is the honest state of it and it is going to stay the honest state of it until somebody changes it:*
*Who wrote the fourth beat out. Blank section 4, four thousand one hundred and six bundles, nine hundred and forty blanks, and a four-year-old precedent I am not able to afford and a man with four assessors who has told me the truth about why.*
*Why the same gap is in a courier's step, in a docker's release, in a fish office, and in a woman's hip pocket, and in at least fifty houses in this city that no school supplied, and a line on a stores form that nobody has tracked since 2019.*
*Whether a boy of twenty can find a fourth beat in four weeks when the strap comes off, when the answer is that it is not a beat and it is not a technique and it is a hand on a box for eleven seconds in a room where nobody wanted him to be all right, and it cannot be witnessed and it cannot be checked and it is the one thing in this term that worked.*

*What I am going to do, and it is four sentences and none of them is a plan:*
*I am going to go and look at a room in a market where a lineage is printed and dated and attributable, and I am not going to tell anybody I went.*
*I am going to ask thirteen people one thing at a time, in a sentence, and take the answers, and not go and find five more because the first one was difficult.*
*I am going to go to Petar Vance before the twenty-eighth, on my own, in a room, with a witness, and say that I remember the wax paper and that I lied about it twice.*
*I am going to stop arranging, and I am going to find out what happens in this case when I am not the man in the room, and I have a fair idea it will be slower and smaller and that people will be angry with me for it, and I am going to have to be able to stand in that and be a man who does not do the room.*

He read the whole page back. It was shorter than the page before it, which was the first time in nine weeks that had happened, and most of it was about other people.

Then he put the pen down and lay in the dark on his back with his gloves under the pillow, and Rafi breathed on the couch, and the laundromat below them went through three cycles of a machine and then stopped, and Marek Senn lay in the dark and did not do any of the eleven things he had planned, and did not sleep for a long time, and was not frightened of it, which was new, and which he did not trust, and which was probably the only thing he had actually got out of nine weeks in a city of two million people.
