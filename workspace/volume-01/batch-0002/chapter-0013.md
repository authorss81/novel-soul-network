# Chapter 13 — Ordinary Breathing

The Saltmarket route had three fixed days. Tuesday and Thursday it stood in the lay-by outside the wholesale kitchens, on Wednesdays it stood in a yard off the fish office on the west quay, and at the weekend it did whichever of the two the list said, which was most often the lay-by, because the kitchens have a car park and the yard is unusable in rain. And the fish office yard is the only place in Nacre where a mobile clinic is asked for by name every week by people who have never once been to a hospital, because the hospital is eleven minutes away and eleven minutes is a bus fare and a bus is a thing you have to decide to do on a bad week.

Marek came out to the fish office on the Wednesday of week 8 at ten past seven in the morning, forty minutes before the clinic opened, with a notebook in his jacket and four things he had prepared to say.

He said none of them.

Mal Farrow was already in the yard, sitting on an upturned crate in an apron with a knife in his hand and a cigarette going in a bit of cardboard, and he was sixty-one and he had the specific stillness of a man whose hip has opinions and who has negotiated with them for twenty years.

"Farrow."

"Marek."

Marek looked at him and found that he did not have any of it. He had looked at this man once, at six feet, in a room, on a Thursday of week 6, and had written him down as *unknown name, 60s, bad hip*, and had gone home and written *not compared*, and had understood at the time that the reason he could not ask was that he wanted something.

"Yes," Farrow said. "You're the one from the school."

"I am."

"What'd you want?"

It was, Marek thought, the single fairest opening he had been given in a month, and he stepped straight into it and took the whole of it, and made it the exact shape of what he was.

"I need to see you do the thing you did on the Thursday. I need to see it twice and compare it against a card that's got a beat missing out of it, and if it were any good I'd take it to a board and say a technique can be finished more than one way. And I know that's a Tuesday night to you, because you've been doing it since before I could read, and I have never once asked you, and there's a man in a hospital with two tendons in his forearm who needs somebody to have never been on a card, and that's how I ended up here. So I've come to watch you, not to help you, and I can pay you for the morning, and if the answer is no then the answer's no and I don't come back."

The yard was quiet except for the water at the quay and a gull.

Farrow put the knife down.

"No," he said.

"Right."

"Before you go — no." He picked the knife up again and looked at the blade and not at Marek. "You've come down here twice now asking me about your four things. Thursday you were stood over there by the ice waiting for me to fall over, and I saw you, and you didn't come over, and I thought: right, he's shy. And now here you are with a bit of paper and a story about a board." He turned the knife. "What you've come for is that I should do a thing in front of you. And I should. I've been doing it in front of people for forty years and it costs me nothing and it doesn't do me any good either. So you'll do it and you'll write it down and there'll be a sentence about me in a report somewhere in a building with a door and a nameplate."

"Yes."

"And I want to know what the boy Arrick gets."

Marek had not expected that and it took him a second.

"Nothing," he said. "He'd get it written down that somebody he'd never met could do a thing he can't. That's it. That's the whole of what he'd get and he would never be told who."

"Aye," said Farrow. "That's what I thought." He put the knife in the block. "So here's what I'll do and it's the last of it. You can stand there. Not on the ice, on the boards, and not behind me, and you can write down what you saw, the way I do it, which is the only way anybody has ever seen me do it. And when he's ready to see it, he can come and see it himself, and I'll do it for him, and you can be in the room or you can be in the country. I don't do it for a report."

"Fair."

"And you can buy me a coffee." Farrow got off the crate. "Not for the doing. For standing in my yard at ten past seven on a Wednesday for three weeks running when you've not got a reason to be here except that you want something out of me. That's a thing you can pay somebody for."

---

The clinic opened at eight. Marek took the fold-down step and stood by the sink out of the way, and Talia Venn looked at him and did not say hello.

"You've been in my yard," she said.

"He's been in my yard," said Farrow, from the chair, taking his apron off. "Coffee's on you."

Marek said, "I told him what I wanted. He said no to the report and yes to being watched, and if Arrick comes, Arrick comes, and I don't get to be in the room."

The clip-board went still.

"Say the rest of that again."

"He'll do it for Tam and I can be in the room or I can be in the country. He decided that. I didn't offer it."

Talia wrote three lines on the clipboard and did not look up. "That's a man exercising a boundary in a yard at eight in the morning and you brought it to me in under a minute and you did not put it in a frame." She put the pen down. "Right. Then here's what you do with the rest of your morning, which is nothing. You stand where you're standing. If I ask you for a hand, you give me a hand and not an opinion. And if you get a thought in your head about a fourth beat, you keep it, because I have got a man in that chair with a hip he has never once complained about in my hearing and I would like him to keep going for eleven more minutes."

At four minutes to nine, Mal Farrow stood up off the chair, put his hand flat on the trestle, and went over sideways without a sound, and took the whole of it on the left side, and the sound the hip made was not a sound a person makes on purpose.

---

Marek was in the vehicle before he knew he had moved, and Talia was already down on the floor with her knee against the man's shoulder and one hand at the back of his head.

"Can you hear me. Mal. Can you hear me. Look at me. Look at the light. Good. That's good. Mal, I need you to tell me something and I need you to answer me properly, not to be polite. Your name."

"Mal Farrow."

"What year is it?"

"Twenty — I don't do this at seven in the morning, love."

"That was the test," Talia said. "You passed it and I'm sorry. Mal, your hip's gone again and I need to know about your chest. Have you got anything in your chest. Any pain. Any trouble. Any history I don't have."

"Two cigarettes when it happens," Farrow said, from the floor. "That's all. Two when it goes. Every time since I was fifty."

"Right."

And then, because she had done this four times and it never got easier, Talia Venn picked up a plastic wallet off the shelf behind her hip and took out a folded card and read it out loud to a man on a floor, at a quarter to nine in the morning, in front of a witness, in ordinary words.

"Mal, this is the care-link. It is a thing we agreed in this vehicle in August and I have brought it out every Wednesday since and you have signed it once and not been asked to sign it again. I am going to read it now because in about a minute you are not going to be able to hear me, and I am not going to do anything to you today that is not on this piece of paper, and I am not going to do anything to you today that is not on this piece of paper *and that you do not know about*."

**CARE-LINK — SALTMARKET ROUTE — PATIENT AGREED IN ADVANCE**
*Reason: sudden breathlessness or loss of consciousness in a patient with a chest or hip presentation on a mobile route with no ambulance within nine minutes.*
*What it permits the named clinician: physical airway support, bag ventilation, and one hand's contact at the load or strap. Nothing else.*
*Duration: until the patient is breathing, or until an ambulance takes over, or ninety minutes, whichever is soonest. It ends on the clock and not on my judgement.*
*What it does not permit: any share, any observation of the patient's practice, any recording of anything the patient did or said about how he works. Under no circumstances a technique.*
*Who may stop it: the patient, by word, at any point, including the wrong word. There is no penalty for the wrong word.*
*Review: the next time he is in the chair in this vehicle, out loud, whatever he says about it, and he says what he says.*

"I've read you that. You agreed to it in August. Do you agree to it now?"

"I agree to it now."

"Say it again, because you are a bad liar and I want it on the record as a bad liar being honest."

"I agree to it now," said Mal Farrow, from the floor, "and I want to say while we're here that the ninety minutes is the part I object to, because I've never once needed ninety minutes."

"Nobody has ever needed ninety minutes. That is why it's ninety minutes." Talia put the card back in the wallet. "Right. And when did you last—"

She got as far as *last* and Mal Farrow stopped breathing.

Not stopped the way a person stops. Stopped the way a person stops when the top of the chest has been taken away by something that is not a panic and is not a heart and is exactly as frightening: the air goes in about a third of the way and then there is a hard stop, and it is the same stop every time, and the body knows the shape of it and panics harder because of the familiarity.

It was over in seconds. Talia counted them out loud and her voice was the only thing in the room that did not shake.

On the seventh second she got the belt open and her fingers went into the plate pocket of the hip unit and found the return contact, and her hand stopped.

Because the plate was not where a plate should be.

She got the belt cut with a stitch cutter she keeps for exactly this, in eleven seconds, and the plate came out of the pocket in one piece in her hand, and it was not cracked and it was not snapped and it was not loose, and the copper return contact on the back of it was a strip of metal that had folded over on itself and set, hard, the way copper sets when it has taken more than it was ever meant to take and nobody was there to relieve it, and it was set hard, in the shape of the load path it had last held, and it stayed like that, and a metal which has done it once will do it every time. Which meant that the load had gone through, unreleased, and that the plate had stayed exactly the way it was left.

Marek said, "I've got nothing written and I want to be holding something."

"You are not holding anything yet."

"I know. I want the sheet before I do, not after, and I want it to say what it is for and how long and who can stop it, and I want Mal to hear it, because if he says no in the middle of it I have to stop, and if he can't answer then I want it written down that you decided, not me."

Talia Venn got a clipboard off the wall with one hand and did not hurry.

"Who can stop it?"

"You. Him, if he can speak. Me, if I put the bag down."

"Duration?"

"Until he can get a full breath on his own or you tell me to stop, whichever is first. Not longer than that."

"What stays?"

"Nothing. No echo, no trace, no use afterwards, and I'm not carrying it into a room with a nameplate on the door."

"Who's the person for?"

"Mr. Farrow, whose hip has just gone, and who is the only reason there is a scope at all."

She wrote it in a hand that was not steady and did not apologise for it, and read it back, and turned it round so that a man on the floor could read it upside down.

"Mal. Read it or tell me you can't see it."

"I can see it." Farrow's eyes moved along it. "Use for the breathing, ends when I can breathe or she says, nothing kept, and the lad can put the bag down." He said it back in his own words, which was the whole point. "That's a proper one. Go on, then."

Talia signed under it and gave the pen to Marek and he put his name on the other side of the line, and then he was holding something, and the something had a review date on it, and the review date was the next time the man was in the chair in the vehicle.

"Air," Talia said. "Marek. Bag."

"Which—"

"Ambu bag, green, second drawer, don't open the other one." She had it in his hands and then she put his hands somewhere specific and told him what to do, in words, like an instructor, because she had exactly no other option. "Squeeze when I say. Gently. Gently. Two seconds. On the count. Mal — Mal, look at me, you're going to breathe something that tastes like nothing and it's going to hurt for four seconds and then it's going to help. I'm sorry. On the count. One. Two. Three."

The bag went. Mal Farrow's chest came up.

"Again. And again. Don't let him fall asleep, he's got a mouth full of a plate he thinks is his ribs—"

Marek squeezed the bag and it was the worst physical sensation of his life, not because it was hard, but because the man's eyes came round every single time and looked for the thing that had stopped, and there was nothing there to be found, and a person in a clinic bay cannot find the exit either, and you can hear it.

And then Mal Farrow got his own hand up and pushed the bag away, and got a whole breath, and then another one, and then he swore at the ceiling with tremendous fluency.

"There," he said. "There, that's it. That's what I was doing on Thursday. That's what I do when it goes. You can hear it, can't you. You can hear me going."

"Yes."

"Then you know what it is," said Mal Farrow. "It isn't me. It isn't my chest. It's that there's no part of me that gets to say *that was the crate and not me.*"

---

The room went very quiet, in the specific way a room does when a patient has said the sentence everyone in it was thinking.

"Say that again," Marek said. "Not to me. Say it again to the room, slowly, the way you said it on Thursday, and I'll write down exactly what you said, and I will not put your name on it and I will not use it for anything except finding out whether the brace took it away from you."

"I'm not doing a thing for a board."

"I know. I'm not asking you to. I'm asking you to say it twice, out loud, in a room with a witness, which is the one thing you have never done, and you did it on Thursday on the floor in a loud market to nobody at all, and a man in a hospital right now thinks a release is something that happens to him." He said it fast, and then heard himself, and stopped.

Mal Farrow lay on the floor of a clinic in a bay and looked at him for a long moment.

"Once," he said. "And he writes it down, and if anybody uses it I'll have his."

"Deal."

"Once. Out loud. And he can write it down. And that's all, and the second one's for the doctor and not for the school."

Marek said, "Say it when you're ready."

Farrow said: "That's the crate and not me. That's the floor and not me. That's me deciding and not the crate deciding. I've got no words for it. I never have. I've never had to have words for it, and now there's a piece of metal in my pocket with the same job I had, and it does it wrong, and it will do it wrong every single time, because nobody is asking it anything."

"And the second one," Talia said, "for me."

"Once it's off, it's off," Farrow said. "Then the next one, when it comes, is mine again. And that's all, because that's the difference and it isn't complicated."

---

He was out of the vehicle on the step with his hands on his knees, and the badge in his pocket was still warm.

"About ten minutes," he said.

"Ten minutes and a bit. It felt like an hour and it was ten."

"Ten." He put his fingers against his temple, where the aftertaste was sitting, and it was not pain exactly. It was a smell. It was the inside of a fish cold store at five in the morning, and it was not his, and it had not gone away when the bay door shut, and under it was the specific enormous relief of a man who has been carrying a thing since he was forty-four and has just been told out loud by somebody that it was not him. "That's the smell. I can still get it. Can I wash it out?"

"You could swim. It isn't on your hands," Talia said, "it's the aftertaste, and aftertaste isn't a thing you clean, it's a thing that arrives and then goes, and it will go in about an hour and you will be left with a headache and a bad taste and a thing you did not have before, which is all I have to offer you this morning, and I am sorry about that as well."

"Fine." He wiped his face with the back of his wrist.

Talia Venn put a sheet of the clinic's own incident form on the counter and wrote the date and the time and the words *patient, uninjured, no staff injury, no equipment retained* in her own hand, and then pushed it across to him with the pen.

"If you are going to write down what he said, write it on mine, in the box that says *narrative of events,* and put the badge number at the top, because that is the yard and the number of the door in it, and if a man in an office ever wants to know where he was on Wednesday morning at a quarter to nine, that is the answer, and it is an answer that does not contain a word about how a man of sixty-one does his job."

He wrote the number and the time and a line and a half, and read it back, and it was the sentence he had promised, word for word, and then at the end of it he wrote, *contact found set under load; plate retained by clinic; refer to review* — and the word *refer* had a person's name in it, because he had written one.

She took the pen out of his hand, drew one line through that name, and handed the sheet back.

"Right," she said. "That is what I am going to tell you about, and then we are finished."

"Can I ask you what the word for the thing is, so I write the right one."

"No. You write exactly what he said. If you write my word for it, it becomes mine." She put his own access badge into the tray, the bad one, with a loop of tape, and wrote the time on the tag in marker, and tore it off and gave it to him. "There. You had it the whole time. He asked for it and I would have given it to him. He didn't ask. Take the sheet, hand it back in an hour, and don't go into a lab with it or to a board or anywhere with a nameplate on the door. This morning it belongs to a man on a floor."

"Badged and unasked," Marek said.

"You have learned that very fast for a man who was doing it in a car park three weeks ago." She held the door. "And the other thing. You wrote the badge number at the top of my form, which is correct, and you wrote my name in the last line, which is not, and there is no version of the fair sentence that gives the room the number of the door and the person who set the plate. I took the name off. Tell me I was right."

"I would have left it in."

"You would have," Talia Venn said. "And you should write down, tonight, in the smallest writing you own, that a fair sentence is not the same as a useful one, and that you are going to want them to be the same every single time for the rest of your life. It is the only vice I've got and I've had it since I was nineteen."

Talia Venn came out onto the step with the folder and put the second sheet on top of the first and wrote across the top of it, in marker, in his presence: **SCOPE CLOSED 10:05. BREATHING RESTORED 09:26. NO REMAINDER. NO THIRD PARTY. REVIEW: NEXT CLINIC VISIT.** And she signed it, and turned it round, and he signed the line under it, and that was the end of the share and the end of its paper, and there was nothing of it left in the world except a headache and a smell of a cold store at five in the morning that had not been in his life at ten to seven that morning.

He sat on the step of the vehicle in the yard off the fish office and wrote it in the book with the crane on the front, on a page with a badged number on a corner of it, and then he read back his own entry and found the sentence *he is not a source* in it, in his own handwriting, three times, which was a thing he had not written and could not have predicted, and which turned out to be the most useful thing anybody had put in that book since the Thursday of week one.

Then he went and found Farrow's apron on the hook by the scales and asked him one question, and the question was not about a board or a plate or a beat.

"Say this again about the two cigarettes."

"What, that it happens every time?"

"No. Say it as a symptom. How long, how deep, does it knock you out, does it come back on its own, have you ever had to be helped up off something." He had his pen out. "I'm not asking you for a report. I want to know if it's going to kill you and I have a reason I can't tell you and it isn't mine."

Farrow looked at him for a while. "You're not a doctor."

"I'm a repairer with a bad record and a bag of bricks in his head."

"Aye." Farrow considered it. "Two and a half minutes. Maybe three. It goes off like a switch and it comes back on its own, and it isn't a heart, and I've had it since I was fifty, and I went to a doctor in '15 and he said what I said." He took the apron down. "And you did not ask me once about the crate."

"No."

"Then write that down as well," said Mal Farrow, "and get out of my yard, you've been in it over an hour and I'm working."
