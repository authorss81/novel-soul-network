# Chapter 14 — Four Small Noes

The letter from the Continuity Office came to the Saltmarket route clinic on the Saturday morning of week 8, by hand, in a grey envelope, addressed to a resonance nurse on a mobile route, and it was one page.

Marek was not there when it came. He was told about it at half past ten on the Saturday morning by three different people in three different buildings, which is what happens in a city of two million when a woman in a vehicle on a market route forwards a government letter to three addresses she has never written to before, on a Saturday morning, with a covering note, and which he wrote down afterwards as *the single most useful thing anybody did for me this month and she did it in order to tell me to keep my mouth shut.*

The covering note was three lines long and it said: *You will have received this from us. We are not telling you what to do with it. T. Venn.*

The letter said this:

**CONTINUITY OFFICE — DISTRICT FIELD REVIEW — CONFIDENTIAL TO SITE HOLDERS**
**Re: local hold placed on Threadline Practicer Kit, Rescue Brace, rev 4-19 (service ref SB-4-19-03), at a public clinic, Saltmarket route.**
*1. The Office notes the hold placed at the above address on the Friday last and does not object to it.*
*2. The Office advises, however, that it is not able to recommend, and is not in a position to indemnify, any local hold placed under section 3.1 of the general practice regulations. A site maintaining such a hold does so on its own authority and at its own risk, and the Office's non-objection is not a finding and is not an assurance.*
*3. The Office's preliminary view is that the material in question is a commercial matter arising between a contracted assembler and its client institutions, and that section 3.1 exists in order to prevent local administrative action being read as a technical finding, which is a matter for this office and for no other body in the city.*
*4. Any further local hold placed at any other address attracts the same advice and the same absence of indemnity. A preliminary review of the service reference is scheduled. Sites will be notified.*
*Hollis Pratt, field assessor, District 3.*

And then, at the bottom, in a smaller face, which is where the whole of it was, in the way of a government that has been burned at least once:

*Recommendation to sites: continue current practice pending review. Sites that have acted are reminded that a local hold which causes an interruption of a public service may be raised against the site and not against this office.*

Marek read that sentence eleven times over two days and it did not get nicer, and on the Saturday he copied it out longhand into the book because it was, in his estimation, the single most useful sentence anybody had written about his case.

*They do not object. They are frightened of the last line of it. And they have put the cost of being frightened on the sites, which is the ordinary way, which is how it is always done, which is a sentence he could have written himself in week one and thought was cynicism.*

And paragraph 4 was the reason everything else that week happened the way it did. A woman on a market route had written to three addresses she had never written to before on a Saturday morning and put a piece of paper in the post, and it was not a letter of support, and it was not a threat, and it was the most useful thing anybody had sent him, because what it said was: *if you do this, you are on your own, and I am telling you that before you do it, and I am not going to be the office that stops you.*

He had spent three weeks looking for a thing he could hand to somebody. What he had been handed, in the end, by a nurse with a clipboard, was a piece of paper that told three strangers that the city was not going to cover them, and she had put it in the post before she had spoken to one of them.

He went to four addresses over the Saturday and the Sunday with three questions and he asked the same three in every room and nothing else, because somewhere between a cage and a fish office in the back of a bay he had worked out that he was not there to arrange anything and never had been, and what he was there for now was to be told.

---

**The first was the Institute**, and it was not a building, it was a cage, and it had happened on the Thursday, and the man standing in front of it on the Saturday morning was Deputy Anneke Roos, standing next to a new card wired to the bars with a chain put back through the hasp and a padlock that was not the old padlock.

"Read the old one," she said, pointing at it. "That's what was on there on Thursday morning. It is a lie, and it has been a lie since the Wednesday, and I am the officer of this department, and replacing a false card with a true one is not an admission. It is the whole of my job and I left it on Thursday."

The old card, still wired behind the new one, said: **TRAINING STOCK — 4-19 — LOCKED PENDING SERVICE. Access: department only. Equipment handling only.**

"I cut that chain at ten past four on Thursday afternoon with two porters and Darla Vint watching, and I read the words out loud before I did it, and it took nine seconds and it was the loudest noise in this building all term, and I have thought about it every day since and I have decided to stop." She put her hand on the new card. "That's mine. I want you to read it before I tell you anything else, because I wrote it in a hurry and I want to know if it is wrong."

Marek read it. **KIT 4-19 — LIMITED LOCAL HOLD. REVERSIBLE. NO FINDING OF VARIANCE. NO CONTACT WITH EQUIPMENT.** And in the corner, in smaller writing, because she was the sort of officer who puts the dangerous part where the light does not fall, *Responsible officer: A. Roos, Deputy Registrar.*

"Where did you get the wording."

"You," said Anneke Roos. "All of it. I sat in this office on Wednesday afternoon with a bolt cutter on the table and wrote down what I have been calling your sentence since you said it to me in a corridor in the middle of the term, and I put it on a card, and then I changed one word. Yours said *the department is not going to name one*, about a responsible person. I took that out and put my name, because the moment that card goes up on a cage a person is named, and it had better be the person who can be found." She took her hand off the card. "Everything else on it is yours, word for word, and I have not altered a syllable, and if anybody asks me in November where the language came from I am going to say that a student of twenty-two said it to me in a corridor and did not know he was drafting an instrument."

"You've signed it."

"I have signed it, and it is the single most expensive thing anybody in this building has done for you this month, and I would like it noted that nobody made me do it, and that I did it on a Thursday afternoon with a bolt cutter, and not in a spirit of heroism, but because I signed the purchase order in July and there are three hundred and forty people inside my building and one of them is three weeks out of theatre in a sling on the other side of the city, and that is the part of it I cannot put down." She squared the card where it hung. "And you did not ask me to. I want that noted too, because you have been in and out of this building for a month and you have never once asked me for anything, and I have checked, because I check."

Then she told him the thing that had been sitting in her file since the Wednesday of week 6, which he had not known about and which he would spend the rest of the term wishing he had known about a fortnight earlier.

"There is a file on you," she said. "It has been open since the Wednesday of week 6 and it is four pages, and it contains a corrected drill record, a school transcript with one line on it, a service notification with a category ticked in a hand that is not anybody's I have met, and a letter you wrote to this office in which you describe yourself, accurately, as carrying an unauthorized echo of a patient's practice. Four pages. In a term in which three hundred and forty people have generated nothing at all." She put her hands down. "I am not going to close it and I am not going to help you, and you should understand what that looks like from outside this building. It looks like an office with a four-page file and one signature on it."

"I know what it looks like."

"Good. Then I will not insult you." She took the bolt cutter back. "And I will tell you the thing that is coming, because you are going to spend the rest of this term working it out and I would rather you spent the time on the next thing. A preliminary review is scheduled. It will be conducted by a man who does not know your name, and it will be competent, and it will be fair, and it will probably be worse for you than a bad review would have been, because a bad review can be argued with and a good one cannot. I want that in your head now so that it does not arrive on you later."

---

**The second was the Cordage, Saltmarket**, which was an old rope works with evening classes in it and a man called Bo Halloran who was fifty-one and had spent twenty-nine years on the water before he taught anything, and who had done it on the Friday morning, half eight, before the day class, and who opened the door with his hip because his right hand was in a sling and had been for eleven years.

There were four plates in a bucket and eleven in a box, and a card in a drawer, and no record at all of anything, and the bucket was on a high shelf.

"You've not written a thing down," Marek said.

"No." Halloran put his hip against the frame. "Plates and card both, off the wall Friday morning, and nobody's touched them and nobody's signed for them, and no, I don't intend to, and no, I won't be doing you a favour if I change my mind about it at eleven o'clock tonight. So don't come back in a fortnight and ask me how I'm getting on with it."

"You weren't using a bolt cutter on your own life on Friday morning. I'll take the answer as it stands."

Marek took the notebook out, wrote two lines on the page, and turned the notebook round so that the man with the dead hand could read them upside down.

*He did it Friday morning, half eight, before the day class, so that nobody would be standing in the room while it happened.*
*He does not want a document. Say so here and do not ask him again.*

"Right," said Halloran, reading it. "That's the first time anybody's written me down as a decision and not as a favour." He nodded at the high shelf without looking at it, because he had put it there himself and knew exactly where it was. "That's the hold. Fifteen plates. They stay in the bucket and the box and they stay on the wall card, because there's a dozen fellas in here on a Tuesday who can't take the card off the wall or they can't do the job. You'll want me to say why. It's a dead hand, that's why. Twenty-nine years on the water and eleven years of this, and the reason I say no to a hold in a working building is a man with one hand who's asked somebody else to make his job dangerous."

**The third was the Ninth Street rooms in Nine Bridges**, on the Sunday morning, and it was Ines Sarr, forty-five years old, nineteen years in a corridor above a community hall, who had shut a door on him thirteen days ago and had remembered it and had thought about it, which was the thing he had not expected.

She had a rig, four plates on a shelf, a shelf of cards with the top one gone, and a piece of cardstock in her own handwriting wired to the rail where the 4-19 card had been, which said that the department's line was not being used in these rooms and that anybody who wanted to argue about it could come to a Thursday evening in the community hall above and say so out loud, in a room, with a chair.

She had done it on the Friday, at lunchtime, and then she had held the meeting on the Friday night, and eleven people had come.

"Nine of them wanted it stopped," Sarr said. "Two wanted a date for a replacement. I told them both of those things would have to wait for a date from the department, which I have asked for twice by letter and had no reply to either, and I am not going to have a room of eleven people standing in a corridor with nowhere to work because a building forty minutes away has not got round to answering a letter."

"I wrote to the department and I was extremely polite and I am extremely tired, because I have run this room for nineteen years and I am not going to be told that my corridor is a low-risk environment." She turned her head slightly. "And before you say anything: no. You asked me for a plate in this corridor thirteen days ago and I said no, and I have thought about that every day since, and I would like it on record that I was not wrong and that I am also not going to help you any more than I did then."

"I wasn't going to ask you for a plate."

"I was in your class on Thursday night," Marek said. "The night before you did this. I sat at the back for two hours and I wrote nothing down, and you let me, and neither of us said one word to the other, and afterwards you said you would have thrown me out and you would have been wrong to."

"You would have written it down," said Ines Sarr. "You would have written it down in that little book and gone off up Nine Bridges with it. I have taught in four rooms for nineteen years, Mr. Senn, I know exactly what a man writes down when he has been in a room. So I made a decision about you on Thursday night and I made it while you were still sat there, and then I did this on the Friday at lunchtime, and I want you to hear the order those two things happened in."

He said, "I heard it."

"You did not, but you will. And you can stop coming to the class, and I want to say that before anything else, because I know what a polite young man does with an invitation." She turned the card over. "Here is what I want from you, and it is one question, and then you can go. Why do you want the plate so badly? Not the case. The plate. What is going to happen to you personally on the day you get it."

Marek did not have an answer to that on Sunday morning in a corridor above a community hall in Nine Bridges, and he stood there for long enough that she filled the silence the way a teacher does.

"You will not," she said. "That is the answer. I have watched a room go quiet like that four times in nineteen years and three of the times the person had an answer and was not going to say it, and once they had no answer at all. And on Thursday night, in the back of my room, with a card on my wall that has been telling about forty people for four months that somebody else has to say the word, I looked at you and thought: he would take the plate off that wall tonight, if he could reach it, and he would not do it for the six of us. He would do it for the eleven days, because he has an examination or a hearing or a landlord. And that is exactly what the plate on my wall has been doing to six people since September, and I let it happen in my room, and the man who let it happen in my room is me."

"You are forty-five," said Marek, "and you have been the oldest person in every room you have ever run, and you have spent nineteen years being the answer for a room full of people, and the card on your wall was doing it for you."

"Forty-five." She nodded, pleased and cross about it. "And I have been running rooms since I was twenty-six, so you can imagine what it is like to work out at lunchtime on a Friday, over a cup of tea, with a cardstock cut and waiting for the bell, that you have been a key-holder for four months in a corridor above a community hall, and that the first person who noticed was a boy of twenty-two with a notebook." She put her cardstock back on the rail. "So that is why. Do not write any of it down." She went to the door and held it, which was a thing that had happened to him twice now in two weeks and which he was starting to understand was her whole method. "Thursday. Seven. And Mr. Senn — the eleven come back because they have nowhere else to go, not because I am interesting, and if you sit in that room and take a single note about one of them I will take you out by the arm, and I have done it before, and the one time I did it the man was forty-eight and a police sergeant. There is a plate on the wall. That is all there is. If you can be in a room with a plate on a wall and a piece of paper in your pocket and not want anything, then come at seven."

---

**The fourth was a white box on wheels in a lay-by outside the wholesale kitchens**, on the Sunday afternoon, and it was the hardest of the four, because the thing being held was not a crate of somebody else's plates. It was work that a nurse had done with her own hands, and it was the last of the four and the reason the other three existed, and it was the only one the government had written to.

Talia Venn had the shelf out and the letter on the counter with a mug on it to keep it flat, because it had arrived folded in an envelope and there was a crease down paragraph four.

"You sent that to three addresses on Saturday morning," Marek said, "with a note saying you were not telling them what to do with it."

"I rang them at ten past twelve on the Friday and told them I was doing it, and an assessor called Harbridge asked me for the lot number and the count and the address of the yard, and I gave him all three, and he said he would put it in front of his office the same afternoon. I did not have to." She straightened the mug. "And then I posted it to three addresses on the Saturday morning with a note saying I was not telling them what to do with it, and I went to bed at ten past twelve and slept eight hours, which is the first time since the Wednesday I was in that bay with a man on a floor."

"Do you know what the worst sentence in that letter is? It is paragraph four. *Any further local hold placed at any other address attracts the same advice and the same absence of indemnity.* She has written that to make sure that if three more institutions stop, nobody can ever say they stopped because the Office told them to. That is a person writing a document whose only job is to survive being read aloud by a hostile committee in five years, and I would like you to notice that I sent it to three strangers I have never written to on a Saturday morning, and that I did it before I had spoken to any of them, and that if you had done it they would have obeyed me."

Marek looked at the crease in paragraph four for a while.

"That is the whole of what I have ever been able to give anybody," he said, "and I gave it to you by accident, in a car park, on a Tuesday night, in the middle of the term."

"Yes," said Talia Venn. "And I have been working on it for three and a half weeks, so you should not feel too proud."

Two boxes on the counter. Twenty-six wrist units, and a smaller one of eight with the plate section cut and webbed into a hip belt, made in a room on this vehicle, on a bench in the back of this vehicle, by a woman with a pair of shears and a stitching gun.

"Twenty-six of the full and eight of the hip variant," she said. "Six of the eight are on people. I took all of them off on the Friday, off the shelf and off the six, and I rang the six on the Friday afternoon and I said why, in one sentence, in plain words, and one of them asked me to put it back and I said no, and I want it written down that I said no to a woman who was frightened and who is entitled to be frightened and who is going to be frightened again next Tuesday without the brace on."

"You've never told them what the fourth beat is."

"No. Because I have not known that there is a fourth beat. I have had a column on that chart since September called *interference noted* and I have written in it six times, in my own hand, in the ordinary course of my work, and one of those six entries is a hip belt I altered myself which did not let go when a man went down, and I did not write *it did not release.* I wrote *variable release under load.*" She put her hand flat on the box. "That is the entire confession and it took me nine minutes to say it and I have been not saying it for eleven days."

"You thought it was your alteration."

"I thought it was thirty seconds of stitching gun," Talia said, "and I thought the word *variable* was careful, and it was careful, and it was a lie I told in writing, in a chart, in my own handwriting, about my own work."

Marek looked at the eight for a while.

"You've put a note on the chart," he said. "*Limited local hold, clinic, Saltmarket route.*"

"I did. Friday, half past eleven in the morning, under my own signature." She squared the boxes. "One of the three other people who has read that chart since September is the Department of Health, and I have been thinking about what happens to a registration when the thing you certified turns out to have been an open end all along."

"So the note is mine and it is for me, and there is a version of this where that is a public-interest disclosure, and I have looked at that version very carefully, and here is what it is: it is a woman with a bad week making a small amount of trouble for a company and getting her clinic hurt. I am not doing that. What I am doing is taking the stock off the shelf and saying so on the phone to six people, and writing down that I said so."

"Which isn't much."

"It is exactly the right amount," Talia said, "and I want you to notice, since you have spent a month learning what the wrong amount looks like, that I have just been handed a small unambiguous wrong by somebody who wanted me to take a big ambiguous one, and that I have declined it, and that you can go and be the big version at somebody else's building."

She picked up the phone. And then she put it down again, and looked at Marek, and said the thing that closed the last door in the room.

"And Senn — you are not a person in this building and you do not get to be helpful here. If you have something to offer me, offer it once, in a sentence, and if it is a person and not a document, I will write down the day I met them, and then you can leave."

"I don't have one," Marek said.

"Fine," said Talia Venn, and lifted the phone.

---

He wrote it all up on the Sunday night of week 8 at the bench at Civic Spine 7, in his own hand, in a load book with a crane on the front, and he wrote it as four separate entries and not as one, because it had happened in four rooms and it was four decisions.

*Week 8. Four holds, four rooms, four reasons, and nobody asked me for any of them.*

*1. Institute. Roos, with a bolt cutter and two porters and a witness, on the Thursday afternoon at ten past four. Put a responsible officer's name on her own card, on her own initiative, in her own hand, and the whole of the wording is mine, which I did not know and would not have believed, and she is going to say so in November if anybody asks. She did it on her own authority and she said so, and she had checked the record and found out that nobody had asked her for anything, which I did not know and would not have believed. It was for her. Not for me. She signed her own order in July and there are 340 people inside it.*

*2. The Cordage, Saltmarket. Bo Halloran, fifty-one, right hand dead, eleven years in a sling, twenty-nine years on the water. 15 plates into a bucket on a high shelf. 61 students, 4 Institute-trained, 57 not. He has watched for nine years which of the fifty-seven put a hand on the crate first, and about eleven do, and all of them are over fifty and have been hurt before. He would not give me the bucket and he told me the reason in his own words and then told me to go and be right somewhere else. The omission does not get into people. It gets into the ones with the mark already.*

*3. Ninth Street, Nine Bridges. Ines Sarr, forty-five, nineteen years in a corridor above a community hall. Had written to the department twice and had no reply to either. Had pulled the wall card and put up her own, and held a meeting on the Friday night, and eleven people came. She refused me a plate thirteen days ago and had thought about it every day since, and she was clear that she was not wrong, and she turned out to be the one who was most right, in her own room, about me, and she worked it out on the Friday at lunchtime, on her own, over a cup of tea, with a cardstock cut and waiting for the bell, and nobody asked her to. Rusk trained Sarr in her second year. He did not train the eleven. Sarr has said what has the plate actually done in that room since before I was at the Institute, and it is being said there now because I said two words in a corridor. I am not going to her class on Thursday. I am going to sit in a room with a plate on a wall and not want anything and she has told me she will take me out by the arm, and she is forty-five, and I have decided to believe her.*

*4. The clinic. Talia Venn, twenty-six wrist units still in the box, which is not the twenty-six she has fitted since September — she counted those separately out of the same box — and eight hip variants, and the eight hip variants are her own stitching. Took all of them off the shelf. Took them off people. Wrote a note on a chart for her own registration, and said no to a bigger version of it, and told me she was not going to let me be helpful. Her own confession in her own hand: she wrote "variable release under load" about a plate she had altered herself, and that was careful, and it was a lie.*

*None of these four is evidence. None of these four is about the fourth beat. All of these four are people protecting people who cannot protect themselves, and not one of them knows what I am doing or why, and not one of them did it for me, and I did not ask.*

*Do not know:*
*Why Sarr's room had it in September and I did not.*
*Whether a woman who has been a key-holder for four months by accident is a better teacher of this than a man who has been one for twenty-two years on purpose. She thinks so. She has not been proved right about anything yet that I know of.*
*Whether there are four more holds I have not found, and who would put them, and who pays when they do.*
*What Talia's registration costs, and whether the six phone calls went well, and I am not going to be the person who asks.*

*And the thing underneath all four, which is the only thing in this entry that matters:*
*I have been running a building. For a month I have been running a building, with a file in one office and a card in a laundromat and a number in a laundromat, and four rooms have just closed the stock on their own authority and I was not in any of them, and the reason the building still stands is that nobody in it has anything to gain by pulling it down.*

*Which is a fact about the four of them and not a fact about me. I did not build it. I asked for the counting and I asked for the doors. The rest of it I have been calling a plan, and it has been working, and that is the problem, because it is working on its own and I have been getting the credit, and the credit is doing something to me that I do not like, and I have not written down what it is yet because I have not got words for it.*

He read that last paragraph back at one in the morning on the Sunday in a repair shop with the strip light off and did not cross it out, and it was the closest he came in the whole of the term to writing down the actual sentence, which was: *I have been getting the credit for a building that four people are holding up on their own, and I have not asked any of them whether they want me holding anything, and the day I do, one of them will say no, and the reason that will hurt is that I have been treating a queue of yeses as a decision.*
