# Chapter 17 — Nothing Is Missing

The scope was written by Ines Sarr, on the back of a class timetable, on the Wednesday evening of week 9, in the corridor outside the Ninth Street rooms, before anybody went in.

She wrote it because Marek had asked to and she had said no, and then she had said that if he was doing it she was going to be the one holding the pen, on the grounds that in her experience a young man with a notebook and a theory produced a worse document on his own.

"Read it back to me," she said, "and if you have written one word in it that is about what we find, we stop here and I will go home."

Marek read it back.

**NINTH STREET ROOMS — WEDNESDAY, WEEK 9 — 19:20 TO 20:40**
*Purpose: to find out whether four people can each do a release that is finished by the person under the load, and whether anybody watching four people can tell whether they are finishing it or waiting to be told.*
*People: SARR, I. (room, rig, card.) HALLORAN, B. (loading, one hand.) FENN, O. (third year, Group C.) PELL, R. (courier, no card, never taught.)*
*Nobody in this room is being asked to reproduce anything they were taught. Nobody is being asked to be right. Nobody is being asked to be kind.*
*Any one of the four may end it for themselves at any point, by word, without giving a reason, and nobody will ask for one afterwards, and it is not recorded as a failure of the technique or of anybody else.*
*What stays: nothing. There is no echo out of this room and there is nothing to take. There is no comparison with anything anybody is carrying already.*
*What is written down: timings and what a person's hands and voice did. The four names are on this sheet and this sheet is held by me, and the four names do not leave it. If any of the four is ever quoted anywhere, that is a breach of this sheet and I will say so in the room at the time.*
*Review: nothing in this room is to be quoted, used or repeated after the twenty-eighth. On the twenty-eighth the sheet is destroyed in front of the four of us and nothing is kept. If anybody wants to keep something out of it, they have three days to say so before the twenty-eighth and say it to me in front of the others.*

"Add one thing," said Bo Halloran, from the wall, with his dead hand in the strap. "Add that I'm not doing this for free, because I'm not doing this for fun, and I want it written that I'm here to find out whether I've been a fool for a fortnight."

"Then it's paid," Sarr said. "Out of my petty cash, because this room's insurance does not cover this."

"I'm not being paid—"

"You're being paid four pounds for an hour and a half and you are going to take it, because a man who has given two hours of his working life to a boy's theory wants a receipt." She wrote it in. "There. Now nobody in this room is a martyr and everybody in this room can say no tomorrow without feeling guilty about a Tuesday."

Rafi Pell arrived at four minutes past seven with his hood down and a paper hat, and said, "Right, what's this—"

"Read that," Marek said, and handed him the timetable, and Rafi read it twice, which he had never done for anything in his life.

"Somebody's told you to ask questions before you answer them," he said.

"Somebody's had it written down for him. That's different."

"No," said Rafi, and handed it back. "That's exactly the same thing, mate, and if anybody's told you different they've been lying to you for your own good." He looked round the room at the rig, the two porters' weights, the shelves of cards, the four plates nobody was allowed to touch. "Right. What do you want me to do."

"You do your step. Off the kerb, up a ramp, with a load. And then you do it without the wall, if you want to, and nobody's asking you to."

"I don't want to." He said it cheerfully. "I'll do the wall one. It's my step."

---

Ines Sarr did hers first, because it was her room, and because she had been doing it in front of rooms since her second year in that corridor, and it took her nine seconds.

"Forty kilos on the bar, load through the middle, nobody under it." She put a hand flat on the plate. "Alright. This rig is allowed to hold forty kilos and nothing else. It is not allowed to hold a person, it is not allowed to hold anything past the line, and it is not allowed to be the reason anybody lets go of a bar. Everybody off, on my count."

And then, in a voice that filled a room the way a voice does when it has been aimed at rooms for nineteen years: **"Scope. Off."**

Nobody was in the straps. The rig sat there. And for a moment Marek could not work out what he had just been shown, and then he could, and it was the thing he had seen in an old gymnasium with the nets taken down, and the thing that had happened to a boy on a wet dock, and the thing that had happened in a corridor forty feet from a falling crate: an instructor's mouth, on the beat, doing the work of an exit.

"It's not for me," Sarr said, before he had said anything. "Say it. You can't, and that's all right, and it's not an excuse. It is a release and it works and I have kept eleven people off ceilings with it. It is not the same as the other thing, because the other thing is *his*."

"Whose?"

"Whose is not the exercise," Sarr said. "Move on."

Bo Halloran did his next, one-handed, at the bar, in a way that was not a demonstration and was the best thing in the room.

He put his left hand on a sack of sand that was a sack of sand and not a rig, and he said, to the man in the strap — who was a nineteen-year-old from his Tuesday class who had volunteered and who Marek had not met and whose name was Conor — "That's a hundredweight and it's on the front corner and the front corner's soft, so if it goes over it's going over forward, and if it goes forward you're not going to stop it with your hands, you're going to put your shoulder on the upright and let it go past you. Your call. I've got you."

And Conor said, "Go," and it went, and it tipped forward, and Conor put his shoulder on the upright and it went past him, and Halloran's left hand came off the sack and hung in the air, and then he said, "That's off you. It's on the floor. You're stood up. Are you stood up?"

"I'm stood up."

"Good." Halloran shrugged. "That's it. There isn't a fourth of it. I tell him what it's going to do and I ask him and he tells me. If I'd stopped talking there he'd have been stood there waiting for me, so the talking's the bit, and the talking's the whole thing, and there's no moment in it where I say a word and he lets go."

"Mr. Halloran," Marek said. "If the man in the strap was on his own, in a room by himself, with the load. What would you say to the sack?"

"Hell," said Bo Halloran. "You want me to talk to a sack? What am I going to say to it? It's a sack of sand. It's not got a anybody in it. There's nobody to hand anything to, so there's no fourth of it, and that's not a fault, that's what it is. It stops when I stop talking, and if I stop talking there's nobody in it and there's nothing to be released." He looked at his right hand. "I've been doing it nineteen year and I've never once had a beat for it, and I'll tell you the truth, son: it hasn't needed one. Conor goes home tonight. Conor's been doing it that way since he was seventeen and Conor's not hurt and Conor's not a machine."

Odile Fenn did hers last, and she did it well, and it was the one that took Marek's breath.

Third year, Group C, twenty years old, who had written *scope: force only, no joint, no sensation* in her own handwriting on the top of a card in week 1 and had caught Tam's fall on the plate in that room and had not been able to act and had told him so and had not once used it against him. She asked for two minutes with the plate and the rig and a strip of webbing, and she set the plate into the rig and took a load through it, and then she stopped, and put her palm flat on the plate — flat, palm down, spread, on the copper — and read it.

"You can feel where it's going," she said. "Not the temperature. Where. If the return contact is taking it and you're not releasing, you can feel the direction before it goes. So you don't decide whether to let go. You find out where it's going and then you decide, and they're different, and if you do the first one and skip the second one you've just got a load and a shoulder."

She lifted. It was good. It was the best thing anybody did in the room that night, and it was the thing that Marek had watched a boy do on a wet dock in the rain with a crate edge under his palm and four fingers of a hand that had been on a sling since the morning.

"That's the one," Marek said, and heard his own voice, and did not like it, and heard himself like it more.

---

And then all three of them did it again, in a row, one after the other, at his request, for the comparison, and at the fourth place — Sarr with her voice, Halloran with his sentence, Fenn with her palm — every single one of them turned their head and looked at him.

Three rooms' worth of looking. Sarr's was a teacher's, quick, checking the room. Halloran's was a docker's, brief, checking whether the young man was about to say something. And Fenn's was a student's, and it went to Marek's face and stayed there for a second and a half and then came back to her hand, and Fenn did not appear to know that she had done it.

Nobody in the room said a word about it, because it was the most ordinary thing in the world. In a room with a man in it, a person about to be observed finds out whether the observer is about to speak. In a classroom it is how you stay out of trouble. In a corridor outside a kit store it is how you find out whether a person with a clipboard is about to say *no*.

And Marek Senn stood eight feet away with a timetable in his hand and a rule in his own handwriting, three weeks old, in a notebook, and felt the whole shape of it close.

* * *

> Three sources agree. Nothing is missing.

Nothing followed it. He stood in a room above a community hall in Nine Bridges and waited for the second half of a sentence that somebody had clearly decided not to write, the way he had waited in a treatment bay in Saltmarket, and nothing came, and the room did not change, and three people went back to packing away a rig.

The shape in the air was a shape with three points and no edge on it, and it felt exactly like the four seconds in week 1, and his whole body went for it, and he was reaching —

*Three different buildings. Three different trades. Three different ages, one over fifty and one a student and one who's run a room for nineteen years. Nobody in this room is paid by the Institute except Fenn, and she's paid by a department that taught all three of them. I have got three. I have got three and they are saying the same thing and I have never in my life had three and I am not going to let wanting press this—*

"Oi."

Rafi Pell was standing by the door with his hood in his hands.

"Oi. Senn."

"What."

"Ask them again." Rafi was not looking at him. He was looking at the rig, and at the plate, and at the three people who had just been through it. "Ask the three of them to do it again and watch where they go at the fourth place. Go on. I'll watch with you."

"What's the point of—"

"They're all looking at you, mate." Rafi said it in exactly the flat voice he used on a ramp. "All three. Every time. The teacher looks up like she's taking a register. The docker looks at you to see if you're going to open his mouth. And the girl—"

"I know what the girl did."

"You don't, because you were in it." Rafi pulled the hood down over his head, which was the gesture he made instead of swearing. "I've been stood at the bottom of that step for two year with my shoulder on a wall, and the wall's not the wall. You know what the wall is? The wall's *a place where somebody has to be*. That's all it's ever been. It takes a body about a year to learn that once somebody points at it, and it's the first thing anybody teaches you and nobody tells you, and I am twenty-one and I have had two year of it."

"Ruf—"

"Four of us in this room and I don't know one thing about the fourth beat." He was talking to the door now. "And the other three of them don't know one thing about it either, except the old one knows a word. And every single person in this room just did the same thing at the same place, and the same thing was looking at you, and that is a room. That's what a room is. That's what a room's for."

He turned round.

"I'm out."

"Say what?"

"You wrote it on the paper." Rafi nodded at the timetable in Marek's hand. "Any of the four can end it, no reason, nobody asks, and it isn't recorded as a failure of the technique or anybody else. So I'm ending it. And I'm not going to explain it in here, because if I explain it in here you'll put it in the book and make it into a thing about me."

"Rafi—"

"Two year," he said. "Two year I've been putting my shoulder on a wall because there's a step in that job and the step needs a wall and I never once asked where the wall came from. And you put three people in a room and they all looked at you at the same place, and you did not see it, and I did, and that is the only useful thing I've done all year and I'm not standing in a room being useful."

He went.

Marek said, "Raf—"

The door went. Somebody's bike went out into the street.

---

It went at about the second he stopped, and it went the way a thing goes that had been standing on one leg.

Three became two and the two were not the shape at all. He felt the teacher's certainty go out of it first, and then the girl's care, and then he was holding nothing, and it was worse than nothing, because nothing was a thing he had had in week 4 on the bench at the Spine and this was the shape of a thing he had wanted for a month.

The aftertaste came up all at once out of the other three and it was the worst load he had ever taken.

Nineteen years of a woman who had run rooms since she was twenty-six and had never once been told to be wrong by anybody who could leave. A woman of twenty who had caught a falling man on a plate in week 1 and had not been able to act and had lived in that for eight weeks. And a docker of fifty-one with a dead hand who had told the truth about a sack of sand for ninety seconds and had not wanted anything out of it.

Three kinds of tiredness. Three different flavours of *I was right, wasn't I*. And underneath all three, in all three, running through it like a wire through three pieces of the same bead, the thing that has no smell and no shape and is the reason he had spent an hour on a Monday of week 7 with his mouth going to a word in an empty building.

The smell of a fish cold store at five in the morning came up again, and he put his hand on the wall, because it was not his, and it was the third time.

"Dav—" Odile Fenn got a chair. "Senn. Davan, he's—"

"I'm fine. I'm fine, I'm—" He put both hands on the back of the chair. The strip light buzzed and the floor was the colour of a coin. It had been the colour of a coin in room two in week 1 and he could not, for about two seconds, place which room he was in, which was a new one, which he noted, and wrote nothing down about.

"You've gone white," Sarr said. "Don't go out on the landing, there's nobody on the landing. Sit down, drink this, and don't say anything for two minutes, and I have had this before and I am not going to have a fuss about it in front of three people I pay out of petty cash." She put a mug of cold tea into his hand. "Right. Now. What happened?"

"Four of you were in it and then there were three and then there were none."

"Which is normal."

"It's not normal, it went the other way, it went *out*—" He stopped, because there was one more thing and he had not said it out loud, and he had known for about ninety seconds and had been hoping not to.

"Say it," said Sarr.

"I was holding a line," Marek said. "I didn't know I was. It was three of you, and I was in the middle of it, and when he left it went, and I want to say the rest of it, and I am going to say it badly, and I want you to let me, because if I wait until I can say it well I will have written it down first and then I will have edited it."

They let him.

"Three of you are trained by the same man," he said. "All three. And I knew that before I asked you, and I wrote it down as a rule in a clinic bay five weeks ago, and I came in here tonight with it in my pocket anyway. And what happened is not that you did it the same way. What happened is that you all stopped at the same place and looked at the same man, and I felt the whole thing line up like an answer, and I *wanted* it." He put the mug down. "And the thing I was holding was me. It was not the three of you. I was the fourth point, and I did not know I was in it, and neither did any of you, and P noticed and left, and he is right, and I am not going to sit here and be sad about it in front of you because I would then be doing the thing again, which is making a feeling out of it and calling it a finding."

Nobody said anything for a moment.

Odile Fenn said, "Right," and stood up and put her chair back, and then stopped, and said, "Hang on. Senn. Are you saying I counted?"

"No."

"Because I did." She was not being dramatic; she was being careful, which was worse. "I counted. I have counted every day since the first Thursday. I counted when Tam went off the ladder, and I counted the day the plate came out in the dock, and I counted in the ward, and I counted when they told me the department record said *equipment sound* and I could not make myself go and look at the drill sheet for a week." She put her hand on the back of the chair. "That's not technique. That's just me not letting go."

"Yes," Marek said. "It is."

"Don't," said Marek.

"Don't what."

"Don't take yourself out of it. I already used you. The line reported three sources and you were one of them, and I said so out loud, and that happened twenty minutes ago in front of a witness, and I cannot un-report it by agreeing with you now."

"Then say the rest of it." She had not moved off the chair. "Say the rest of it, Senn, because you are about to go round that room and take me out of a count to make yourself feel better, and I have watched a man do that in a corridor at the start of a term and it does not end well. What I did in week one is not a release and it is not an observation and it is not a source. It is mine, it has a date on it, and it is a horrible thing that happened to me. And if you put it next to the docker and the teacher then I *am* a source, and I have been one for eight weeks, and you have had three this whole time and not one of them was me."

"Yes," said Marek.

"Say it out loud, then, and write it down, and do not write down that I told you to."

"I used you. It was wrong and I'm not taking it back." He wrote it in the column headed *what a person's hands and voice did*, with her name against it, and then he stopped and looked at the top of the sheet, which said the names do not leave it, and then he wrote under his own name: *and I was in the room all evening and did not say so.*

Odile Fenn read the two lines back, and said, "That's the first honest thing anybody has written in this room tonight," and then she said, "Don't you dare quote that to a panel," and Marek said, "I won't," and she said, "I know you won't, that's why I said it," and then she picked up her bag and left before anybody could thank her for it.

---

He did not go home. He walked to Pell Street and then kept going, and got as far as the canal end by the laundromat and sat on a wall at half past nine at night with a timetable in his hand and a load book in his pocket and the aftertaste of three tired people moving round inside his head, and did the thing he had not been able to do in any room.

He read his own rule back.

*Do not let wanting press two things together until they look like evidence. If I ever catch myself doing that, the wanting is the answer, not the thing.*

He had caught himself doing it, in a corridor in Nine Bridges, at ten to eight, at a moment of actual danger and with no board in sight, in a room where it could not possibly help anybody, having written the rule himself in the smallest handwriting he owned.

The rule had not failed. That was the part that took him twenty minutes on a wall. The rule had worked. It had caught him inside of about eight seconds of the shape closing, and he had felt it, and he had still gone looking for the seam, because a term had four weeks left in it and a panel in November and a boy in a sling who had asked for three people who did not agree, and because there was a version of the next four weeks in which he did the thing again with better people and it worked.

He had not been wrong. He had been out of time, which is a different thing, and it had felt exactly the same.

He wrote it up on the wall, in a load book, on his knees, in the light of a phone.

*Week 9, Wednesday, 19:20 — Ninth Street rooms.*
*Four people. Four scopes. One name on the paper who stopped it and did not give a reason and was not asked for one, per the paper.*
*Result: nothing. There is no comparison. There is no line. There is a hole where there was going to be a line and the hole is the size of the thing I wanted.*

*What actually happened, in order:*
*1. The fourth beat of three of them was a turn of the head towards me. All three. Every time. Nobody in the room but me had any reason to notice, and one of them did, and he left.*
*2. Sarr, Halloran, Fenn. Three buildings, three trades, three ages, three genuinely different practices, and one shape at one point in all three, which is a head turning towards the man who is asking. One teacher. Davan Rusk trained Sarr in her second year in that corridor and put her in a room at forty-five. He put Halloran in front of an evening room at forty-nine for two terms. He is teaching Fenn now, this term, along with a hundred and ten others. One man, one building, one card, one voice, nineteen years and one term.*
*3. I was the fifth thing in the room. Not in the scope. The paper on that table is four names and four bodies and one woman with a clipboard who can stop it, and I was standing in the middle of it holding the paper, and I did not tell her, and she did not stop it because she did not know. That is the actual consent failure of the evening. Everything else this week is a footnote to it.*
*4. P's step ends at a wall. He has known for two years that the wall is a place where somebody has to be and has never asked who. He is the only person in this case who has correctly described the mechanism, on his own, without being taught anything, and his reason for leaving is that he did not want to be in a room, and that is the correct reason and it is a reason about him and not about me, and I am not permitted to make it about me, and I am writing this down in the eleventh hour because I can feel myself about to.*

*Do not know:*
*Whether the three would have agreed if any of them had not been taught by him. I have no way of finding out and it does not matter, because a thing that has been through a door once is a thing that has been through a door, and I cannot get the door undone.*
*Whether a fourth person who was never taught anything would have seen it. One person saw it. He was not paid, he was not Institute, and he was not looking for it. He saw it because he had been standing at the bottom of his own step for two years.*
*How I find three people who disagree with each other. That is not a thing anybody has ever been able to ask for. There is no procedure for it. There is no card.*

*And the part I have to write down last, because it is the one that is actually the lesson and I have been putting it underneath everything else for two hours:*
*I wanted it. That is the whole of the failure. The network did not lie to me and the three of them did not lie to me and Sarr's mouth is nineteen years old and Halloran's hand is dead and Fenn's hand on that plate is the best thing I have ever watched anybody do, and the only person in that room who was performing was me, and I was performing for a board that is not going to sit until November.*
*A false agreement is not a failure of the network. It is a failure of the man who wanted it. And I have spent a month building evidence out of the fact that people are fond of me.*

He read that back, on a wall by a laundromat, in the dark, and did not cross any of it out, and walked home with the fish cold store behind his eyes and the smell of it going very slowly, on its own, the way Talia had said it would.
