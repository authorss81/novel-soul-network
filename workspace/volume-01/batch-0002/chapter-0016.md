# Chapter 16 — Two Voices

The twelfth line on the sheet said *nothing, I just stopped*, and the next three, all written on the Monday afternoon on a table in a room over a chip shop, said the same thing with one word changed here and there, and by the Thursday of that week Tam Arrick had written it eleven times out of eleven and had begun, in the privacy of his own head, to be proud of how consistent he was.

He got bad on the Tuesday night of week 9, at about half past eleven, on the fourth round of the department exercises, in a room over a chip shop, with the radiator going and a bag of bricks on the table that he had been ignoring for an hour.

The first two are for the tendon. They are flex and extend, in a sling, forty times each, and they are dull, and they hurt in a way that is specific and dated, and he had got good at them in the sense that they no longer made him swear, which is not the same as good.

The third one is resisted rotation, and the fourth is a grip, and the grip one he does on the edge of the table because the department wrote *light, at the edge of a surface, no load*, and light means light, and he has been a professional about light since he was sixteen.

He got to the end of the fourth one at about eleven o'clock and sat on the edge of the bed and did the thing he had started doing on the Monday, which was to reach the fourth place of a rescue sequence that was not happening, in an empty room, with nobody in it, and see what his body did.

Nothing. His hands did nothing. They stopped.

So he did the thing that he had been writing *nothing* about for two days, which is the thing that every one of the four department exercises is quietly not for, and which no instructor had ever told him to do and which he therefore had to do entirely on his own authority, alone, at half past eleven at night, with a sling on.

He said the word.

He said it perfectly.

Not loudly. He was careful about volume, because the room had a wall and the wall had a landlord. But in the shape, in the pitch, in the fall at the end of it, in the way it arrived in the room as a thing a competent adult was handing a frightened person — he said *scope* the way Davan Rusk said it in room two on a Thursday morning in September, and his body took the load off his own mind the way a key turns in a lock that has been oiled.

And then, without a pause, because he wanted to know, he said it the other way.

He said it the way Marek Senn had said it across fifty feet of wet concrete on a dock in the rain, in a flat terrified register with none of the training in it and the whole of the wanting in it, and that one went further into him than the first one, and it went into him further because it was the voice of a man who was frightened, and a boy of twenty lying on a bed alone at half past eleven at night is frightened, and being frightened is what the second voice is *for*.

He did it four times in the first voice. He did it twice in the second.

Then he wrote on the sheet, under the twelfth line, in a hand that had gone strange:

*17. Tue. 23:11. No bag. No sequence. I said the word twice, out loud, to an empty room, and my shoulders came down both times, and I did the second one twice and it is the one that works, and it is not a copy of his, it is him, it is how he sounded when he was frightened and I do not know how I have got a man's fear in my mouth.*

And then, underneath, in the same breath of the pen:

*18. This is not a fourth beat. This is the opposite of a fourth beat. A fourth beat is me deciding and saying it out loud and handing it back. That is both of those, and I do them, and they work, and the day I stop being able to do that is the day I know what I've lost. Write it down like that. Do not write it down like anything else. And do not write down that I can say the word, because a person who can say the word on purpose is not a person who has got a fourth beat, a person who has got a fourth beat cannot do that, I have checked both, and only one of them is me.*

He read the two lines back twice and then folded the sheet and got his coat, because a sling and a door are a bad combination and he had done it before and had fallen and had been embarrassed about it for a week, and he walked eleven minutes from Sennet Row to Pell Street in the cold with his arm up against his chest and his good hand on the rail on the stairs, and he knocked on the door of the room above the laundromat at five to twelve at night, and Marek opened it and saw the coat and the arm and the time and said, "Right," and stepped out of the way.

"You weren't asleep."

"I was doing your Thursday. Twenty minutes ago." Marek put his shoes back on, because it was the same set of shoes and there was no point. "What's happened."

"Nothing's happened." Tam came in and sat on the floor by the door with his back against the wall, which he had done once in April when he had come back from a night shift and had refused a bed, and which meant he was not going to be in the room for more than about ten minutes. "I need to say a thing out loud to one person and then I need you to not be kind about it, and I couldn't do it to you in a corridor and I couldn't do it to Deb because she'd have put her arms round me and started making me tea. So."

"Say the thing."

"You didn't have to say it." Tam looked up. "I want to ask you to say the word to me, once, in that voice, so I can hear whether it's still in your mouth or whether it's gone cold, and you're going to say no."

"Yes."

"I know. And I'm asking anyway, because the fact that you didn't have to is the only thing I've got to write down, and I need to be able to write down that a man could have and didn't." He put his good hand flat on the floor. "So. I'm going to do both of them. On my own. In front of you. And you are not to write anything down, and I am going to be slightly rude about it afterwards and I want that in advance."

"Do it."

Tam Arrick said: **"Scope."**

It was Rusk's, exactly, the pitch and the fall and the little lift at the front of it, and Marek Senn felt it go through the floor of the room he was sitting in and arrive in the back of his own chest, and nothing about it was a memory.

Then Tam said: **"Scope."**

And that was the other one, and it was a twenty-two-year-old's, and it had no training in it at all, and it came out of a boy on a floor with his arm in a sling at a quarter to twelve at night, frightened, in a hurry, wanting it to be over — and it went further into Marek than the first, exactly as it had gone further into Tam, and Marek put his hand flat on the boards because there was nowhere else to put it.

"That," Tam said, "is the second one. That's the one from the dock. I've been doing it for eight days."

"Tam—"

"You've got your hand on the floor." Tam said it without any malice. "That was mine. I'm not taking it back, I'm giving it to you, and I want you to have it, because I have had five doctors and a department and a plan and a bag of bricks, and the only person in this city who is allowed to know what is actually going on in there is a man with a bad transcript and a notebook, and that's not a favour to you, that's me being practical, and I'd do it again in a second."

Marek said, "How long?"

"How long what."

"How long can you do it." He said it because he had to, and because he was the person who had to. "First voice and second voice. How many times, how clean, and for how long does it work."

"Four and two, clean, and it never has not worked," said Tam Arrick, "and I did it in an empty room with my shoes off. And I can do it in a queue. I've done it in a queue at the market. And I know exactly what the first beat of it feels like now, and it's *getting ready for it*, which is a thing I never had before the dock, because before the dock I never once got ready for anything."

"Does the second one go deeper?"

"Yes." Tam said it like a man reporting a fault in a machine. "His goes deeper than the instructor's. I don't know why. Maybe because he was frightened and I only ever did it frightened."

Marek put the words down, because he was the only person in the city who was going to write them down, and he wrote them on the inside cover of the load book with a date and no name, and the handwriting was bad.

*Week 9, Tuesday, 23:11, Sennet Row. Not for circulation. Not for a board, not for a report, not for a person.*
*He can do both. Perfectly. On his own, at volume, in a queue. He gets ready for it now. The second one goes deeper than the first and the second one is mine and the second one is me being frightened at him.*
*This is not progress. This is the most fluent thing I have seen anybody do this term and it is a copy. Do not describe this to anybody as recovery. Do not put it in a form. Do not use it.*
*Do not know whether the real one is still in there underneath or whether the copies have taken the place of it. He is twenty. I do not know what happens at twenty.*

"That's the whole of it," Tam said, from the floor. "That's the bad night. I thought it would be that I couldn't do it, and I have been doing it all week in a warm room on my own, and I got better at it, and I did not ask to get better at it."

"Then what do you want me to do with it."

"Nothing," said Tam. "That's the answer and you're not going to like it. You can't help and you can't use it and you can't tell a single person, and if you try, then in about three months there'll be a man in a room with a clipboard who can say either of those two words, and he won't have earned it, and I'll have taught him by accident." He got up off the floor, badly, with a hand on the wall, and stood for a second getting his breath. "So write it down and keep it, and that's the end of what it's for. And don't you dare tell Deb, and don't you dare tell Rusk, and don't you dare tell the department, because the department would put it in a column."

"I won't."

"And one more thing and then I'm going." He was at the door. "The plan says I want three people who don't agree. You haven't found them. And I want to be quite clear that I'm not being difficult, I'm being specific — the whole of your month is a man with a hole in his chest looking for people who'll say he's right, and I have watched you do it to me and to Farrow and to a nurse with a clipboard, and every single one of us has let you, because we're all a bit busy and you're very persuasive about the thing you actually want."

"I am not persuasive about—"

"You're extremely persuasive about the thing you actually want," said Tam Arrick. "It's the only thing you're good at. Goodnight," and he went down the stairs one-handed, slowly, being careful, and Marek stood in the doorway and listened to the whole of it and then went back inside and did not sleep, and wrote the line *do not bring anybody a thing you got from a boy in a sling* and underlined it, and knew that he would not be able to keep it.

---

The Exchange kitchen at twenty to seven on the Wednesday morning of week 9 was a kiosk at the back of a room that had been a ferry waiting room for sixty years, and it had a back door onto the quay-side yard where the market's fish came in, and there were four cookers going, and there was a girl of nineteen on the fish, and there was a delivery docket, and there was Peta with a hand truck and forty boxes off a truck at the tail lift in the cold.

"Six-forty in the morning," the man with the hand truck said. "You're the one from the college. Come to do the boxes with us, they're not going to get themselves in and I've got to be at the quay by seven."

"I am. He's not," Marek said. "He is, actually. He's helping and he's not lifting and here's his piece of paper and I'm not touching him without it."

Tam held the paper out. It was a delivery docket with the top half torn off and the grid still printed on it, and he had written on the blank half with a biro, and the writing was the worst Marek had ever seen from him.

**T. ARRICK — Sennet Row — Weds, wk 9, 06:50**
*What this is for: to find out whether I can do the fourth place with my own hand on a box I can put down again, at a bar I picked, with nobody talking, one time, and to have it written down whatever it is.*
*What it is not for: not to get good at it, not for a board, not to prove anything to anybody, not to show anybody.*
*Who can stop it: me, by word. If I say stop, that is the end of it and nobody writes a paragraph about it.*
*Who is holding this: Marek Senn. He is holding paper. He is not holding anything else and if he puts a hand on me I want that on the paper as well, so: no hands.*
*Afterwards: he can read it. He cannot use it. He cannot put it anywhere.*
*If it does not work we do not do it again for four days.*

Dessa Kwan came in from the front with a tea towel over her shoulder, read it over Marek's shoulder while he held it, and said, "Well. That's the first thing anybody's written down in this room that isn't a delivery address."

"Are you going to say no?"

"I'm going to say four things and then I'm going to go and do my pass." She put the tea towel in the sink. "One: I read it and it's a good piece of paper and the fact that a lad of twenty wrote it at ten to seven in the morning is the only reason I'm going to stand here at all. Two: I am not your observer and I will not be your witness and I am not going to be the person who writes your record, because a kitchen is a hot room with a schedule in it and I will not have one day of my working life turn into a laboratory. Three: I'll watch, because I'm stood here and there's fish coming off a truck. Four: if anybody in this room says a word, I will put the kettle on the head of it and you'll all be able to hear it."

"Understood."

"Are you the fourth place?" the man with the hand truck asked.

"I'm a man holding a piece of paper," Marek said. "That's all. If he looks at me, that's the test failing, and I'll write down that it failed and we stop, because that's what it says on the paper."

Peta put the first box on the bar. It was a small box of roe, about nine kilos, the kind Tam had put ten thousand of through his right shoulder in a two-year career in a kitchen in a room that used to be a ferry waiting room.

"Right," Tam said. "Right. Nine kilos, that's not the crate. That's fine — the crate was forty, and I have been in a sling since the Friday before last." He stood in front of it. "I'm going to do it three times with nothing and then once for real, and the three with nothing are me deciding whether to bother, and if I don't bother then the fourth doesn't get written down and the whole thing is a waste of Peta's time and I'd rather do it now than at seven. And it is column two. It is column two and I know that it is column two, and I am writing it at the top of the page before I do it, because if I do it and I have not written it down then it is a rule somebody else broke."

He did the three empties fast, standing at the bar with his hands at his sides, and each time he visibly did not reach.

Then he stopped talking.

Marek had never in his life seen a room go quiet in that particular way, and he understood about half a second later that the noise had been Tam, talking, and that it had stopped.

The box came up. Nine kilos onto a shoulder, the body under it going through anchor and load in a line so plain that a man who had never been taught anything could have drawn it, and the hands came to the two points on the box and put the load there.

And then the fourth place.

And the right hand — the right hand, the one in the sling, the one with two tendons that had been repaired on the Friday of week 6 — came out of the strap. Not the left. The right, flat, palm down, on the top edge of the box, spread, touching nothing but the top of the box, in the place where the load was about to go wrong or where it was about to be fine, and it was there for eleven seconds.

He put it down. He put the box on the bar, with the left hand, on his own, and stepped back.

Marek wrote the time. He wrote it and the number of seconds and the exact position of the hand, in the grid of the delivery docket, in a hand that was not steady, and the whole of what he wrote was: *07:08. Right hand, palm flat, on the top edge, spread, 11 sec. Load stayed in. Box went down on his own. Nobody said anything.*

He did not write *that was a release*, because he was not allowed to.

"That wasn't a release," said Dessa Kwan.

Tam turned round. "Say the rest of it."

"That was a man being careful." She had her arms folded and she was looking at the bar and not at him. "I've watched you for two years and I've watched you do that thing with a hot pass, and this isn't it. This is you putting your hand on something to find out where it's going, and you could do that before you could write your name, and it's the most useful thing I've seen anybody do with their body in this building, and it is not a technique and it does not have a name and it will not go in a report. That's what I was going to say. The kettle's not going on. I just didn't want you writing it down as one because you'll get it wrong for the rest of your life."

"Write it down as both," Tam said.

"I'll not write anything down at all."

"Then write it down as both," said Tam Arrick, "because you being right and me being wrong is the only thing that's happened to me in five weeks and I would like it on a piece of paper, and Peta can tear the top half off, and Marek can read it in the corridor."

Peta looked up from the hand truck. "D'you want me to tear it, then."

"No," said Tam. "Leave it. He reads it in the corridor and he doesn't copy it and he doesn't use it and he doesn't tell anybody, because that's what it says, and because if I can't keep a promise about a piece of paper then I can't keep a promise about a plan."

Dessa Kwan said, "And for what it is worth, Peta does not read the top half of my delivery dockets, so you may as well know that what you have written on this is a piece of paper."

---

The fourth time, at four minutes past seven, with the truck beeping and the girl doing the fish and forty boxes still on the pavement, the right hand did not come out of the strap.

It did not come out slowly. It came out about a third of the way and stopped, and stayed there, half in and half out, and Tam's face did something that Marek had only seen once before, on a wet dock, in the rain, in a different year.

"Stop," said Tam.

Marek said, "Stopped."

Nobody wrote anything for a while. Peta put the hand truck down and went and looked at the road for a minute and came back.

"It's in the strap," Tam said, to the bar, not to anybody. "It got as far as the strap and it stopped and the strap's the department's, that's the department's bit, and I was the one who couldn't do it four days ago, and now it's the strap and I can't do it and I can't do anything about the strap because the department's physio wrote it and it's a good strap and it's got a light grip that comes off with two fingers, and I've got one hand that comes off with two fingers." He laughed, once. "So that's it, then. The hand works and the strap's in the way, and the strap comes off in about four weeks, and then we find out."

"Four weeks," Marek said, and could not make it sound like anything.

"Four weeks." Tam pulled the strap back over his hand. "Peta, I'm sorry. I can't do your boxes. I can stack and I can't take and I can't put them on the bar, and I'll do the kettle and the boards and I'll be no use to you at all until the four weeks are up, and I'm not going to pretend otherwise in a room where you've just watched me try."

"You've just moved nine kilos up a bar with a hand you can't close," said Peta. "Don't be daft. Boards and kettle. Go on."

Tam went and did the boards and the kettle, and worked until half past eight, and then sat down on a crate in the corner of a room that used to be a ferry waiting room and put his right arm across his knees and shook it for about forty minutes, in the manner of a man who has been told not to do exactly that.

Marek stood in the corridor for all of it, holding a delivery docket, and read the third line of it again, which was *not to get good at it*, and understood that in the space of eleven minutes he had watched a boy do the most difficult honest thing he had ever seen anybody do and fail, and that both of those were in the same eleven minutes, and that the second one was what the first one was for.

He wrote it up at a table in the room above the laundromat at half past four that afternoon, on a page of his own, and read it back, and then he did something he had never done in a load book, which was to rule two columns on a page and fill in the left-hand one and leave the right-hand one empty, and the left-hand one said:

*Week 9, Wednesday, 07:08–07:15. Exchange kitchen, Saltmarket. Delivery docket, top half retained by T. Arrick, in his pocket. Not copied. Not photographed. Not going anywhere.*

*What happened: he did the first three beats and then took his right hand out of the sling and put it flat on the top edge of a nine-kilo box and kept it there for eleven seconds while he read where the load was going, and then he put the box down with his left hand and stepped back. Nobody said anything. It is not a sentence and it is not a name and it is not a technique and it is the only time in eight weeks that anything has worked.*
*What it was not: it was not a release. D. Kwan said so in the room, out loud, to him, and was right.*
*Why it worked and I do not know: because his own hand is in it. His own hand on the load, at the fourth place, for eleven seconds. It is not a thing anybody taught him. It is a thing he has done in a kitchen since he was fourteen. On the dock on the Tuesday of week 6 it was the only thing that worked too.*
*The bit that is going to be the whole problem for the rest of this: it only works where there is nobody in the room who wants him to be all right. The only two times in three weeks that a hand of his has found a load unaided, there was nobody in the room who wanted him to be all right. I cannot check it. I cannot witness it. I cannot record it and I cannot be in a room with it. The one condition under which it happens is the one condition under which it is not evidence.*

*And the fourth time, four minutes past seven, the hand got as far as the strap and stopped, and the strap is the department's, and it comes off in four weeks, and the reason it stopped is a grip that comes off with two fingers and he has one hand that comes off with two fingers.*
*So: eleven seconds, once, on a thing he chose, with nobody watching, and then a strap.*
*Do not know whether there is a version of this that survives a strap. I do not know whether eleven seconds is a technique or a coincidence with a bag of fish in it. I do not know and I cannot find out, because the only person who could tell me is the person it happened to and he has asked for it to stay in his pocket on a piece of paper with a hole torn off the top.*

He read that back, and then he wrote at the bottom, in the small handwriting, the rule he had written on himself in a treatment bay four weeks ago, and then the answer to it, and the answer was the correct one and it took him until four in the afternoon to arrive at it.

*Do not let wanting press two things together until they look like evidence. If I ever catch myself doing that, the wanting is the answer, not the thing.*
*Caught. Twice today. Once at seven in the morning wanting to write down that a boy made a release, and once at four in the afternoon wanting to write down that eleven seconds is a technique. Both times the wanting was the answer and the thing was nothing.*

Then he sat in the room above the laundromat with the page in front of him and worked out what he actually had, which was: three lines in a courier's step and a kitchen handoff and a fish-market release, one of which he had been inside, one of which was a colleague, one of which belonged to a man who had said no to him twice; a plate that had set itself in a man's pocket and taken two and a half minutes of his breathing; a service number and a lot number and two blank lines; a four-page file he was not allowed to see until November; a boy with eleven seconds and a strap; and a piece of paper with three conditions on it, the first of which was *three who do not agree*, and the second of which was *he is holding paper*.

He had a month, a term, a panel in November he was not ready for, and no route in.

And the only thing in the world he had ever been able to do well was go and ask somebody for something, which was a thing that worked exactly once and then, like everything else in this term, had to be earned from the ground up in front of people who had no obligation to do it.
