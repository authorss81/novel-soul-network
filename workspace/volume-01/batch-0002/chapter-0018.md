# Chapter 18 — A Smaller Question

Mal Farrow was in the fish office yard at a quarter to seven in the morning on the Thursday of week 9, doing the same thing he was doing at a quarter to seven every morning, which was standing at the end of a bench with a knife and a cigarette in a bit of cardboard and being the only person in the yard who had not hurried.

"You've come down here three times now asking me about your four things," he said, before Marek had got the gate shut. "So either the four things have gone wrong, or you've come down here to do something else and you're going to pretend it was about the four things."

"I've come down here to ask how many."

Farrow turned his head.

"Ribs," Marek said. "In 2011, in the March, on the ice. How many."

The cigarette went into the cardboard. Farrow looked at Marek for a while, and then he did a thing that Marek had not expected and would think about for years, which was that he went and got a stool and sat down on it.

"Three," he said. "Two, and one that's cracked and not through. And the cracked one is the one I can feel when it's cold and the two are gone. And the doctor at the time said I'd be lucky to see fifty and I saw fifty, and there's eleven years on that and here we are, and the cough is a cough and it's the smoke and the cold store and it's twenty a day when I'm on and none when I'm off, and it goes for two and a half minutes and it doesn't take me anywhere, and the inhaler the doctor gave me is in a cupboard and I use it about twice a year." He put the cardboard down. "That's it. That's the whole of it. Nobody's ever asked me that. Six weeks — longer — you've come down here three times and asked me about a technique and not once about a man."

"You can write it down."

"You can write it down," Mal Farrow agreed. "It isn't yours. And you'll not read it out to anybody with my name on it, or I'll have your ear."

"Not without asking you."

"Ask me, then, and I'll probably say no, because that's the answer I give to people now, and you'll have to live with it." He picked the knife up and looked at the blade. "Now go on. You've come down here at a quarter to seven on a Thursday and I've answered a question nobody asked me, and you look like a man who's come to take something away, so take it and go, and tell the boy Arrick I'll see him when he asks me and not before."

---

Tam Arrick was awake and dressed and had a cup of tea going on the table with the plan from Monday, unfolded, face down, which meant he had been looking at it.

"I want to ask you for less than I asked you for on Monday," Marek said, from the doorway.

"That'll be novel."

"I want to read it."

Tam put his hand on the back of the chair. "Which of it."

"The whole of it, once, and I'm not going to copy it. And I want to be allowed to quote it — your words, exactly as written, no summarising, no paraphrasing, no making it sound tidier than it is — in something I put in writing and hand to somebody, for a thing in November, and not to put your name on it unless you want it on it."

"Why."

"Because it's the only thing in this entire case that is in your own hand and it is the only thing anybody in that building cannot answer. The department can say the kit is sound. They can't say *a man of twenty wrote down the fourth place was nothing, eleven times, and then wrote down that he can do a stranger's voice perfectly, on his own, in a queue, and doesn't know if the real one is underneath.*" He heard it out loud and it sounded exactly as bad as it was. "That's not evidence of a technique. It's evidence of a harm. And I need evidence of a harm more than I need evidence of a technique, because a harm is the only thing in this that a panel is actually obliged to look at."

Tam Arrick sat with that for about a minute and a half.

"Right," he said. "Terms."

"Mine."

"I know they're yours, I'm giving you mine." He turned the sheet round. "One: you read it here, now, in the room, and you do not take it out of the room. Not folded in a jacket, not photographed, not in a satchel. You have read it and you know what a boy of twenty wrote on a Monday and that is enough. Two: when you use it, you use all of it. Every line, including the finger, including the strap, including the two lines you just repeated back to me out of your own head, and if the part about the finger is in there then a person in a hearing room is going to hear that a boy of twenty wrote the word *finger* and underlined it three times, and if you leave that out then you have made a document and not a witness and I would rather be in the room."

"That's going to cost you."

"Yes," said Tam. "That's the point of it. A thing that costs me is a thing that happened to me and not a thing you arranged." He took his hand off the chair. "Three: you tell me the day you use it. Not the week. The day, and you tell me before, and you do not decide on the way to the building. Four: you do not read it out in a room where I am not there, because I am not having my handwriting performed at me by somebody, and if somebody wants to know what I think then I will come and say it in my own voice or they can wait until I feel like it, and I will feel like it eventually because I am twenty and I am not going to be able to shut up about a plate for the rest of my life. Five—"

He stopped, and the stopping was not dramatic, it was a boy running out of steam at eight in the morning.

"Five: if you are going to do this," Tam said, "then you do not get to arrange anybody else. Not Farrow, not Sarr, not a docker with one hand, not a girl in a corridor, not my mother. You do not build a room out of people who have not been asked. If somebody helps you, they were asked, and they were told what it is, and they can say no and still be in it. Right?"

"Right," Marek said.

"Say the whole thing back."

"You said all of it or nothing. You said tell me the day, before, not on the way. You said you come and say it in your own voice if somebody wants it. And you said I don't get to arrange anybody." He stopped. "And I want to say something about the last one, because I don't want to agree to it for the wrong reason."

"Go on."

"The reason I want to agree to it for the wrong reason is that on Tuesday night you walked eleven minutes in the cold with an arm in a sling to tell me you could do my voice perfectly in a queue, and on Wednesday morning you put your hand on a box for eleven seconds in front of a man you had to beg, and I have spent nine weeks building a case out of people who love me. So I'll agree to it because you asked, and I'm telling you that I noticed the wanting, because if I don't tell you then you can't hold me to it later."

Tam Arrick looked at him for a long moment and then said, "Right," and pushed the sheet across the table, face up.

"Read it there," he said. "And put the kettle on afterwards, because I've done the exercises and I can't get the strap one and I'm sick of it."

The clinic was in the lay-by outside the wholesale kitchens on a Thursday, where it always was, with the hand-lettered sign on the door and the fold-down step and the smell of a bus.

Talia Venn was writing when he came in and she did not stop, and she finished, and then she said, "Your review is tomorrow."

"I know. I have got a letter here that says tomorrow."

"Then you know, and you have come anyway, and I have forty minutes before the next slot, so say the thing you came to say and I will either do it or I will not, and I will not be warm about it, and I have thought about this for two days so I know what I'm saying and I am not going to be talked round."

"I want the scope extended," Marek said. "A new one, with a different purpose, and I'd sign anything you want and put whatever date you want on it and it doesn't have to be a comparison. It could be that I sit in the bay twice a week and you talk me through what the fourth beat is in your hands, and I write it down, and I don't take it anywhere."

"No."

"May I ask why not?"

"Because a scope is not a relationship," Talia Venn said, "and it is not a friendship, and it is not a thing that gets extended because a person needs it to go on being true. If I extend a scope for you, then every scope I have ever written is a scope I extended for a person I am fond of, and the minute that is true, none of them are worth anything, including the ones that mattered, and including the six I have written this year for women who did not understand what I was offering them and said yes anyway."

The clinic's diesel auxiliary clicked over into a second phase and made a noise like a bus pulling away.

"That is the reason," she said, "and it is the same reason I stopped you in a car park in week 5, and you were angry then and I would rather you were angry now than compliant, because compliant is what got a boy into a splint."

"I remember what you said in the car park."

"Then remember it harder, because the thing I said in the car park was that you were carrying a man's practice in your chest that he never gave you, and what you are doing now is trying to get mine, and the difference is that in the car park I was able to give you a smaller thing, and this time I am not, because a smaller thing would be me being kind to you on a schedule, and that is the exact shape of the thing you are trying to prove is wrong."

"I want the chart."

"No."

"It's his chart and you know it and I'd have to ask him and he said no, and you told me so on the Wednesday of week eight and I've not forgotten—"

"You have not forgotten it, which is why I am not going to insult you by pretending there is a version where I can release it." She put the clipboard down. "But do the sentence out loud, once, so that we have both heard it. What is the chart?"

"The chart is a record of what was done to a man," Marek said, "by a nurse, on a bench, with a stitching gun, in a vehicle, and the man has said no, and so it stays in the vehicle."

"Good. Write that down somewhere with your name on it."

He did write it down. And then he said the last thing, because it had been sitting on him since Monday, and because he had promised himself in a corridor in Nine Bridges that he would say things out loud rather than assemble them under pressure.

"You are the only authorised comparison I have ever had," he said. "And I cannot have you, and I am not angry, and I want you to know that I am not angry, because I know exactly what the not-angry is, and it is me putting it in a place where it is useful to me."

Talia Venn looked at him and did not soften, because she never softened, and because this was not a moment for it.

"Then here is what you have," she said. "One independent observation, and it is the only one, and it is on your own page as an event and not as a source, offered in a written scope on the Thursday of week 5, performed twice, use and remember, no derive, no retention by any third party, review tomorrow, and it closes tomorrow in the usual way with a line through it and a date. That is a fact and it is in your favour and it is worth more than a comparison, because a comparison would have been a fact about a technique and this is a fact about a person. Put that in your book and stop looking for a way to make it bigger."

She picked the clipboard back up.

"And now I am going to tell you the last thing, and I am not going to soften it either, and then I am going to call my next patient." She wrote without looking up. "What you are going to do now is go and try to find a fifth person. And you are not going to get one, because there is no fifth person, because the thing you need is three people who disagree with each other and the city does not have those, it has four hundred people who were taught the same way and eleven thousand who were taught by somebody who was taught by those four hundred. And you are going to write to the Office, because you have a file and a reference and it is the only door left, and when they open it they are going to say a careful no, and then in November you are going to stand in a room and say *I had one and she said no*, and that will be the end of your case this year."

"That should not be the end of it."

"No," said Talia Venn. "It should not. But it will be, and you should have the shape of it now, and you should go in with your eyes open instead of with a speech." She looked up. "And Senn — one thing, because I am not going to leave it. Everything you have done since the Thursday of week one has been arranging. Every single part of it. G. H., because you were in the water. Sarr because you said two words in a corridor. Me because you were in my bay with a bad record and a thing in your chest. That has all been you, doing a thing to a person, and being right, and the person letting you, and every one of us letting you because we were busy or frightened or fond of you."

"Am I arranging now?"

"You are standing in my vehicle asking me to make an exception," said Talia, "and I want you to hear how that sounds, and I want you to hear that I am not saying it to be cruel, and that I would have said the same thing to anybody else in this bay today."

She opened the door and let the cold in and called the next patient, and Marek Senn stood by the sink for a moment with his hands in his pockets and then went out into the lay-by, and it was the third time in six weeks that a woman in a box on wheels had told him the truth about what he was, and every one of the three had cost him something, and this one had cost him the only thing he had.

Civic Spine 7 was on night shift and there were six jobs on the board and Marek did four of them, and at half past ten he put a notebook on the bench with a page open in it, and went and stood at the far end of the bench with his back to the sink and waited.

Sera Quill came in at eleven, saw the notebook, came over, and looked at the page.

It was a hand-copied row in a careful clerk's hand, and a typed block at the foot of it, and a name he had never been able to place and had stopped trying to place.

"You've brought it to my building," Sera said.

"Yes."

"That's a thing you took off my wall at eleven o'clock at night in the first week of term and never gave back, and you have carried it into a copy room and a fish office and a corridor above a laundromat." She did not touch the page. "And you have now brought it here, to me, unasked, at eleven at night, on a Thursday, which means something has happened to you that has made you feel like you owe me a piece of paper, and I want to tell you that you don't, and that I would rather you did not."

"I know I don't."

"Then why are you standing at that end of the bench."

"Because you saw it in week one and you went cold and you have been going round the subject of it for eight weeks and I have been going round it as well, and I have been using it as evidence of something I have not proved, and I have got a panel in November and no technique and a boy with a strap on, and I thought if I brought it in you'd tell me what it is."

Sera Quill put her hands in the pockets of her coat and stood very still, which was not a thing she did.

"No," she said.

"That's fair—"

"It isn't fair or unfair. There is nothing in that row that I am able to tell you, and I have not got one thing to say about it now or ever, and if you come at me about it again I will tell you to take it out of my building." She took her hands out of her pockets. "And I'll tell you the two things I *will* say, and then you're going to pick that book up and put it in your jacket, because I have a night list and I have not got a second's worth of time for you tonight and I have not had a second's worth since the Thursday of week one."

"Go on."

"First: if that is a pattern, and I am not saying it is, and I am not saying it isn't, then it is not a school matter." She said it to the parts cage. "And a school matter is the only kind I have ever been able to do anything about. I have run this bay for eleven years. I have replaced four hundred return contacts and I have written every one of them down in a book with a location and a date, because that is what a return path is, and if a thing goes out of a school and into a fleet and into a butcher's and into a woman's hip pocket, then there is no bay in this city that fixes it, and the man who has to fix it is somebody in Crown Terrace with a form, and I have watched that form for eleven years and it has never once been the answer to anything I could physically reach."

"And second?"

Sera Quill picked up a marker pen off the bench and looked at it.

"Second, you came in here at eleven at night with a page out of a book of mine and put it on my bench and stood over there like a boy waiting to be told off." She put the pen down. "That's the first sensible thing anybody from that building has done in this bay in about four years, and I don't want it doing that to you. You don't have to bring me things for me to be straight with you. I've been straight with you since week one. It costs me exactly nothing to be straight with you and it will go on costing me nothing, and if you turn it into a transaction then in about two months you will not bring me anything, and I will have taught you to bring a witness when you want a favour, and that's a bad thing to learn at twenty-two."

She looked at the notebook on the bench.

"Put it away," she said. "And don't come back up here on a Thursday pretending it's about the page. Come up on a Saturday and do the backlog, and there is a backlog."

He sat on the end of the bench at twenty to one in the morning and ruled a clean page in the load book, gave it four headings, and then did the whole of it in one sitting, in the smallest handwriting he owns, and it took him until half two, and it is the shortest and the most complete piece of paper he has produced since the load book started, and he never showed it to anybody, and he is not going to.

**WHAT I KNOW — four things, of which two are a document.**

*1. A printed card says a boundary and performs nothing underneath it. The booklet, in eleven pages, says the word is a support beat and not a step in the technique, and says a learner must not proceed on a self-initiated release. The 3-08 manual says the practitioner confirms aloud. I can prove that by reading two documents aloud. I do not need anybody's help and I have not been allowed to say it in a room with the equipment off.*
*2. A plate sets its own return contact when a load goes through it and nobody releases it. Rusk told me he has seen it twice and once out of a fish crate. It happened to a man of 61 and I watched it and it took two and a half minutes of his breathing and I did not stop it by myself.*
*3. Four sites have stopped the stock on their own authority and the Office does not object and does not indemnify.*
*4. A courier's step has no fourth beat. He has said so out loud and he has known for two years and I have compared it directly, once, with a written scope, and he used only.*

**WHAT I WANT — one thing, and it is not in the file.**

*5. I want to be the person who found it. That is what all of this has been for, since the Thursday of week one, and I did not decide to want it and I have been living in it. I have a panel in November and a boy on a sling and a mother on nights and a scholarship that is suspended, and I have built a thing in the middle of my life out of people who were kind to me, and I did not decide to build it and I have been living in it, and last Wednesday I stood in a room and watched three of them turn their heads at the same place and I felt it close like an answer, and that was the most relieved I have been since the Thursday of week one.*

**WHAT IS NOT EVIDENCE AND WHICH I HAVE BEEN TREATING AS IF IT WERE — six things.**

*6. T. V.'s brace. A colleague. The same family of courses, the same twelve people, a lineage back to a woman called Vandermeer who is still alive and still teaches in Lattice. It is a colleague. It is not a second opinion. It was the first thing I ever had and it was company on the day I got it.*
*7. The hip variant. It is real and it cost a man of sixty-one two and a half minutes of his breathing, and it does not show that anything about the card is wrong, because the fourth beat was never in the card to alter. Eight hip units in a bag and six people without braces and none of them can say why, and a nurse has put a note on her own chart and I am not allowed to see it.*
*8. Mal Farrow. Not comparable. I was inside it for about ten minutes on a Wednesday morning with a written scope in my name on the other side of a line, and a thing I was inside is not a thing I get to use. He has said no once, about a report, and he was right, and he has said yes twice, both times on his own terms and both times at a cost he had budgeted for, and neither of those things makes him a source.*
*9. R. P.'s step. He has a wall, and it is the only one of the three where the man himself named the mechanism, unprompted, and he named it exactly, in a room, on a Wednesday night, while I was busy feeling pleased about the other two. He has not been paid, he is not Institute, he was not looking for it, and I have no instrument that can hold a person's words for six months.*
*10. O. F. She has been counting for eight weeks. She counted the drill and the dock and the ward. It is a horrible thing that happened to her and it is not a technique and she said so in a room before I did and she was right and I wrote it down.*
*11. Three people who agree. Not evidence. They all stop in the same place and look at the same man, and that man is me, and the man who taught all three of them the thing they stop at is the man who is on the panel in November. The network said nothing is missing and the network is not wrong, and I wanted it to be wrong, and wanting it to be wrong is the whole of my evidence now.*

**WHAT I DO NOT KNOW AND WILL NOT WRITE DOWN AS IF I DID — four things.**

*12. Who licensed the upstream bundle. Two blank lines, ruled the same day with the same rule, on a label on a crate in a store room behind a porters' cage, on the Thursday night of week 5. Blank is not a secret. It is a question nobody was asked.*
*13. Why there is no name, no date and no initials in eleven pages of instruction. Four explanations and I cannot tell them apart from inside a store room: a bad run, a card printed against the wrong revision, a supplier assembling somebody else's book, or a person who chose it. I still cannot tell. I am going to keep all four alive and I am not going to be the man who collapses them into the one I want.*
*14. Whether the same gap is in fifty houses in this city. There is a line on a stores form for spare stock issued to households in September. Ninety out, forty back. Nobody has tracked it since, because tracking it would mean a list. I asked Roos and she said it is not a scheme she knows about and that she is now going to have to think about it, and I have done that to a woman who did not sign anything.*
*15. What the man in a sling is going to be able to do in four weeks when the strap comes off. Nobody. I do not know, he does not know, the registrar does not know until the fourteenth, and the one thing that worked happened once, on a box of roe, with nobody in the room who wanted him to be all right, and cannot be checked and cannot be witnessed and cannot be evidence.*

Then, below the two columns, in the ordinary handwriting, not the small one:

*And the last of it, which is not under any of the four because it is not a fact:*

*Tam Arrick has asked me for three people who do not agree. I have spent a week trying to get three people who agree. He is twenty, he has no technique, two tendons in his forearm, and he has read more about the inside of this thing than I have, and on Monday he handed me a sheet of order form with four ruled columns and told me to be useful and not to be sorry, and on Tuesday night he came eleven minutes across Nine Bridges in the cold to tell me he can do my voice perfectly in a queue, and to give it to me, on purpose, so that I would be the only person in the city who knew.*

*He is the only person in this whole business who has given me anything that cost him, and he did it to be practical, and I have spent the last eight minutes of this sitting trying to work out what to do with it, and there is nothing.*

*So: I have one independent observation and it said no. I have four sites that stopped. I have a file I am not allowed to see until November. I have a panel made of one teacher. I have a service number, two lot numbers and two empty lines. I have a piece of paper in a boy's pocket with the top torn off. I have a good chest and a bad shoulder and a head that has been somebody else's three times this week.*

*And I have a rule in this book that I wrote myself, and it caught me on Wednesday in about eight seconds, and it did not stop me.*

He read the whole thing back twice. It was eleven hundred words and it was the first page of it he had ever written that did not have anything on it that he wanted to be true.

Then he wrote a letter, and the letter was the shortest thing he had ever written to anybody, and it took him forty minutes and four false starts.

*To the Continuity Office, District 3.*
*Reference: my file, and service reference SB-4-19-03.*
*I am not withdrawing my notification and I am not asking you to treat this as a fault.*
*I am asking for one specific thing: a preliminary review of the assembly record for lot TL-4-19-0311 and the four holds placed on it, and a view on whether the Office is able to identify the body that licensed the upstream technique bundle named at section 4 of the bundle label.*
*That field is blank. I am not asserting that anything is concealed. I am asking whether a blank field is a fact this Office is able to act on.*
*Marek Senn, third year, no licence, no standing.*

*Postscript, added at ten to three in the morning, in a different pen:*
*If the answer is that you cannot, then please say so in writing, because I am going to have to take it to a panel in a fortnight and I would like to be able to say that I asked.*

He posted it at the box on the corner before the first bus, and then he slept for four hours with his clothes on, and in the morning — the Friday of week 9 — he went to the practical department counter at nine o'clock and asked Darla Vint, in a completely ordinary voice, whether there was any news.

"There is news," said Darla Vint. "There's a man from the Office. He's in the department conference room at half past two and he's seen the file and he's agreed to see you, and he says you can bring one person, and I want you to understand that I have never in eleven years been asked to pass that on to anybody."
