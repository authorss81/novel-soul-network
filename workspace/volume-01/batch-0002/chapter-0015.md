# Chapter 15 — A Plan in His Own Hand

Tam Arrick's room over the chip shop on Sennet Row had a single bed, a table, a chair, a radiator that worked if you hit it, and on the table, squared to the edge, a sheet of paper that was the back of an order form from a kitchen supply company in Nine Bridges.

He had ruled it himself. Four columns, in biro, with a borrowed ruler, and he had ruled the lines twice in places, and the heading at the top said **RECOVERY — MY OWN — T. ARRICK** in capitals, and underneath it, smaller, *this is not the department's. If they want it they can have a copy and they can write on it.*

Marek stood in the doorway for a moment and then came in and sat on the floor with his back against the bed, because it was the only place there was.

"Read it," Tam said. "And don't do a face."

Marek read it.

Column one was **What the hand can do.** Column two was **What the hand is not going to be told to do.** Column three was **What I am allowed to be bad at.** Column four was **Who watches, and what they are allowed to say.**

The first column was a list of eleven things. Four of them were ticked. The rest were blank, with dates against each one in the margin of the first column, running out to a date five weeks out and then, in a second margin on the far side, the date of an appointment, with a question mark on it, which was not a question about his hand.

"They're not dates for the hand," Marek said. "They're dates for the writing."

"The dates are in the margins," said Tam. "The hand's column is what I do it on and the margins are when, and those are separate and I have thought about it for two days and I am not changing it."

He made Marek read the rest out loud, which took eleven minutes, and it was the plan of a boy who had been in a ward for five days and had then spent two and a half weeks in a room with a door on it, and it was not one single thing like a technique.

Column one said: every morning, once, the first three beats, on a bag of four kilos of dry rice, in this room, on the counter, at working speed, alone. And then: **write down what my hands do at the fourth place. In words. Even if it's nothing. Even if it's "nothing, I just stopped." Especially then.** And, at the bottom, underlined twice: **I am not allowed to write down what I think the answer should be. I am allowed to write down what happens, and "nothing" is a thing that happens.**

Column two said: **I do not start a lift with this hand. Not a crate, not a tray, not a kettle, and not to be helpful.** And then, in the margin, in smaller writing, *the kitchen's told me twice and I've agreed twice and I'm writing it here so that if I do it I have broken a rule I wrote and not just a rule somebody else wrote.*

Column three was the best one and it made Marek's throat do something.

**Things I am allowed to be bad at, and which are not a fault.**
*1. Doing the four department exercises badly. They are for the tendon and only for the tendon, and I have been calling them practice for two and a half weeks and they are not practice and that is not a criticism of the physio, that is what the job actually is.*
*2. Taking longer than somebody else. I am 20 and I have a plate in a sling and everybody who finishes before me is a person who finished before me.*
*3. Failing on the fourth place. It is missing. I have read the booklet and I have read the manual and I know what the department says it is and I am not going to write in this book that I have not got a thing that is not there, and if the department wants a version where a man of 47 says a word on a wet dock then the department can have that and I have read the same eleven pages.*
*4. The finger.*

And in the margin next to the fourth, in pencil, and then gone over twice in biro, so that it was the only thing on the whole sheet that had been gone over at all: **it does not move. 6 weeks. Ask the reg on the 14th. Write down the answer whatever it is.**

"Do not," Tam said, watching him. "I can see the face. That is item four and I wrote it on Thursday and I have looked at it every day since and it has not moved, not once, not a bit, and I have got the two other fingers and a hand that can write, and I have made my peace with it in the time it took you to get from the door to the floor."

"Sorry."

"Do not be sorry, be useful, there is a difference and it is the only one anybody in this city has ever taught me." He turned the sheet round and put his finger under the last line of column four. "This is the one you've been waiting for."

**Who watches, and what they are allowed to say.**
*1. Somebody who was never taught the card.*
*2. And I want to know they said no.*

"Second time you've said it to me."

"Because the first person you told didn't write it down. Rusk's not the observer. Rusk's going to be on something in November and he's going to be busy being fair." Tam took his hand off the page. "Read the rest. Under the two lines there's a list of names and crosses next to them and I did that myself, over three weeks, in a hospital corridor, on my phone, in thirty-minute chunks when they let me off strict bed rest, which they don't, so I did it in the corridor and got caught twice and got told off once by a porter called Winnie who has become the most important person in this city to me."

Marek read the list.

**Rusk, D.** *No. November.*
**The physio.** *No. She is not allowed to make me fail and she told me so and it was kind of her and it is disqualifying.*
**Odile Fenn.** *No. She came up to the ward on the Thursday with a box of biscuits for the whole bay and sat with me for two hours and did not ask me once how the hand was. And when I asked her whether she would watch me do it, she said no straight away and gave me a reason, and the reason was that she is on the same course as me and has never once in her life been allowed to stop a release, so she could not tell one from a fake. And she was right, and I felt like a twat for asking, and she is the best person in that corridor and I would rather she had nothing to do with me in a room.*
**Wick Oyelaran, St Anselm's amateur boxing, Lattice.** *No. Asked. He said to stop pretending I could do the thing in a ring when I cannot hold my own hand up, and that is the single most useful sentence anybody has said to me since the operation, and it has nothing to do with you.*
**Dessa Kwan, the Exchange kitchen.** *No. Asked. She said, and I'm writing this down word for word because I want it here: "I don't do it in a kitchen, and I don't do it for a boy, and if you want to learn a rescue release you do it on a table with somebody who knows what a table is."*
**Farrow.** *Not yet. He says when I'm ready he will do it for me, in front of me, and I have to be the one who asks and I have to be the one who watches and I have to be allowed to tell him no as well. I'm not ready and my middle finger's not moved and I'm not going to ask a man of 61 to stand in a room and show a boy a thing on a Tuesday in case the boy has a bad day.*

"That last one's a good no," Marek said.

"That last one's a good no and it took me nine days to work out and I have written nothing else down for nine days."

He turned the page over and there was nothing on the back.

"Now the part that's actually the point," Tam said, "and I have been saving it, and I would have liked to have got here on a good day and there isn't going to be one, so here we are."

---

They did it at half past two in the afternoon on the Monday of week 9, on the table, with a canvas bag out of the kitchen on Sennet Row that somebody had given Tam, and inside it four kilos of dry rice from a shop on the corner, and it was the first time he had done the first three beats since the operation.

He was slow. He was slow in a way that had nothing to do with his hand and everything to do with a body that had spent two and a half weeks being told *you are not to do anything with this*, and he got the heel down and the floor and he took the weight and he walked it up onto the shoulder — four kilos of rice, which is nothing, which he knew was nothing when he wrote the number on the bag, and which was the only number in the whole of the plan that came out of a bag in his own kitchen rather than out of a plate in a frame in a room with a man in it. and Marek, sitting on the floor with his back against the bed, watched the whole thing arrive in his own shoulders and had to put his own hand flat on the floor to stay in the room.

Close. The hands found the two points where the bag was allowed to sit and put it there.

And then the fourth place.

Tam's head turned.

It was about four inches. It was not a flinch and it was not a search. It went to the left and up and stopped, the way a person's head stops when it has found the thing it was looking for, and it found Marek's face, and Marek watched it happen and understood the entire architecture of what he had done to this boy on a wet dock in the rain eight weeks ago, in about two seconds, standing on a floor.

Tam put the bag down. He did not drop it. He put it down the way you put something down when you have decided to stop, and then he sat on the edge of the table and put his good hand over his eyes for a second.

"How many times have I looked at you," he said.

Marek's mouth was dry. "Every time."

"Right." Tam took his hand off his eyes. "Right. Get out."

"Tam—"

"*Get out*, Marek."

He went out onto the landing. He stood on the landing with his back to the door for a while, in a corridor over a chip shop on Sennet Row in Nine Bridges, with a chip fryer going two floors down and somebody shouting an order, and he looked at the wall opposite, which had a calendar on it from a chip supplier with the wrong month on it, and he worked out what he had just been told.

He had assumed — for two days, in a clinic bay, in a corridor outside the porters' cage, on a tram, in a load book with a crane on the front — that the useful thing he could offer Tam Arrick was his hands and his handwriting. A pair of eyes on a twenty-year-old's hands at the fourth place, and a pencil, and a record. It was, he would have said on Monday, a free gift. It cost nothing, it took nothing from the boy, it put nothing in anybody's chest, and it was going to be the one clean thing he did all term.

And the boy had just told him, in one line, standing at a table, that the gift was the disease.

Because a share is not the only way one person's attention gets into another person's practice. There is a second way and it is older and it is free and it does not need anybody's consent and it is called *being the person who looks up.*

He had been the person Tam looked up at, for eight weeks, on and off, in three rooms, and every single time Tam had reached the fourth place his body had gone to Marek's face, because Marek's face was the place the answer used to be, and Marek had known that, in the way he knew the shape of a hole in a practice, and had gone on offering himself in the room anyway, because being in the room was the whole of what he had to give, and because a man who cannot be useful will at least be present.

Marek Senn stood on a landing above a chip shop and found that he had been the key-holder of a room with nothing in it, on purpose, for eleven days, out of the best intentions available to him.

He knocked.

"Am I coming back in."

A pause. "You can. Bring the book. Sit at the table, not on the floor, and don't look at my hands when I do it, and if I look at you, write down the time and how long it took, and that's all, and then go and stand in the corridor. Because if you're in the room, the fourth place is you, and everything I do after that is a boy performing for a person, and I will not be able to tell the difference between getting better and getting better at being looked at."

He came back in. He took the book. He sat at the table. He wrote the time, 14:41, and he wrote, above the time, in his own hand and underlined once, *broke column two, four kilos, not a lift, writing it down so it is a rule I broke and not a rule somebody else wrote,* and then he wrote the time, and he wrote *looked at me, held for about a second and a half, at the fourth place, bag on the shoulder*, and he wrote *no fourth beat, nothing, hands did not move, I stopped* — and then he read that last line back and had to stop for a second, because the boy had written a plan in four columns and had spent two and a half weeks making a thing that was going to hurt him in an honest direction, and Marek was the one being asked to watch.

"Go and stand in the corridor," Tam said, not looking at him. "I'll shout you in when I've done the other three. It'll take me a while, because I'm doing the bag four times and then I'm writing it down and my handwriting's worse than yours."

Marek went and stood in the corridor for thirty-one minutes.

He heard the whole of it through the door and most of it was the sound of a bag of rice being put on a table forty times, and four times in the middle of it a chair went back and a boy swore at a radiator. At the end of it Tam shouted, and Marek came in, and the sheet of paper had four new lines on it and the four lines were almost all *nothing, I just stopped*, and one of them said *nothing, I just stopped, and I looked at you, and it took less time*, and the last one said *nothing, and I did not look, and I do not know why and I am not going to write a reason.*

"That one," Tam said, jabbing it. "That's the whole page. Don't you put that in your book."

"I'm not putting it in my book."

"You can put the times in your book. You cannot put that one in your book. If that goes in a book it's a thing somebody said about me and I only said it to myself." He took the sheet and folded it once and put it under the pillow, which is where a boy keeps a document he is not yet prepared to lose. "Right. Now go and do your Thursday and don't tell anybody about any of it, and don't ask me tomorrow how it went, because I will tell you if it went."

---

He did the Thursday. It was the second week of paid invigilation and it was fourteen credits, and there were nine third-years in room two working a 4-19 card that a man in front of the room said the word for, and Marek sat at the back with a clipboard he did not need and wrote down the times because the department paid him by the evening and the evening had to be justified, and at twenty to nine he counted nine releases and nine instances of the word coming out of a man standing in a teaching position at the front of a room, and it was exactly as bad as he had said it would be, and it was not a single new thing.

At nine he walked to the kitchen on Sennet Row and helped the cook put the Thursday close down, for money, at the rate, and on the way out the head cook, a woman of about fifty called Deb with a ledger and no face, said:

"You've been in all week."

"Since the Tuesday before last."

"Since the Tuesday before last." She wrote something in the ledger. "He's not on the board. You know that, right. He's not on any board here and he hasn't been since the week he went in. I kept him on the books as long as I could because he's got no wage and I couldn't have him doing nothing, and last week I had to take him off because the fella above me said I was carrying a liability on a set of books, and the man said the word *liability* out loud, in a kitchen, about a lad of twenty with a sling on."

"Sorry."

"Don't be sorry, be useful." Deb laughed, and it was not a cruel laugh, and then it stopped. "But you can tell him, and you tell him from me and not from yourself, because if he hears it off you he'll do something stupid like apologise. Tell him the kitchen's had him and it's not a favour and it's not a debt, and tell him to come back in September and be on the board before I book the kitchen again, because by September he'll be the only one of my three who can do a hot pass in his left hand and I'll be having to pay him for it."

The ice was out and the water was running and the fryer was going and a boy of twenty was standing at the end of a pass with his good hand on the rail, waiting for a man to be sick behind a chip shop, and he had not been sick, because a man standing on a landing above him had said *get out* and meant it, and then had done what the boy asked, and then had come back in and written down the time.

"Deb," Marek said, when he got to the end of the pass. "Arrick. He's twenty. You get to say the word to a person of twenty and have it land like that, and I've watched you do it to two cooks tonight who are forty, and I'd like to be the one who tells him, if that's all right."

"That is exactly all right," said Deb. "You're not the only one who's noticed. Tell him and tell him I'll be the one who has to live in this kitchen if I don't."

Then he walked back up the landing and knocked on a door and said, "I've got to tell you something from Deb and I'm not doing it for me, it's for her, and it's one sentence, and you can do what you want with it."

He did the one sentence.

Tam Arrick stood in the middle of his own room in a sling with a sheet of order form under his pillow and listened to the whole thing, and then said, "Right," and then said, "that's the first thing anybody has said to me in two and a half weeks that was not about my hand," and then, after a second, in a completely different voice, "which means you can't use it, can you."

"No," Marek said. "She won't let me and I wouldn't want to."

"Right." Tam went and sat on the bed. "That's the second thing in a week somebody's said no to you where the no was worth more than the yes, and I want you to notice that, because I think you're the only man in this city who hasn't."

Then he said: "You know what the problem with your three people is."

"Which three."

"Whatever three you get." Tam pulled the sheet out from under the pillow and smoothed it on the table. "You want to bring me three people who do a thing a certain way. And then you're going to put them in a row, and they're going to agree with each other, and then you're going to write *three independent sources agree* and you'll believe it, because you have been alone with a hole in your chest for eight weeks and three people agreeing is the only thing on earth that would make you feel better."

Marek did not say anything.

"I'm not saying you're wrong about needing three. I think you need three. I'm saying three is the number of people who will agree with you if you ask nicely, and a man who takes that to a board in November gets laughed at, and worse than laughed at, he gets believed, and then a real technique that was tested by a family gets shut down because a student with a bad record found three people in a room who thought the same thing." Tam put his finger on the plan. "So no. Not three. Get me three who do not agree."

"That is a completely different thing to ask for."

"Yes," said Tam Arrick. "That's why I wrote it down."
