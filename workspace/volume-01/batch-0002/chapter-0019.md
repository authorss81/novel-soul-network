# Chapter 19 — A Commercial Matter

Hollis Pratt came to the practical department in a grey coat at twenty past two on the Friday of week 9 with a document case and a folding rule in his pocket, and he counted the chairs in the conference room before he sat down, which is a habit of his and not a thing he did on purpose.

He was fifty-one. He had a face that had been in front of a lot of people who were frightened, and he had the specific tiredness of a man who had come to do a job he did not agree with and had decided, somewhere in a taxi, to do it properly anyway.

"Sit where you like," he said. "I've got two forms and one piece of paper I've read about four times and I'd like to start with what I think, and then I'll take what you've got, and then I'll tell you what I can and can't do, in that order, so that nothing in this room surprises anybody."

Marek sat down. Darla Vint stayed, because she had said she would and because nobody had told her not to.

"Right," said Hollis Pratt. "First, what I think."

He put the piece of paper on the table and turned it round so both of them could read it.

"One thing before I do, so that the time on your letter is not sitting in the back of my mind all afternoon. I am not here because of your letter. I am here because a clinic rang my office at ten past twelve on the Friday and told me she had taken twenty-six wrist units and eight hip variants off six people, and a department, an evening class and an annexe have each put a local hold on the same lot since, and none of them told me, and all four of them had a duty to. I have to come and look at four holds and write four sentences about them. Your letter came in the internal post at nine this morning and I read it on the train, and I want to be able to tell you honestly that I would have been in this room at half past two whatever it said."

"Then I have not made your morning."

"You have made my Friday," said Pratt. "Which is a different thing and I will come back to it."

He put the piece of paper down again and put a finger on it.

"I have read your notification, your follow-up letter of the eleventh, the department's file, four pages, the corrected drill record, the transcript line, the two photographs of a bundle label, and a longhand crate count of twenty-four plates across nine crates." He put a finger on the paper. "I am going to say three sentences about that and I am going to say them slowly, because they are the only three sentences in this room that I have the authority to say, and I would like them on the record whatever happens after."

"Go on."

"One: I accept that the printed card for revision 4-19 contains a boundary label with no performed boundary-release beneath it, and that the accompanying instructor notes describe the instructor's spoken cue as the expected completion of that beat. That is a documentary fact and you do not need me for it and you should stop asking me for it." He looked up. "Two: I accept that a plate can set its own return contact under load when no release is performed, and that this is a known failure mode which appears in our own guidance as *boundary not performed, contact set*. Three: I accept that four sites have placed a limited local hold on stock from assembly lot TL-4-19-0311, and that none of them did so at my request or on my authority."

"That was the easy part," Marek said.

"Yes. It was." Pratt took the two forms and squared them. "Now the part you have been waiting three weeks for, and I am going to give it to you at half past three in the afternoon with a room full of witnesses rather than at eleven at night on a stairwell, because that is what I am for."

---

Petar Vance was in the corridor outside the conference room at ten past three with a service case and a bad coat, waiting to go in, because Threadline serviced the plates at the Institute and a field assessor had to see the engineer.

Marek said, "Petar."

"Senn." Vance did not look pleased. "I've got forty minutes and then I've got to be at the Cordage, and I want to say one thing to you outside a room and then I'm going in and I'm going to be professional."

"Go on."

"I asked you about the wax paper." Vance shifted the case. "Twice. The Wednesday of week six and the Friday, in a store room, with the cage open, and both times you said *I don't remember*, and I have written both of those down, because I write everything down, because if a crate comes back with the wax off it and I can't say who pulled it off then it's my name on the warranty and not the department's."

"You did write it down."

"I did. And I've told the depot, so that's on the record, and if you turn up in November and say somebody pulled the paper off, the depot will produce two lines in a book with your initials against both of them, and a man with two initials in a book is worth nothing to anybody." He looked at the door. "So: if you remember something about that wax paper between now and then, you come and find me before I go to the depot on the twenty-eighth, and you tell me yourself, in a room, with a witness, and I will write *volunteered* next to it. That's all I want. Everything else I've got."

Marek looked at him.

"Petar — before I go in. Do you want to be told something before the twenty-eighth, or would you rather I didn't?"

Vance stopped in the corridor with the case in his hand.

"That's a question," he said.

"That's the question. If you say yes, I'll come and find you in a room with a witness, before you go to the depot, and I'll tell you what I remember, and you'll write *volunteered* next to it, and if you say no, I won't, and I won't come and find you."

Vance put the case down on the floor.

"Right," he said. "Well. That's a thing you could have said in a store room in September and didn't, and I'm not going to give you a lecture about it because I'm a service engineer and not a priest, and I've got forty minutes." He picked the case up. "Twenty-eighth. Before I go. And bring nothing, just bring yourself."

---

Hollis Pratt had the Threadline answer by then, folded in three, on a photocopy with a stamp on it that said COPY — TELELEX, and he did not have to look for it.

"I'll read it out," he said, "because you should hear it, and because I have been in this job long enough to know that a person who has never heard the actual words assumes they are worse than they are. This is a copy off the depot telex, which is how it came back to me in four hours, because that is what happens when a field on a form is empty. The letter on their letterhead will arrive in the post in three to five weeks and it will say exactly this and it will be signed by a director." He unfolded it. Four paragraphs on depot letterhead. "The 19th: *The bundle was assembled by Threadline Service Depot, Lattice, from a supplier pack received under our standard component intake. The supplier pack for that intake carried no originating technique author and no licence reference at section 4. Threadline is an assembler and distributor and does not hold the source documentation for the technique content. We have no further information as to origin and we are unable to assist further in the absence of a defect claim.*"

They sat with it.

"Read it again," Marek said. "Section 4."

"*The supplier pack for that intake carried no originating technique author and no licence reference at section 4.*" Pratt folded it back up. "It is true. I have checked it against the intake summary, which I am not permitted to release and which also does not carry a name at section 4. They assembled it out of a pack that arrived without one, and they have told us so, and they cannot help, and the reason they cannot help is not concealment, it is that there is nothing there to conceal."

"So the man who wrote the fourth beat out—"

"Is not named on anything in this city that this office has been able to reach, and I am not going to tell you he exists, and I am not going to tell you he doesn't, and I am going to tell you the reason I am not going to do either of those things, and it is the only reason in the whole of this afternoon that is mine."

Pratt took the folding rule out of his pocket and put it on the table, and then did not use it, which Marek understood later was the tell.

"If I identify one licensor, I have to be able to identify any licensor," he said. "That is how it works and it is not negotiable and it is not a matter of courage. The moment this office finds one blank field it can act on, every blank field in Nacre is an act of omission by this office, and there are a great many of them, and they are not a conspiracy, they are a form that nobody filled in for forty years because nobody made anybody fill it in."

"How many?"

"I counted them myself," said Hollis Pratt. "On a Sunday, in the register, with a pencil, because I wanted to know what I was being asked to be the first of. There are four thousand one hundred and six technique bundles registered to institutions, public bodies and holders in this district. Nine hundred and forty of them have a blank upstream licensor field. Two hundred and eleven of those have been revised at least once since registration. I did that in a day and a half with a pencil, and I would have had to do it again every year after that, for the rest of my working life, with four assessors, and there is a version of that where I get it wrong in a way that ends up in a newspaper with a photograph of this building on it."

He put his hands flat on the forms.

"I am not frightened of the licensor," he said. "I want to be exact, because I know how this reads. I am frightened of the precedent, and the precedent is a very large one, and it is *one*, and it does not require anybody to be a villain, and that is the whole difficulty. If I trace this one, then every institution in this city with a blank section 4 has been let down by an office that had the power and did not use it, and there are nine hundred and forty of them, and about a hundred of those are clinics."

"Then what *can* you do," said Marek.

Hollis Pratt turned a form round and wrote on it.

"Four things, and I am going to do all four of them before I leave this building, and two of them are already done." He wrote as he spoke. "One: the preliminary review of the service reference SB-4-19-03 is recorded, and this office does not object to the four local holds and does not instruct any site to lift them, and I will put that in writing today and it will go to four addresses and it will be binding on me and not on them, which means each of those four people has to keep saying no in their own name every time somebody asks them, and I am not going to pretend to them that I have taken it off their shoulders." He wrote. "Two: the intake summary for lot TL-4-19-0311 carries no licensor at section 4, and I am recording that as a fact about a document rather than as a conclusion about a person, and I am recording it in a form that a person can read."

"That's thin."

"It is extremely thin," said Pratt, "and it is what I am permitted to write, and I would rather write something thin that is true than something thick that a district office can overrule in six months."

"And three."

"Three: I have put a question to the vendor through the ordinary channel, and I have the answer, and I have just read it to you, and the signed version of it will be on this desk in three to five weeks and it will say exactly what that says, in a director's hand, on good paper." He put the pen down. "Which is the thing you came here to be told, and I am telling it to you at half past three in the afternoon with a nurse in the room, because I have seen what it does to a young man when he is told it in a stairwell, and it is not the words, it is the hour."

Darla Vint, from the side of the table, said, "Mr. Pratt. There's one more thing on that form and I can see you haven't got to it."

"I've got to it," said Hollis Pratt. He did not look pleased about it. "Four: this office is not able to act on a blank field, and the reason is a limit of authority and not a judgement about anyone's conduct. But the term panel in November is a different body with a different job, and I am allowed to tell you what that body can do, and I am going to, because it is the only useful thing I have got in this case and it is not mine."

He turned the form round and read it out.

"The term panel cannot act on a document. It cannot act on an absence, and it cannot act on a suspicion, and it has no power to make anybody produce a document that does not exist." He put his finger on the line. "What it can act on is a demonstrated alternative. A working boundary-release performed by a named practitioner, in a public room, with witnesses, using no substituted cue — used, followed, or accepted — inside the scored window. That is the one thing in this city that a panel can act on without anybody having to prove who did anything to anybody, and I am telling you that not because it helps you, Senn, I am telling you because in four years somebody is going to need to know it and I would rather it was in a file than in a corridor."

Marek sat very still.

"So the Office is telling me the only thing that works is the thing I can't do."

"Yes."

"And the Office is not going to give me a date for it."

"No. That is the department's and the board's and not mine, and I have already written to the department about a room in November, and I am going to tell you exactly what they said to me on the telephone on Monday, because you should have it from me." Pratt folded his hands. "They said the examination hall is unavailable before the week following the term review, and that the board observer is committed, and that a provisional board requires fourteen days' notice to a member resident outside Nacre. And then they said — I have it written down, I always write it down — *we will consider a date when the term review has concluded.*"

"Which is a fortnight."

"Which is a fortnight," said Hollis Pratt. "And that is the whole of this office's position, and I have been in this job nineteen years and I have never in my life been able to hand anybody a better sentence than *that is the whole of my position.*"

He shut the case.

"Two more things and then I have got a train." He looked at Marek directly. "First: is there a person in this who has been hurt by this and who is not in your file?"

"Yes."

"Then they are not in mine either, and they should be, and I am not going to open anything on them without their permission, and I would like you to stop bringing me people who have not asked me." He said it without any unkindness at all. "I have had four conversations with you in nine weeks and in three of them you have told me about a man and a nurse and a boy, and in all three of those conversations you told me about them in the third person, and one of them is standing in this room, and she has not said a word for fifty minutes, and I would like that noted, because it is going in my notes as *the witness did not speak*, and it is going in your file as a thing you did."

Darla Vint said, "That's fair," and shut her folder.

"And second." He stood up and got his case. "The warrant on that wax paper. Service engineer in the corridor has told me you have something to say before the twenty-eighth and he has told me to say nothing about it in a room, and I am honouring that, and I am telling you that I know about it, which means that if you do not come and find him I will know, and so will he, and so will the depot."

---

Anneke Roos caught him at the counter at ten past four with the look of a woman who had been waiting since half past two for a specific moment and was annoyed that it had gone past it.

"Inside," she said. "Now. I have got a room until four forty and I have had it since this morning and I have been sitting in it watching a clock."

There were two sheets of paper on her desk. They were not the same papers as the Wednesday of week 6. They were newer and they were on department letterhead and there was a reference number on the top one, which was the number a thing gets when it has been agreed by somebody above her.

"Sit."

"Ma'am—"

"Sit, because I have written three lines and I have not shown them to Darla and I would like it to be the first time I have ever handed you a piece of paper in this office that I have not had a witness in the room for." She turned the first one round. "Conditional progression. You re-enter the third year as a candidate in good standing, effective the start of week 10. Your transcript line is amended to *supplementary completion route pending* — not removed, amended, and an amended line is one that can be amended again, which the current one cannot. Your scholarship is restored on the day you sign. The department raises its vendor enquiry on the return contact in the ordinary way, which is true, and which takes six to nine months, and which is the truth about the thing that actually happened to a boy in a hospital in week 6."

"And the price."

"The price is on the second sheet and I am going to read it to you because if I hand it to you I will watch you read it and I have watched enough people read things in this office." She turned the second one round and did not push it across. "The variance item tabled at the term panel on the fifteenth of week 6 is withdrawn. The withdrawal is recorded and the withdrawal is dated and it is signed by me, and it goes into the file, and in November you will not be standing in a room at all, because there will not be a room."

Marek did not say anything for a while. In the corridor outside the conference room a telephone was ringing and nobody was answering it.

"Thursday," he said. "The fifteenth of week 6. You tabled it on the Friday of week 7."

"I tabled it on the Friday of week 7 and it is dated the fifteenth of week 6 because that is the date of the variance, and you are the only person in this building who could think that was an accident, so I have come to tell you before you did: it is not an accident, and I did it deliberately, and I have been waiting all afternoon to watch you notice it."

"You tabled an item four days after you cancelled the date it was going to be heard at."

"I tabled an item in the only forum available to me on the only day the department had left," said Anneke Roos. "Do you understand what I am telling you? The examination hall went in October. The variance was still open and the item had to be tabled by Friday or the term closed without it. Those are the two facts. And I put them in a room together on purpose, and I have had a bad fortnight of my own life over it, and I would do it again, and I would not do it in front of the Office, because the Office would have had to look at it."

"What do you get?"

"I get a department that can be defended on Friday of week 11." She said it flatly. "That is what I get. Not the item. The item is not worth anything to me. I get to stand in front of forty people in a fortnight and say that the department has taken a documented action on a documented variance raised by a trainee, in the ordinary way, and that it has not lost anybody, and that the four holds in four sites are consistent with what we did. That is the sentence I have to say on Friday of week 11, Senn, and if you take this I can say it, and if you don't I cannot, and I have known that since the Wednesday and I have been sitting on it for five days because I wanted to give you the review first."

"You could have told me on the Wednesday of week eight."

"I could have told you on the Wednesday of week eight and you would have taken it on the Wednesday of week eight," said Anneke Roos, "and then in November you would have stood in a room on my item and told forty people that the department had bought him off, and you would have been right, and it would have been the end of the department's ability to do anything about a return contact in this city for a generation. So I waited, and I let you have the Office, and I am aware of exactly what that makes me."

Marek looked at the two sheets for a long time.

"I'm going to say no," he said.

"I have written a line for it."

"Ma'am—"

"I have written a line for it, and I am going to turn the form round after, and I would like you to look at it before you say the reason, because you have now said no to me in this office once and the reason you gave then was the best sentence anybody has ever said to me in nineteen years, and I would like to see whether you have got another one or whether you are going to reach for the old one."

He looked at the paper.

"In the summer," Marek said, "a man with twenty-two years in this department signed for the kit itself off a depot docket without reading it, because signing for things is his job, and he told me that himself, in a corridor, and he was right to, and he is not to blame for it. And in the first week of term there were nine crates in a corridor and a queue going out of the door, and I was in it, and I put my own name and my own student card against the delivery note without reading it, because the queue was moving and it was Tuesday and I had somewhere to be. A man signs what is in front of him at half past four on a Tuesday. And the man in front of me signed it in about ninety seconds, because his own daughter was ill, and I have never met him and I am not going to. And a crew lead at the west berth called G. H. has four other men who signed for a plate in August, and he says so out loud, on a dock, on his own, and he was right, and he has been owed an answer since the Tuesday of week six and got one this week. And there are fifty houses in this city that have had a plate out of the same bundle, and the form that sent them there has not been tracked since 2019. And there is a woman on Ninth Street who has been asking her own room what a plate actually does since before I was a student. And there is a man of sixty-one with two broken ribs and one cracked out of 2011, and a nurse with her own stitching in a hip pocket."

He put his hand flat on the desk.

"Those fifty houses did not go through this office. If I sign this, then in eight months there will be a plate in a hip pocket in Ashfields and a panel in this city that is the only thing in the world that would ever look at it, and I am the reason there isn't one, because I am going to be sat in a workshop in a reference and a wage being extremely careful in public for the rest of my life." He looked up. "That's the reason. It isn't the old one. The old one was about me."

Anneke Roos picked up her pen and wrote three lines, and turned the form round so that he could see it, which she had done once before, in this room, in week 6, and which he had spent five weeks deciding it meant something.

It did not say *he would have taken it if being wrong had cost him nothing*. It said:

*Offered and declined, Friday of week 9, in the presence of no witness, at the candidate's request. Grounds: the candidate states that he does not wish to close the only forum available to him. Reason given and recorded.*

"Read it back," she said.

"It's wrong. You didn't ask for no witness. I didn't ask for privacy, I said—"

"I am not going to write *no witness* in a departmental file in the year we are in," said Anneke Roos, "because the last person who wrote a true thing into one of these without checking it has spent four years regretting it. You get the reason. You do not get the absence of a witness. Take it or argue with me and waste both our afternoons."

"I did not say I wanted privacy. I said I did not want a lecture. There's a difference and you know it."

"Yes," said Anneke Roos. "And the difference is not mine to record, because the form does not have a box for the difference, and if I start putting the difference in boxes then in five years somebody will be putting everything in boxes and calling it procedure. You have your reason. It is a good one. It is the best one anybody has given me in nineteen years and it is not about you, which I notice, and which I am not going to remark on."

She put the pen in her pocket and squared the two sheets and put them in a drawer, and locked the drawer, and the key went on her belt.

"Do not thank me," she said. "And Senn — in a fortnight I am going to have to stand in front of forty people and explain why this department is holding four sites' worth of stock on the evidence of a third-year who has no licence, and I made that harder twice this week on purpose, and I would do it again, and I am not doing it for you. Do you understand the difference between the second time and the first?"

"Yes," Marek said. "The first time you had the label and the blank lines and I was on my own. This week I had four sites and a nurse's own note and a man with three ribs."

"Correct." Anneke Roos opened the door. "It is not going to get easier from here and that is the part I cannot help you with. Out."

---

He stood in the corridor outside the department office at ten past five on the Friday of week 9 with a piece of paper in his hand, because Hollis Pratt had given it to him on the way out and Hollis Pratt had said *this is yours, take it, it is the only thing in my case that belongs to somebody else.*

It was one line on Continuity Office letterhead with a reference number.

*It is the view of this office that a term panel is able to act upon a demonstrated non-hinged boundary release, performed in a public room by a named practitioner, with witnesses, and with no substituted cue used, followed or accepted within the scored window. It is the further view of this office that no other matter within its knowledge is capable of being placed before that panel to the same effect.*

One sentence, a reference number, and a date.

That was what he had on the Friday of week 9, in the ninth week of the term, with a fortnight to the term review, no licence, no technique, no date, no money, a shoulder that was still bad, a head that had been three tired people and one cold store, one nurse's copied card, two broken ribs and one cracked, in a man of sixty-one's own history, a boy in a sling, and a page in his own handwriting with two columns on it and fifteen numbered lines, of which two were things he could prove by reading two documents aloud, in any room, to anybody, and which no one has yet let him read out loud in a room with the equipment off.

He stood in the corridor for a long time, and then he wrote it in the load book, because a number you have written is a number you can say out loud in a room, and then he went home and did not do any of the eleven things he had planned to do.
