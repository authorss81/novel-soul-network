# Chapter 11 — The List on the Wall

The theatre list went on the board at ten past seven on the Friday morning and Marek stood in front of it for eleven minutes, which was longer than he had ever spent reading a piece of paper in a waiting room.

It was a printed sheet in a plastic sleeve, three columns, names in a small serif font, and somewhere in the middle of the second column, in the same font as everybody else's name, it said **ARRICK T. — 3-19 — RND 4** and underneath that, in brackets, **TENDON REPAIR, R FOREARM — FDS (MID) / FCR**.

He did not know what FCR and FDS meant. He wrote them down anyway, because he had learned in the last five weeks that the abbreviations were usually the part where somebody had decided something on somebody's behalf without asking them.

Below the list there was a whiteboard with times in a dry-erase hand, and beside the door a rack of surgical gowns in three sizes and a rack of gowns and a sign asking people not to lean on the wall because of the vibration. The corridor smelled of floor polish and heat. A porter came past with a bed. Somewhere behind the double doors a woman was saying a list of numbers out loud to nobody in particular, the way people do at seven in the morning.

Marek had been in the building since half past six because he had not been able to be anywhere else, and he had spent the first half hour in a plastic chair opposite a vending machine, and he had eaten a bun he had bought at the corner at ten to seven and had not tasted.

At twenty to nine a woman in green came out and said the boy's name twice, to two different people, and Marek found out later that she had been looking for the second of them, which was not him, and that the whole business of the family being told was handled in about ninety seconds by people who did it forty times a day.

He did not go in for that. He was not family. A boy in a hospital had put his own name in the name box of that drill sheet in week one, and four days ago the record had been corrected to the word *student*, and there was a whole category of person he had quietly graduated into without deciding to, and it was *people who are not told anything until it has already happened*.

---

Tam came out at twenty past eleven.

He was on a trolley with his right arm up on a pillow in a sling now — a proper sling, cloth, the kind with a strap round the neck, and the arm inside it wrapped in a great deal of white — and his face was the colour of the wall behind him and his eyes were half open and moving.

Marek walked alongside. He did not touch the trolley and he did not talk for the first four minutes, and the reason he did not talk was that Tam Arrick had spent five weeks being talked at by everybody, including him, most of all him, and that a boy coming out of an anaesthetic with two hours of his own afternoon missing does not need a man who needs something.

"Where's the third thing," Tam said.

"What?"

"You've got a face on you. You've got the face you had outside the porters' cage. It's the one where you've worked something out and you're waiting for somebody to be pleased." His eyes moved. "Third tendon."

"They did the middle one. It came back in pieces." Marek said it carefully, because he had been told it in a corridor and had written it down on his forearm in pencil. "They said the middle one's worse than nothing, which sounds like the opposite of what it is, and the other two are fine. The registrar says he'll know more in a fortnight than anybody knows now."

"Right." Tam worked that over. "So the finger's the question mark and the rest of it isn't."

"He says he's not going to promise you the finger. He says he'll promise you the other two, and he said it twice, which is how you can tell a man means it."

"That sounds like her." He shut his eyes for a second. "Six weeks before it carries load. Three months before it holds anything over a kettle. He said all that too. And then he said, because I asked him, whether I could still write."

Marek said, "Can you?"

"He said yes. That's the one I asked about and I asked it first and he said yes before I finished the sentence." Tam's mouth did something that was not quite a smile and cost him something. "So that's the one I've got. A hand that can't lift a crate and a hand that can write. Everybody keeps acting like those are the same hand."

They got him onto a bed in a ward of eleven with the lights up and a radio going somewhere. A physiotherapist came and looked at the arm for forty seconds and said six words and went. Somebody else came and asked him his date of birth and his allergies and whether he had anywhere to sleep tonight, and Tam said he had a room over a chip shop on Sennet Row, and the woman said *that's fine, we'll sort something,* and Marek understood that he had just watched two systems operate on the same boy without either of them asking the other anything, and that this was the ordinary condition of the world and always had been.

She left. The radio was still going.

"I want the plan back," Tam said.

"You've got it."

"You've got a copy of it, which is different, and I know you've got a copy, and I'm not going to be a man who takes a piece of paper off a person who's holding it for me." He shifted to look at Marek properly, which took effort. "So here's what I've changed. On the first page, under who watches, I wrote *an observer who was never taught the card.* That's still right. But I've put a second line under it and I want you to read it to me back before I go to sleep, because I wrote it at four in the morning and I don't trust four in the morning."

"Read it out."

"'And I want to know they said no.'"

Marek waited.

"That's the second line," Tam said. "Not *who was never taught the card.* Both. The card bit's easy, everybody's never been on a card, that doesn't make them able to tell me anything. The hard bit's the no. Because the department's physio isn't allowed to make me fail and my instructor's going to want me to pass, and the only person in this city who is going to be allowed to tell me I've faked a release is somebody who has got no reason at all in the world to want me to be all right."

"Who's that?"

"I don't know." He shut his eyes. "That's the whole point. That's why it's yours to find and why you can't just hand me somebody you've already decided is honest, because you'll decide they're honest by the fact that they said something you wanted to hear, and you do that, you do that *constantly*, you did it to me in a clinic car park and you did it to me on a wet dock."

"Yes," Marek said.

"Say it again when I've got a hand back."

He was asleep before he got to the second repetition. Marek stayed twenty minutes and then went and found the registrar's number in the file and stood in a stairwell and put the whole thing in the load book in his own handwriting, the whole of it, the FCR and the FDS and the three months and the six weeks, because he had a rule now and the rule was that things he was told in a corridor got written down in the same hour.

---

On Monday morning there were two sheets on the noticeboard outside the practical department office, and Marek stood in front of them at ten to nine in the morning and read both of them the way he read everything, which was twice.

The first was his.

**PROVISIONAL WITNESSED ASSESSMENT — THURSDAY OF WEEK 7, 09:00 — EXAMINATION HALL — BOARD CONVENED**
**Candidate: SENN, M. — provisional only — scholarship suspended pending outcome**

Three days.

He put his hand flat on the paper and it made no difference, which he had expected, and he read the second sheet.

**PRACTICAL DEPARTMENT — SUPPLEMENTARY INVIGILATION**
**Thursday evenings 18:00–21:00. Examination hall and rooms 1–3.**
**Rate 14 credits per evening. Three-year students may apply. Applications to the office counter, room 2, before noon Wednesday.**

Fourteen credits an evening, which was four times the Sunday's rent. He had been doing the arithmetic on the tram for five weeks and the arithmetic was not improving, and here was a piece of paper that made it a different number.

He filled the application in at the counter at nine twenty, in the pen they had on a string, and Darla Vint read it, and stamped it, and did not say anything encouraging.

"You know what it is," she said.

"It's the Thursday before the assessment."

"It's the Thursday of the term," Vint said, "and there are forty-one third-years in this department who have not got anything to do on a Thursday evening, and the department is not going to pay a student to sit in a room on his own initiative." She pulled a drawer. "It's also four evenings and about fifty-six credits, which I am aware is not a fortune."

"Does it help me?"

"It helps you." She squared the stamp pad. "It will not help you with the thing you actually want, and if you spend four Thursdays in that hall you will be extremely well informed about how a hundred and eleven third-years perform a card they were all taught in September, and every one of them will do it the way they were taught, because that is what you do when you are twenty-one in a room with a man who has been teaching for twenty-two years."

He wrote that down in his head and then, later, in the book.

"That's the honest version," Vint said. "There's also a second honest version, which is that your scholarship is suspended and the department would prefer not to be the reason a student in this building doesn't eat. Neither of those is the whole truth and I have no idea which one is bigger."

---

He got on a tram to Nine Bridges at half past ten with no invitation, no letterhead and no standing whatsoever, and stood in the corridor outside the Ninth Street rooms for eleven minutes before a woman with a set of keys in her hand opened the door and asked him what he wanted.

They were four rooms above a community hall in a building that had been a print works. There were two practice rigs at the back, a load bag, a wall of laminated cards, and a smell of floor polish and old card.

"Marek Senn. Third year. I'm looking at the 4-19 stock."

"Who's asking."

"Senn."

The woman did not open the door any further. "Right. I'm Ines Sarr, I run the Thursday and the Saturday here, I've been here nineteen years, and I have never once had a student from the main building come up to this corridor unless the department sent them with a piece of paper. So I'll tell you the same thing I'd tell anybody: no."

"I'm not asking to run anything. I'm asking to watch."

"You could watch from the main building. Room two is where the cards are." She shifted her weight, and Marek understood that she was not enjoying this and had decided to get it over with quickly and honestly, which he respected. "It's not you. There's forty of them come through here on a Saturday and not one of them has ever asked me for anything, and I'm not going to start saying yes to the ones with a theory."

"Then can I look at a plate?"

"No."

"It's a practice plate on a wall."

"It's on a wall in a room that has forty people in it on a Saturday, and one of them is a man of fifty-eight who has a bad back and lifts things for a living, and if I let a person I have never met stand in this corridor and photograph a brace on the wall, then in six weeks there'll be a reason why he isn't allowed to use the rig any more, and I'll be the one who did it." She said it without heat. "I have been here nineteen years and I have watched three good technicians get ground down for standing near a thing that turned out to be somebody's paperwork. I know what the paperwork is. Don't hand me a shape at eight in the morning."

"Sorry."

"Don't be. Go and be brilliant somewhere that isn't mine."

She shut the door, and it was not slammed, and Marek stood in a corridor on Nine Bridges and found that it was a completely different feeling from being told *equipment sound* in a department office, and that the difference was that she had given him a reason and the department had not.

---

He was at the bench at Civic Spine 7 by one, in the middle of a Wednesday-to-Monday backlog of six jobs, and he got four of them done in three hours, because he is a very good repairer and that has never once been in dispute.

Sera Quill came in at two with a parts order and stood at the other end of the bench and let him work, which is her way.

He tried the fourth beat at half past four, on the bench, alone, in a locked building.

He set up what he had set up six times before. The 3-08 manual open to the page, a scrap of plate under his left palm, the load bag from the shelf with forty kilos in it because forty kilos is a load and not a crisis, and he did the four beats. Anchor, load, close.

And then he got to the fourth place and reached, for the first time in six weeks, for the thing he had actually seen in week one, which was not a sentence at all and had never been a sentence. It was a hand. His own hand, palm flat on the plate, reading where the load was going, with the four words *this is what I am allowed to have touched* in his mouth.

He got as far as the first of them.

The taste came up the back of his throat, metal, and behind his left eye a door opened the way it did, and the crane on the front of the load book on the shelf across the bench stopped being a colour. Not green. Nothing. He put his hand flat on the bench and waited it out with his eyes shut and counted to sixty, which is a thing he had learned to do in October and had never told anybody about.

When it had passed he looked at what he had written, which was nothing, and then he looked at his own forearm where the pencil notes still were from Tuesday, and he understood — standing there, with a screwdriver in his hand and nobody in the building — that the reason he was doing this at a bench at half past four on a Monday was that he had no technique and no date in three days' time and no fourth person, and that the entire method he had left was to try it again harder.

He was reaching for the word. That was the part he had to write down afterwards. His mouth had been going to *say* it, at a bench, in an empty building, with no plate and no man in it, because the word was the path of least resistance and it was sitting there in the front of him where the fourth beat should be.

Sera, from the other end of the bench, without looking up: "Senn."

"Sorry."

"I don't know what you're doing and I've decided not to know. But if you're going to keep standing there with your hand on a plate, do it on the Friday night list and not at four in the afternoon on a Monday, because you go white and you don't notice you go white, and I have to work with you."

He put the plate down.

"Sorry," he said again, meaning a different thing this time.

"Go and do your visit," Sera said. "You've been sat on that bench since one o'clock and you've been somewhere else since about four."

---

Gus Haldane's fish office was open at ten to five and the man behind the counter was not Gus Haldane, and Gus Haldane came in out of the cold at four with a paper hat on and a clipboard and looked at Marek and said, "Right," which was how Gus Haldane said *you came*.

"You said tomorrow," Marek said. "I said tomorrow."

"It's tomorrow. I don't know what day it is and neither do you." He put the clipboard down and pulled a chair out from under the desk and sat on it backwards. "Go on, then. All of it. From the top. And don't do the short version, because I've been on a wet floor with a crate coming down at me and I have had six days to make my own version up and I want to see how much of it you invented."

So Marek told him. All of it, in the wrong order, in a fish office on the west berth, with a queue behind him and a man in a paper hat listening with his whole front.

The word. The card. The four beats and the fifth one that wasn't there. The bit about the instructor's notes calling it a support beat and saying a learner is not expected to perform it alone and must not proceed on a self-initiated release. The 3-08 manual with *the practitioner* in it, in a box in a corridor, requisitioned by a man with twenty-two years in his own handwriting on the eleventh of March. The two empty lines on the bundle label, ruled the same day with the same rule. The service notice that said *not a fault* and *do not claim* and *prior practice continues unchanged*. The nine crates moved to a cage with a chain on them, which is a locked crate and not a stopped thing. The boy on the trolley this morning at twenty past eleven, and the two tendons, and the six weeks.

And Gus Haldane listened to the whole of it with the hat still on.

"Right," he said. "Now the part I want."

"Ask."

"Tuesday of week six. Wet floor, four crates, Gus's heel off the edge. And you said the word." He leaned in. "Where'd you get the word, mate. Not the card. You've clearly never touched a card. Where'd you get the voice."

Marek said, "Out of my own head. I heard Davan Rusk say it in a room in September and it has been in there ever since, and I have never said it to him, and I have never said it to the department, and I have said it exactly once out loud and that was to a boy on your dock while he was falling."

Gus Haldane sat back on the chair.

"Then the voice is free," he said. "That's the bit that frightens me. Not the kit, not the school. A word that any man in this city who's ever been in that room can say out loud for free, in the right shape, and it will work. That's not a supply problem, that's a — " and he stopped and made a noise with his mouth, because he was a courier crew lead and not a man with words for that.

"Yes," Marek said.

"Right." Gus got up and went to the door and looked at the water and came back. "Then here's my question, and I'm going to ask it once and you're not to pretend you know the answer."

"Ask."

"Who checked them before they shipped?"

The office was loud. Somebody was shouting a number across the quay. A gull went over.

"I don't know," Marek said. "That's the honest answer and it's the whole answer. There's a service ref, SB-4-19-03, that's a Threadline number, and an operations name on the department's purchase docket, O. Vey, and a depot in Lattice, and a lot number, TL-4-19-0311, and two blank lines on the label where the technique bundle and the licensor should be. So we know who assembled it, and we know the name of the man who authorised the spend, and we do not know who signed for it in this building, except a man with twenty-two years who told me he signs for things. We do not know who wrote the thing, and there is not one name, date or set of initials anywhere in that eleven-page booklet, which I have read all of, or on the label, or on the crate, and that is not because somebody has hidden them, because you cannot hide a thing that was never printed."

Gus Haldane said, "Right."

"And there's a Threadline engineer who asked me twice in a store room whether the wax paper was still on the crate, and who told me the notice is there because it's a design change and isn't covered by the warranty and they don't want three hardware claims off one building. So the question you're asking has an answer, and the answer is *somebody, once, in a building I have never been inside*, and the only place in this city where that answer is written down is an office that has already told me the equipment was sound."

Gus Haldane chewed that for a while. Then he went over to a filing cabinet, unlocked it with a key on a bootlace, and took out a brown envelope with a courier's docket stapled to it.

"August," he said. "Nineteenth of August. That's when ours came. Four boxes, twenty-two plates between them, and I've got a count because I'm a pedant and I've got a form for everything. Lot number's not your lot. It's TL-4-19-0307."

Marek looked at the number on the docket for a long time.

"Yours came a month before ours," Gus said. "Different lot. Different month. Same thing in it, and our crew's been doing work-setting checks on them since the twenty-ninth of August because that's when the service notice turned up in the folder, and I have never once in my life asked a man in a corridor what a lot number was doing on a different number."

"You asked me," Marek said. "You asked me on the Tuesday."

"Yeah, well." Gus turned the clipboard over and wrote the number out again, larger, in the middle of the page, and then took a pen out of the drawer and drew a box round it. "There it is. And here's the thing I want you to understand about it, and then I'll let you go. Somebody is going to have to come and take those off my crew. Somebody with authority, with paper, and probably in a hi-vis. And I'm the one who's going to have to stand there and say no to him, because they're in my people's hands four nights a week. So whatever you do next, that happens, and it doesn't happen because you're right. It happens because you've got four little pieces of paper and no one in this city is frightened of you."

He tore the docket off the clipboard and pushed it across the counter.

"That's the copy," he said. "You can have the number. Not the docket, it's got my crew's insurance on it, and if you turn up with a subpoena I'm finished and so are four other men. And there's no, and there's not a maybe. And no, I'm not going to come and stand next to you when you say it. I like you. I don't like being next to you."

"All right," Marek said.

"And one more thing." Gus put the hat on the back of the chair. "You said the school wrote a correction and the school says the equipment was sound. There's a signature on that sheet, and it isn't yours. It's a boy of twenty's, and he put it there himself, and he's in a hospital because of it. That's how that works. It isn't a lie. It's worse. It's about three hundred and forty people who signed."

"About three hundred and forty," Marek said. "And not one of them was you, which is the other thing. Rusk signed for nine crates in a corridor in September without reading them because the queue was moving and it was Tuesday and he had somewhere to be, and he told me that himself, in a corridor, two days after I asked him for it."

Gus Haldane looked at him for a while.

"You'll be a terrible witness to your own case," he said. "You'll be a great one to other people. Make sure somebody's keeping that."

Marek stood on the quay for a while with a lot number in his hand, in the cold, with the water going in and out under the west berth.

Three days to a Thursday that was going to ask him to do the one thing he had established, in writing, in a clinic treatment bay, that he could not do. Fourteen credits a night on the other four Thursdays, which was money and was company and was the single most reliable way in the world to confirm the thing he already believed. A satellite site with a door shut in his face and a woman who had told him why. A bench in an empty building where his own mouth had gone to the word out of habit, with nobody there at all, because the word does not need anybody, that is the entire finding, that is what he had learned this afternoon at four thirty on a Monday in a city of two million.

He got the tram and sat in the front with the notebook open on his knees and wrote the week, in the small handwriting, the first person.

*Week 7, Monday.*

*Assessment: Thursday of week 7, 09:00, examination hall, board convened. Three days. Requirement on the sheet: a scored release completed alone and unprompted, no substituted cue used, followed or accepted in the window. So: prove I can do it. Not prove it's missing. I asked Vint twice what the word is for and got a procedure both times.*
*Three things I tried today and what each one was:*
*1. Paid invigilation, Thursdays, 14cr. Took it. Vint told me, unprompted, what it is worth: 111 third-years who were all taught one card by one man in September. That is not a second source. That is a hundred and eleven copies of the first one. Money. Not evidence. Take the money.*
*2. Ninth Street. Refused, with a reason, by name, in a corridor, in nineteen seconds. She is right. I have no standing and I asked a woman for something for me in a building I was sent to by nobody. Refusals that cost somebody something are data. Do not go back.*
*3. Bench, Spine 7, 16:30, alone. Tried the fourth beat. Got to the first four words and lost the crane on the front of this book for about four seconds. Mouth went to the word at an empty plate with nobody in the room. That is the finding. The word does not need a man. It works in a lock-up at four in the afternoon. Whatever else is true today, that is true, and I would not have believed it from anybody else.*

*Do not know:*
*Who checked the plates before they shipped. Nobody will say, and I cannot get it out of a label with two ruled lines and nothing in them, and I cannot tell you whether those lines are a blank somebody left on purpose or a blank nobody ever filled in, and there is no way at all on this earth for me to find out which.*
*Three sources I have never met. Have one door shut and one man who says the answer is in a book and not in a bundle.*
*Whether the department's plan is a plan at all or just a series of doors.*

He got off at Pell Street at nine with three days left and the phrase in his head that had been sitting there since the Sunday: *the fourth beat is the last thing anybody in that building can show me, and that is a different problem, and I have been treating it as one for a month.*

He had been treating it as one for a month and he was now, on the Monday of the sixth week, treating it as one for a term.

He turned the light on in the room above the laundromat and Rafi was asleep on his back with a courier bag on the floor and the whole week sitting on top of him like a plate, and Marek lay down in the dark and did not sleep, and did not try.
