# Chapter 103 — Somebody Was Here First

The second barrier was on the same contract and it was in a different company's building, and the difference between the two places was about eleven hundred yards and the whole of the argument.

Marek Senn had the reference for it in his head and not on paper, in the way he had a box number in his head and not on paper, and he got a bus and then walked the last stretch because a bus would have got him there at nine minutes past and he did not want to be at nine minutes past.

The building was a distribution centre on a service road behind a timber yard, one storey, green, with about four hundred pallets inside it and eleven people working in the office. The barrier was a roller shutter on a side entrance with a card reader on the wall and a chain across the pedestrian side and a hut with a window, and the hut had a kettle in it and a chair and a rack of leaflets for a charity that had been giving away bread.

The man in the hut was sixty-one or about it and had been in the hut for four years, and when a man in a company lanyard asked to sign in he looked at the lanyard and then at the man's face and said his name before he was asked, which Marek Senn noticed and did not comment on.

"Steve Akers," the man said. "And I am going to write it down, because the last time I did not, nobody believed me about it."

"Write it down."

"No, I am not going to," said Steve Akers, and he wrote it down.

The visitor book was on the shelf by the window with a rubber band round it. It was about four hundred lines deep and it was a hardback ledger with a printed column for company, a printed column for name, a printed column for purpose and a printed column for the time in.

Marek Senn read the last four months of it in about four minutes standing up, in the way you read a thing, and Steve Akers watched him do it and did not offer a chair and did not stop him, and at about the two-minute mark the man moved the book about four inches to the left so that his own arm was in front of the bottom half of it, and did it without looking down.

"About a fortnight back," said Steve Akers. "A gentleman. Very polite. He was on the site list, so I let him in and I wrote him in, and he had a notebook and a phone and he asked me a question about the card on the card reader."

"What card."

"Not the reader. **The card on the post.**" He pointed with his chin at a control post about six metres inside the shutter, and there was a laminated sheet on it, and it was cloudy, and it had been on there long enough for the cloudiness to be the colour of it. "**I have never once been asked about that card. Four years. Not once.**"

Marek Senn walked in, because the shutter was up and the man had a kettle, and he read the card.

It was nine words. He had read a copy of the description of it twice and it did not help, and the words were: *RELEASE IS GIVEN BY ANOTHER VOICE. DO NOT RELEASE ON YOUR OWN JUDGEMENT.* Beneath it, nothing. No name, no date, no company, no version. Two cloudy cable ties, one with a small buckle somebody had put there.

"Who put that up?"

"**Not me. Came with the building. The contractor before, they took it down when they finished and then somebody — not a contractor, a man in a van — put it back up about four years ago and I did not ask him to and I did not stop him and that is the whole of my involvement.**"

"Do you know what it means?"

"**No.**"

"You've been standing six metres from it for four years."

"**I have, and I will tell you what I think, and you can write it down or you can leave it, and I have not said it to anybody before so you may as well have it.**" Steve Akers put his hand on the kettle and did not lift it. "**It is not about the load. It is about who is allowed to say it is all right to go. And the reason I have never asked is that if I ask, and somebody answers me, then it is a thing I have been doing wrong for four years, and I would rather go the rest of my life not knowing.**"

Marek Senn stood in a warehouse aisle about nine metres from a chair and a kettle and thought about a foreman of fifty-eight on a gate off the Meridian who could not write down the tenth time in twenty-seven years, and about a woman of thirty-nine who has read a visitor book six times and has a page of her own that she has given to nobody, and about the fact that this man had just handed a stranger the reason in a single sentence without being asked, and that he was going to do it to anybody who came down that road.

"Who was the gentleman," he said.

"About fifty. Fifty-one, maybe." Steve Akers's face did not change at all. "**Grey jacket. Took him about four minutes to get that out. He said there is no district, there is a practice being handed about, and he asked me if the card on that post was on the company list, and I said I did not know what company list meant and I have never been given one.**"

"And then."

"**Then he said it was a company practice and it was being looked at, and that a gentleman from a company had told him so, and that it would be in the paper, and he said nobody was in trouble with me.**" Steve Akers took the kettle off and poured two mugs without asking. "**And he wrote it out. On a bit of card off my pad, and I wrote it, and he photographed it with his phone, and he put it in his notebook after.**"

"After he photographed it."

"**He put it in the notebook, and he said, you do not need to keep a copy of that, mate, that is on the company record, that has been on that post since before you were here, and I will put it in front of somebody.**" Steve Akers put a mug down in front of Marek Senn. "**And I have kept a copy in this drawer ever since, and I would like to know what you think I should do with it.**"

He opened the drawer. It was a bit of pale card, in biro, nine words, and a date on it, and the date was a Saturday.

Marek Senn looked at it and did not touch it.

"**That is my mother's date to the day and I did not think about it when I wrote it, and it is the only thing in this hut I have ever been frightened of, and I am sixty-one and I do not work weekends any more.**"

---

"Two things," said Marek Senn. "The first one is a question and the second one is an answer, and I am going to do the answer first because I have not got the question."

"All right."

"**The four words have been stamped into a visitor column at a gate about eleven hundred yards that way,"** he said, and nodded, "**eleven times out of about four hundred lines, by a man who has been at that gate for six years, and he does not know this gate exists, and he does not know you exist, and nobody has ever asked either of them what the nine words mean.**"

Steve Akers did not say anything. He put his hand flat on the shelf the way Talia Venn puts her hands flat on a table, and Marek Senn watched him do it and wished afterwards that he had not seen it.

"**I am not going to tell you which gate,**" he said. "**I am not going to tell you that man's name, and I am not going to tell you which company, and I am not going to write any of it down. That is not me protecting anybody. It is that a piece of paper with two gates on it is a piece of paper about two men, and in about four years it is a piece of paper with two men on it and neither of them will have been asked.**"

"**So it is one gate.**"

"It is one gate and a piece of card in your drawer and a man with a notebook. And that is the answer, and it is worse than the thing I came to ask about."

He asked the question.

"Since the gentleman was here — has anything gone in the book?"

Steve Akers turned round and took the book off the shelf, which was four inches further to the left than a visitor book lives, and put it on the sill and opened it at the right place and gave it back, and the whole of the operation took about nine seconds and he did not once look at the page he had opened it at.

The last four months of the purpose column said *delivery*, *delivery*, *contractor*, *contractor*, *delivery*, *contractor*, and on the days the contractors came it said *post check*, two or three times a week, in a different hand every time, in a rubber stamp anybody in this trade would recognise.

Marek Senn went back through about two hundred lines with his finger on the edge of the page and then asked the question properly.

"The four words. AWAITING RELEASE. Have you ever stamped that in this book?"

"**About two hundred and forty times in four years,**" said Steve Akers. "**Two or three times a week, near enough, on the days the contractors come, and it is a rubber stamp and it is not my stamp. And I have got no idea what any of it means and I never had, and that has never once been a problem, because nobody ever came and asked me, and now you have and you have not asked me either, you have asked the book.**"

"**No,**" said Marek Senn. "**I have never stamped it, and the man who has stamped it has been at a gate for six years, and the two of them do not know each other exists.**"

Steve Akers said, "Then what has happened to me, then. Because the book has not changed."

"**Nothing has happened to the book. About two hundred and forty of them are in it and about two hundred and forty were in it last week, and there is another one coming on the next contractors' day and I shall stamp it, because that is the job and I have done it for four years.**" He put his hand flat on the counter next to it. "**What has happened is that it is on the shelf now instead of in my hand, and I have moved it about four inches to the left twice this afternoon, and you saw me do it and I did not want you to, and that is the first thing I have done in this hut that I have not told anybody about in advance.**" He looked at the shelf. "**For four years, if a man came in here and asked me what the four words meant, I would have told him exactly what I know, which is nothing, and I would have said it in about nine seconds and it would have been the end of it. And now I know that there is a person in this city that it is about, and I have got his bit of card in a drawer, and I am sixty-one, and I have got a daughter-in-law who is about thirty-four and works in a building in Bower Street, and if I say a word about this to a stranger in a hut then in about four years somebody is going to be very interested in my daughter-in-law, and she has got two children, and none of that is in the book and all of it is in my head.**"

---

He got the bus back and then walked the last stretch, because a bus would have got him in at a quarter past and he did not want to be in at a quarter past.

It was about twenty past two in the afternoon and the yard at the depot was empty in the way a yard is at twenty past two on a Wednesday, and he sat on the bumper of a pallet truck for about nine minutes and worked out what he had done.

He had gone to a second gate and had found a man who had been willing, and who had been willing right up until about two minutes before Marek Senn asked him anything, and who had been told, kindly, that he did not need a copy of anything.

**The man who came first did not take anything. He did not have to. He gave a witness an answer, and the answer was true enough to be believed, and the witness believed it, and the book is still on the shelf with about two hundred and forty of the four words in it, and the case is four hundred lines better off this morning than it was last term because of a man with a notebook.**

**And the thing that is missing is not a line. It is a man of sixty-one who was going to tell the next stranger who came into that hut exactly what he knew, which was nothing, in about nine seconds, and now knows that there is a person in this city that the four words are about, and has a daughter-in-law of about thirty-four who works in a building in Bower Street.**

And the second half of it, which was worse, and which he had to write down somewhere:

**It is going to be the same at every gate between here and the end of the year.** There are about four hundred of them in this city on contracts, and every one of them has a book, and a man in a lanyard has been saying the same nine words of a laminated card into one of them about twice a week for years, and Marek Senn cannot go to four hundred gates with a lanyard in eleven weeks, and the man who can go to four hundred gates is a man with a notebook and a fee and a microphone, and a fee gets used up.

He got up off the pallet truck.

The lamp over the parts cage was on. On the wall by the door there was a leaflet in a plastic pocket, about four of them, and it was the first time this leaflet had been in this building, and there was a District Practice Assurance logo in the top corner that was a stylised open hand, and the hand had a thumb and a forefinger about a millimetre apart, which was a design decision somebody had made about a hand meaning a small thing, and it was going to be on about four thousand of them by the end of the term.

He read it twice, standing up, in about forty seconds, with a man of forty-four's own words in his head about eleven thousand contributors and ninety in a room.

The first line was the line. The line about unofficial technique sharing, and techniques held privately, and the organised promotion of disconnection as a substitute for safe practice.

The second line was the session titles.

The third line was a name, on the first Wednesday of a month, in a room in Crown Terrace, with a microphone, and **fee paid**.

Marek Senn took the leaflet out of the plastic pocket, folded it in half, and put it in his pocket, and did not take it out again that day, and it was still in his pocket on the Wednesday of the following week, and that is a separate entry.

*106. Wednesday of week 31, about twenty past two in a yard, and this entry is about a man of sixty-one at a gate for four years who handed a stranger the reason he has never asked a question in about nine seconds, and then asked me what he should do with a bit of card, and then did not want me to see him move his own book.*

*The card, and the two hundred and forty.* Nine words, in biro, on a bit of pale card off his own pad, with his mother's date on the top of it that he has never been asked about and that nobody in this case is ever going to hear. **The four that matter are not on the post, they are in the book, and they are AWAITING RELEASE, and there are about two hundred and forty of them in about four hundred lines going back four years, two or three times a week on the days the contractors come, in a different hand every time, and there will be another one on the next contractors' day and he will stamp it, because that is the job. And about eleven hundred yards away there is a book of about four hundred lines with eleven of them in it, and nobody on earth can tell me today why one gate has eleven and the other gate has two hundred and forty, and I have written that down and I am not going to resolve it this week.** So the second half of what the man of fifty-two said in week 24 is now in two buildings and not one, and I have got it in about eleven minutes in a hut with a kettle in it, and it took nine minutes of a woman's job to go round a corner for me, which is about the same as it takes anywhere. **And the thing that is worse than the book, and which nobody is going to put in a leaflet: he has moved the book four inches to the left twice this afternoon and did not want me to see him do it.** I asked him about the sheet and he said nothing has happened to the book, and he is right, and I have spent a term with a blank field on three documents in three buildings and this is the same shape exactly — **a record of an absence, a record of an absence is the easiest thing in the world for somebody to fill in wrongly** — and what has gone missing in this case is not four hundred lines. **What has gone missing is a man of sixty-one who would have told a stranger exactly what he knows, which is nothing, in about nine seconds, and there are about nine of those left in this city and I have just met one and used him.**

*And the mother's date, and the only thing he is frightened of in that hut.* **A Saturday, written in biro, by a man of sixty-one, on a bit of card off his own pad, because a man asked him to write the words out and the date went on the top and he did not think about it.** He said it in one sentence and did not make anything of it. I have written a woman's five lines in this book with no date on them at her own instruction and about two inches of docket left empty on purpose, and I have thought about that every day since, and here is a man of sixty-one in a different trade doing the same thing by accident, with the worst possible date, because a date on a thing is a day a person can be found by and he found that out on his own in a hut with a kettle in it. **Two of us in this case have now removed a date on purpose, one for a reason I gave and one for a reason he found himself, and I am not going to put them in a room.**

*And what I did not do.* **I did not say which gate. I did not say the man's name and I did not say the company and I did not write any of it down, and the reason is not protection, it is arithmetic: a sheet with two gates on it is a sheet about two men, and neither of them was asked.** And the man who has been at that gate for six years, who has stamped it eleven times out of about four hundred lines and has never asked what any of the nine words mean, does not know that this exists today and he is not going to know this term, and I have had a whole afternoon to decide whether that is a kindness or a theft and I have got no answer and I have decided to carry it as unanswered, because answering it either way would have been for me and not for him. **He has got a lanyard and a card and a job and a woman with a diary, and he did not know any of that either until a woman in a sling read out his own words in a room in week twenty-eight, and the whole of what I have learned in a term and a half is that the fourth thing in a room is the person who gets found.**

*And the leaflet, which is new, and is the reason this entry is about him and not about me.* **Four in a plastic pocket by the parts cage door, first time in this building.** A stylised open hand with a thumb and a forefinger about a millimetre apart, a first line that is the line out of a specification word for word, a list of eleven session titles, and a man's name on a Wednesday in Crown Terrace with a microphone, and **fee paid**. There are going to be about four thousand of these. I put mine in my pocket at about twenty past two on the Wednesday afternoon and it was still in my pocket on the Wednesday morning of the week after, and that is seven days, and I have not decided yet what that is, and I am writing it down before I decide so that whatever I decide is not the first thing I thought.
