# Chapter 105 — The First Friday

The rota is not a list of places. Marek Senn worked that out in about nine seconds at ten past seven on the Friday morning of the first week of the new term, standing at a board with eleven names on it and a supervisor's name at the top and a telephone number under that, and the eleven names were not eleven people who were waiting for him. They were eleven people who had asked to be on a day when a person with a card came out.

That is a different thing and it took him about nine seconds to get his hands round it.

A release callout is when somebody cannot complete a lift on their own. A service callout is when a machine is broken. **He has known the difference since week twenty-two and he has said it out loud to about four people and written it in a book, and on the Friday morning of week thirty-one he found out that a person can know the difference perfectly well and still be the fourth thing in the room.**

He got there first because he was told to.

---

The first job was at about a quarter past eight and it took twenty-two seconds and it was a load of about two hundred kilos on a ramp in a yard behind a workshop in Nine Bridges, and a man of about forty-four on the end of a chain hoist, and a man of about thirty-one on the load, and Marek Senn with a plastic sleeve on a lanyard around his neck, and in the sleeve a single sheet folded twice with a reference in the corner and four fields on it, and the fourth field empty, and a strip of tape across one corner that somebody else put there.

He said the eleven parts.

*Scope. He is in scope. I confirm your scope. Do not release on your own judgement. If I do not say it, you withdraw and you report it.*

That is the whole of it. It is about eleven parts and it takes about eleven seconds and it is the fourth beat with a name on it.

The hoist came down about four inches and stopped.

"**Say the scope.**"

"The load is in scope, the ramp is in scope, he is in scope, I am in scope."

"**Release.**"

"It is released."

It worked. It works. It worked about nine times in about nine hours on a Friday in about eleven rooms, in a city, in a week, and the reason it works is that it is correct, and the reason it is correct is that somebody with twenty-two years in a department said on a stage in front of four hundred and six people that the fourth beat is the safest piece of engineering he has ever been taught, and he was right, and Marek Senn was not able to argue with him for about a week and has stopped trying to.

And the word.

**He had got to about ten past nine when he noticed that he had said *released* and had not heard the other one, and that there was a shape in his own head where the other one goes.**

*Confirmed.*

It is not a sound. It arrives from outside, in about the same place a sound would arrive, and it is the only part of the eleven that arrives as a word. The other ten arrive as a procedure, the way a man who has driven the same route for nine years arrives at a junction. The eleventh arrives as a word with an edge on it, and every time it arrives it takes the end off whatever he is in the middle of and puts a different end on it, and the different end is the right one, which is the difficulty, because a sentence that ends in the right place feels like a sentence that is finished.

**He had said in front of four hundred and six people in the last week of the term that has just ended that he could not keep a sentence of his own in a room where a card holder is confirming somebody, that there is no way to turn it off, that it came on in about week twenty through equipment that was carrying something for somebody else, and that it is not pain and it is not fear and there is nothing to look at. He was not believed. A man in a district fleece said nerves. A woman in the second row said, love, and about nine people laughed kindly. He has said he is not going to say it twice.**

**So on the Friday of week thirty-one he was in the room about eleven times, and he was not going to say it, and he had the whole of the day to work out what to do instead.**

---

He worked it out by about half past eleven and it cost him about nine minutes of a stranger's day and one sentence of his own, and it is worth writing down in the order it happened rather than in the order it makes sense.

**The first thing that went wrong was that he stopped being able to wait.** He had been standing in a doorway at eleven o'clock, holding a clipboard, with nothing to do for about ninety seconds, and he had thought *he is going to be another two minutes, I will think about the tray while we wait* — and the thought had come out as *he is going to be another two minutes, which is confirmed* — and the sentence had been his and it had been fine and about nine seconds later he had no idea whether he had thought it or heard it.

So he gave up waiting, which was the only method available to him at seven in the morning, and a man who never waits is a man who talks over people, and that was not going to be fixed by four o'clock.

**The second thing was a count.** He counted, and not for exits. He counted the number of people in a room and he counted the seconds of a hold and he counted the number of times the word arrived, because counting is a thing you do with your mouth closed and the word arrives anyway, and a count is the only thing in his own head this term that the word has not managed to put a different end on. He got to nine, ten, eleven a minute by about a quarter past twelve and he wrote it on the back of a docket in the cab of a van because he was a man who wrote things down.

**The third thing was the sentence he lost, and he wants it recorded that he lost it and did not get it back and did not pretend to.**

He said, at about twenty past twelve, to a woman of about twenty-nine standing on a stair landing: "**I will be honest with you, I have not read the sheet.**"

"I did not know there was a sheet."

"There is a sheet on the landing with a lift on it and a load on it and a person on the load and I have not read it, and I have not got time to read it, because I am on a rota and there are about eleven of you today and the rota does not slow down because a technician has not read a piece of paper."

"That is not what the course says."

"What does the course say."

"The course says the technician confirms scope, and you cannot confirm a scope you have not read."

"**That is correct,**" said Marek Senn, "**and I have never once in my life said the other thing and I am not going to start at ten past twelve on a Friday, and I would rather you knew that I have not read it.**"

The woman looked at him for about two seconds.

"**Okay,**" she said, "**and for what it is worth, I have not read it either, and I have been on this stair for about forty minutes, and nobody else has come.**"

---

She was called Marguerite Okpara and she was twenty-nine and she had been doing the job for six years and she was on the rota because her employer paid for the card, and she asked him the question at about half past two on the same floor.

"**Do I have to wait?**"

He had a person on a load and a hoist and a rail behind her and about four seconds.

"Yes."

"**Every time?**"

"Yes. If I do not say it, you withdraw, and you report it, and the report is the point and not the paperwork, because the report is how anybody finds out that the word did not come."

"**And if you are not there?**"

"Then ring the number on the top of the board and they will send somebody, and if it is me they will ring me."

"**And if you are there and you do not say it for some reason.**"

"Then put it down, say so out loud, and tell the supervisor, and nobody will be in trouble with you, because that is exactly the situation the practice exists to catch, and I have written it in that order since I was nineteen and I have never once had a reason to write it a different way."

Marguerite Okpara nodded.

"**Right,**" she said. "**That is the answer I expected and I am glad you said it out loud rather than writing it on something, and I am going to say something and I want you to know I am not being difficult.**"

"Say it."

"**Nine seconds.** The card says eleven, and I have done about nine hundred of these and it is about nine. And I am not being difficult, I am being a person who has to go and catch a bus, and nine seconds is a bus.**" She did not sound angry and she did not sound like a woman making a point. "**I am not saying it is the wrong amount. I am saying that in about four years somebody is going to write down a number and they are going to write down the number on the card, and it is going to be eleven, and it is going to be wrong, and there will not be one person in this city who can say what the right number is because nobody has ever written it down.**"

Marek Senn looked at her.

"That is the best thing anybody has said to me about a card since the summer," he said, "**and I want to tell you the honest reason I cannot do anything with it, which is that if I write down what the right number is, then in about four years the number has a source, and the source is a technician on a release rota, and then the number is not a measurement, it is an opinion with a man's job title on it, and the first person to use it will use it to say the technique needs a supervisor. So it has to stay in your head, and I know that is not a fair thing to say to somebody who has to catch a bus.**"

"**That is worse than the nine seconds,**" said Marguerite Okpara.

"Yes."

"**Then I will keep it in my head, and I will not be the one who says it, and if I am ever asked I am going to say I do not know.**"

"That is allowed. That is the correct answer. Nobody can be put on a form for saying they do not know and I have never met one who should have been."

He held the rail for her and let the load go and she took the stair two at a time and did not look back at him, and that was correct, and he thought about it for the rest of the afternoon.

---

At about ten past four, in a yard in the Lower Wards, the fourth of the four who was not on the rota put a load down in a way that was safe, and a man of about twenty-three who had asked for a Tuesday and a Wednesday of week twenty-four and had been issued a card in four seconds put a hand up.

"**Are you the technician on the kit?**"

"No," said Marek Senn. "**I am the technician on your lanyard.**"

"Then how do you know you are in scope."

"**Because the load is on a pallet in a yard and it is going eleven feet and you are in scope and I am in scope, and the reason I know is that the booking says so, and I have read that, because that is a piece of paper I am paid to read and it has my company on it and not the client's.**"

The man of twenty-three looked at him for about four seconds, and then he said, "**Sorry,**" and went and did it.

**Nobody said sorry to him in a room in a term and a half. He said sorry to a man of twenty-three in a yard because the man thought he was the man from the poster.**

---

He got in at about half past five and sat in the cab of the van for about nine minutes before he started it, and he wrote the docket out properly, in a hand he could read in the morning, on the back of a job sheet, because the job sheet goes on the file and the file goes in a drawer and the drawer is a company's.

*Callout, Friday of week 31. Eleven jobs, about nine of them with a card in date. Confirmed eleven times each, plus whatever was said in between. Counted nine, ten, eleven a minute between about ten past nine and about two o'clock. One sentence of my own lost and not recovered, on a stair, in front of a person. Stopped being able to wait; consequence is talking over people; not corrected yet. Did not read the sheet and said so. Marguerite Okpara, twenty-nine, six years, and about nine seconds and not eleven, and she is not going to say.*

Then he sat with the pen on the knee of the cab and thought about the last thing, which was the only thing about the day that he had not been able to put anywhere.

**About eleven people a week are going to be taught that a release waits for a technician, and the eleven parts he says to them are correct, and they are the same eleven parts a woman of thirty-one made at her own kitchen table over four nights with a strip of tape across one corner, and she got them right on her own, in about nine years of doing the work, and he has been watching that for a term and a half and has not been able to say a word about it.**

**And the man who is going to be taught the eleven parts is him. And the eleven parts are going to be in his mouth, on a Friday, about eleven times, for the rest of whatever this is, and there is a company system that says he is a release caller and a company course that says he is not in date, and both of those are true, and the only difference between him and the four words on the laminated post on a control post off the Meridian is that there is a form.**

That is the whole of what the day was.

He started the van.

---

*108. Friday of week 31, about ten at night in a cab in the Lower Wards, and this entry is about the day, and it is the first one this term that is about me, and it is about me because I am the thing in it.*

*What I did.* **Eleven jobs in about nine hours, on a Friday, on a rota, in a company's system, at grade three, at the release rate.** I said the eleven parts eleven times and it worked eleven times and it is correct eleven times, and I have not got one word against it, and a member of staff of twenty-two years said on a stage in the last week of the term that has just ended that this is the safest piece of engineering he has ever been taught, and he is right, and I have known he was right since the Tuesday of week thirty and I have not been able to say so in a room since.

*And the word.* **It went nine, ten, eleven a minute between about ten past nine and about two o'clock and I counted it on the back of a docket in a cab because counting is the only thing in my own head this term that it has not managed to put a different end on.** I did not say it out loud to anybody and I am not going to and I have said I would not. **The whole of what I have instead of an answer is that I gave up waiting, and the consequence of giving up waiting is that I talk over people, and the sentence I lost was mine and it was about nine seconds long and it went at ten past twelve on a landing in front of a woman of twenty-nine and I did not get it back and I did not pretend to and it is on this docket in a file in a drawer and that is where it is going to stay.**

*The nine seconds.* **A woman of twenty-nine with six years on the job says the card says eleven and it is nine, and she is not asking for it to be less, she is asking to catch a bus, and she worked out in about nine hundred lifts that the number on the card is wrong, and she has never written it down, and I have not written it down either, and the reason I have not is that a number with a source in it is an opinion with a man's job title on it and in about four years somebody uses that to say the practice needs a supervisor, and a supervisor is a card and a card is a receipt.** She then told me that the correct answer is worse than the nine seconds, and she is right, and I said yes. **And she said she is not going to be the one who says it and that if she is ever asked she is going to say she does not know, and that is not obstruction. That is a person doing the only thing that is available inside a system that has no box for what she knows.** I have no answer to that and I have not got one now and I am not going to pretend the fact that I noticed it is an answer.

*And the sorry.* **A man of twenty-three with a card in a plastic sleeve, who had put a question with no target on it through a box on Weaver Street at four minutes to five in week twenty-two and had got four sentences back in about eight weeks, put his hand up in a yard and asked whether I was the technician on the kit, and said sorry when I told him which technician I was.** Nobody has said sorry to me in a room in a term and a half, and I have spent a term arguing that a poster with no name on it is a hole in the shape of a man, and the poster is off the wall and the hole is still in the yard and the man who is standing in it this term is me. **A hole in the shape of somebody with a lanyard does not get smaller because the man in it is honest with people about which man he is.**

*And the last thing, which is the entry.* **The eleven parts on my card are the same eleven parts a woman of thirty-one made at her own kitchen table about a term ago with a strip of tape across one corner, and she got them right out of about nine years of doing the work, and there is no company anywhere in this country that has ever given anybody a thing that good for nothing, and I stood next to that card with my hand four inches away in the second week of last term and could not say a word.** There is now a company system that has put me in the room about eleven times a week, and a course exception with an empty box in it that is the only reason I am allowed to be there, and a district service manager who has undertaken in writing to write in that box that I have not got a reason and will not invent one. **The whole of the last two volumes in one sentence: the thing I have spent two volumes being proud of not doing has been done for me, correctly, on a Monday, in eleven seconds, by a man with a form, and the form is in a company's system in a building on the far side of the city from my own bench, and the reason he did it is that a bad reason in that box is worth less than an empty one.**
