# Chapter 137 — The Rule And Not A Mood

The Exchange meets on the first Wednesday of the month at eleven at night in a back room with the shutters up, and there were nine people in it, and there is a tin on the table, and the tin is not locked, and a four-column book is on the table with forty-four lines in it, and the fortieth line is three years old.

There is a fifth chair against the wall. It has been empty since the spring and nobody has moved it and about four people in this room have noticed that it is still there.

---

Bea Nunn is sixty-one and she has not run this room for nine years because anybody asked her to.

She did not do the two minutes, and she did not do the thing she usually does at the top, and the reason was that at about a quarter to eleven a man of twenty-two who works for a district service at grade three on a Friday stood up in the middle of the room and said he had put his name in a tin in week thirty-two for a room and not a practice, and that the card had Bea Nunn's name on it and not his, on purpose, and that nobody in this room had to do anything about that, and that he was saying it because a woman of twenty-nine had been in a clinic on a Tuesday and had counted forty-one dates that are not leaflet dates and had written the number on a pad.

Nine people listened to that and about four of them looked at the table.

Then she said: "**You have used your four minutes and you have not brought me an item and I am going to write the row with the name on it and the date on it and nothing in the other two columns, and I am going to do it now while you are standing there, and nobody is going to read the two empty columns in about four years, and I am telling you now that I have done it in front of you on purpose.**"

---

The book has forty-four lines in it and it is not a register and it is not going to be a register, and the rule of the room is that what goes in is the caller's own words, in the first person, unchanged, and a signature at the end, and about four minutes an item, and no vote for anybody.

There were four items. One of them was a woman of twenty-nine with a pad and a number and no sample, and one of them was a man of thirty-eight in a district fleece who had a fifth line in pencil in a site diary and had wanted to know for a fortnight whether anybody else had written one.

And the fourth one was Asha Reed, and she did not say the sentence.

---

She is nineteen and she has been coming to this room since she was fifteen and she has been on a first-year common room's rota since about week nine, and for about nine weeks she had said the same seven words in this room seven times, and it had cost five pounds out of a tin each time, and on the seventh occasion she had said that it was the only sentence in this city that was wrong and that she was going to say it anyway and would like somebody to tell her in about four years what she should have said instead.

She said nothing for four minutes.

She had a tin of her own money in front of her and she did not open it, and she had her card about a room on it with Bea Nunn's name at the top of it, and she sat in a chair and looked at the table and said:

"**I am not going to say it this month. I have stopped. I stopped on the Thursday of week thirty-six and I did not decide to stop, and I want to write that down properly, because I have said that sentence seven times and I have not once written down why I am stopping, and if I do not write it down then in about four years it will look like a mood.**"

---

"**Then write it down,**" said Bea Nunn.

"**I cannot, because it is not mine.**"

"**It is seven words and you have paid for them five times.**"

"**It is not mine and that is the reason I have stopped,**" said Asha Reed. "On the Wednesday of week thirty-six, at ten past ten, a man of forty-one stood up in a room in Crown Terrace and said out loud that a company cannot recall a document that nobody admits to having issued. He is owed nothing. Nobody thanked him. He said he found it out from a man in a repair shop and he said it standing up with his name and his job on it, and I was in the fourth row with a notebook I had not opened."

She put her hands flat on the table.

"**Seven times I have said the case is Senn's and the technique is nobody's, and on the Wednesday I sat in a room and heard a man say the true sentence about the whole of it, out loud, with a name on it, and he was not the case. He was the man who found out that the case was a photograph of a card in a sleeve about the size of a postage stamp.**" She stopped for four seconds. "And I have been nineteen for about four months and I have been buying a sentence with my own money in a tin for nine weeks, and on the Wednesday I found out that there is a thing you can do instead, which is to stand up in a room, and the man who does it gets a name and a job and nine people writing it down, and I have been saying a thing that is now the only sentence in this city that is wrong, and I did not know it was wrong until the fourth session, and I am not going to say it for money for another four minutes."

---

Nobody said anything for a while.

Then somebody at the back said: "**So what are you going to say instead.**"

"**I do not know,**" said Asha Reed. "And I have got a tin and I have got nine weeks of thinking to do and I am not going to spend four minutes of a room saying I do not know, because I have said I do not know out loud to a man of twenty-two in this building in the spring and he told me it was the only sentence in this case that somebody could be defended with, and I have been thinking about that for nine weeks and it has not got better."

She put a pound on the table and not the five, and got up.

---

Bea Nunn wrote the row and put B. Nunn in the fourth column and left the other two empty, and then she did something she has not done in nine years, which was to say a rule out loud in a room with people in it, for about eleven seconds, and it was about line forty-five.

"**Now the thing I have to say, and I am saying it at ten to midnight with four people in the room who have come to hear me say it and five who have not, and I am saying it because the next four minutes are empty and because there is a pad in this city with forty-one on it and a wall in Bower Street with the wrong number on it, and nine people have asked me in the last fortnight whether it can go in.**"

She put her pen down on the book.

"**The rule is not a mood. The rule is that the caller's words go in, in the first person, unchanged, and a signature at the end. And what happened in that room on the Tuesday of week thirty-eight cannot be written in the first person by anybody who was not the person it happened to.**"

---

"**Forty-one people are on a thing that cannot be ended. I am one of them — no, I am not, I am the one holding the telephone — and Tess Anley is one of them, and a girl of twenty-nine counted them off the backs of four hundred leaflets in forty minutes and wrote the number on a pad. A man of fifty-one is one of them and he went into a building on Thursday for a procedure on a leg and he came out of it on Thursday afternoon and he has a leaflet on his own table with his employer in a list of things that went right, and nobody rang him before or after, and I telephoned him on the Tuesday and told him the true thing in one sentence.**"

"**That is not one person, that is forty-one, and none of them is standing in this room, and I have got nine minutes and a first person.**"

Somebody said: "**So it does not go in.**"

"**It does not go in,**" said Bea Nunn. "And I want to say the half of it, and the half is that it does not go in because there is no one person to write it, and not because it is a committee, and not because it is a district, and not because it is a programme. It does not go in because a first person is a person and what happened on the Tuesday was forty-one people and a plate on a wall and a girl with a pen, and I could write *I* at the top of it and I would be writing about a wall."

---

"**I have refused to enter a line twice in nine years and I was wrong both times, and one of them was about my own book,**" she said. "And I have come close three times this term and every one of the three was in a room of nine people, and this is the first time it has not been a room of nine people, and I have been asked by nine people in a fortnight, and the answer is the rule and the rule is eleven seconds long and it has been there since before I ran this room."

She wrote a date at the top of a page and nothing else, and put the pen down, and the book is still at forty-four lines.

---

Marek Senn was at the back wall for about four hours and did not speak once.

He was not asked for an item and he did not have one and Bea Nunn told him that at half past ten, in nine words, in front of the room, and he said that was correct, and she said she had said it in front of the room on purpose, and he has now been refused an item in this room three times in three months by three different women and two of them said why.

There was a fifth chair against the wall and it was empty and a man of sixty-one sat on the end of it for about an hour and said nothing at all.

He is Steve Akers. He has been at a barrier on a contract in another company's building for four years and he has nine words in a pocket that a man in a grey jacket told him he did not need, and on the Thursday of week thirty-six a room of forty people in Crown Terrace found out that he still had them, and a chair asked him for them and he said no, and the reason he gave is that in about four years there is a document with nine words in it and a man of sixty-one is the only person in this city who can be found from it.

Nobody in that room asked him for anything.

He has been asked about the four words by a man of twenty-two, a man of fifty-one at a microphone, a man of forty-four from a levy and nine people in a corridor, and that is five times in four months, and nine people in this city know he has them, and tonight nine people were in a room with him and not one of them said the word *barrier*.

He walked out at ten to midnight and stood in a corridor for four minutes and then went home, and he has never done that before, in four years, in any weather.
