# Chapter 134 — The Room Is Out

Room 2-07 is a former store cupboard on the first floor of a building in Bower Street with the shelving brackets still in it, one chair, a table, a rack of about four hundred consent leaflets, and a plate on the wall.

The plate is the right size for the wall. It is in the building's typeface. It says **2-14**.

The room is 2-07, and it has been 2-07 since before the woman who runs it came, and every one of the about four hundred leaflets in the rack on the table was printed with **2-07** on it.

It came up on the Wednesday of week thirty-seven at ten past two in the afternoon, in about four minutes, on a booking that carries a job and not a name.

---

Tess Anley is twenty-nine and she started in the middle of last term and she held the door open for nine minutes one morning in the spring because a man came with a case study about somebody in the room above her and she did not want him standing in a corridor.

On the Tuesday of week thirty-eight she was in the room on her own from nine, and she telephoned at eleven minutes past nine, and she said her name first, which nobody in that building has taught her to do.

"**I am not reporting a fault,**" she said. "I am ringing because I have counted about four hundred and forty-one and I do not know what to do with the number."

---

Ianthe Roke is thirty-one and she has been in that building four years and she has a diary with a date in it for every share she has opened and she has no post above her whose job it is to make a call, and the answer to that is nobody, and it has been nobody for a term and a half.

She got there in nine minutes and she did not go to the plate first. She went to the rack.

"**Say it again,**" she said. "Say how you counted."

"**Off the backs. Every leaflet in that rack has a date and a plate number on the back and a rectangle for a signature, and there is no signature in any of them, and I have been through all of them twice and I have got forty-one different dates that are not a leaflet date.**" The girl was not looking at her. "There is a leaflet for the twenty-second of last month with two names on the front in a different pen, and one for the eleventh with three, and one for the fourth of the month before with nine."

"**You counted the fronts.**"

"I counted the fronts."

"In about nine minutes."

"**In about forty minutes before I rang you and I have been here since half past seven.**"

---

"**Forty-one,**" said Ianthe Roke. "I am going to say the rest of this before you write it anywhere. Forty-one is not a rate. There is no sample behind it. It is a rack of four hundred leaflets in a room in a building in Bower Street and there is no sample in this city for a room in Bower Street, and a number without a sample is a complaint, and a complaint gets a form and a form gets a return, and I have not got four days."

"**It is on the page anyway,**" said Tess Anley. "I have written it on the pad."

"Good. Leave it on the pad."

---

Then she said the thing that is this chapter and she said it standing in front of the rack with her hands at her sides.

"**Now the part that is good news, and I am going to say it properly, because there is going to be nine people in this building by lunchtime and every one of them is going to be frightened, and I would like one true thing to go round first.**"

She turned round.

"**There is no card holder in this building. There is no man in a lanyard in this corridor whose word anybody is waiting for. Nobody in this room is going to be told to hold on because a person with a sleeve is standing in a doorway.**" She put her hand flat on the rack. "I have wanted to say that sentence for four years."

Tess Anley said: "Then why is it the hard part."

"**Because in about four years there is going to be a document that says a room went out on the Tuesday of week thirty-eight,**" said Ianthe Roke, "and the first thing anybody in that room is going to ask is who was holding the line, and there is not anybody, and I have been trying to think of a way to write *nobody was holding it* into a sentence that a person reads in about four years and does not immediately turn round and look for somebody to blame."

---

The district came back in eleven minutes and the eleven minutes is the whole of what a district is at nine o'clock on a Tuesday.

The call was from a man of thirty-four who gave his name as a surname and a building, and he had a screen.

"**The plate is on a schedule,**" he said. "The schedule has a room field. The room field says 2-14. I can see 2-14. I cannot see 2-07 anywhere on anything I have."

"**There is a plate on that wall with 2-07 on the inside face of it under the new one. There has been one on that wall since before I came and there is a clean rectangle in the dust two inches wider than the new plate, and I have a photograph of it.**"

"**A photograph of a wall is not a record of a wall.**"

"**It is the only record of that wall in this city and I am the only person who has taken one.**"

He was right about the first half of that and she knew he was right about the first half of that, and the second half is the second half.

"Then what can you do."

"**I can put a fault ticket on a company's system with a room reference of 2-14, which will be accepted, and it will go to a queue, and somebody will come in eleven weeks.**"

"**What is actually wrong with the room.**"

He was quiet for four seconds.

"**I have a rack of four hundred leaflets with a room number on them that my schedule does not know about and forty-one share ends in four months that I cannot match to a plate, and the consequence of that is that I cannot confirm the plate a share passed through, and where I cannot confirm the plate I cannot end the share, and where I cannot end the share it does not end.**"

"So the room is not out."

"**The room is on the shelf,**" said Ianthe Roke. "Nothing has broken. A number went onto a wall on the Wednesday and now about forty-one people are on a link that nobody can end and none of them can be found by a person with a screen."

---

Roy Amiss is fifty-one and he is at home in Nine Bridges with a frame and a stick and a leaflet about a case from Bower Street that he put on his own table, which is the only copy of that leaflet in this city that anybody has kept on purpose.

He telephoned the clinic on the Monday morning at ten past ten and he was put through to the room, and Tess Anley wrote down what he said, and the note is in the pad, and the pad is on the table in room 2-07.

"I am fifty-one and I am going in on Thursday for a procedure on my leg and I have got a leg that has been bad since the spring and I would like the thread off my side before I go in." He had said it in nine seconds and he had read it off the back of a leaflet. "**I have had it eleven days and I never knew, and I read about it in about four thousand leaflets, and I would like it off before Thursday and nobody has rung me once.**"

"**I will do it on the Monday,**" said Tess Anley. "I will telephone you on the Monday morning and I will do it and there is a form."

"The girl said there is no form and said it like it was her fault."

"**There is a form and I have not got to it yet and it is not your fault and it is not hers either.**"

---

**Nobody rang him on the Monday. On the Tuesday, at half past ten, Ianthe Roke telephoned him and told him the true thing in one sentence, and then she told him the second true thing, which is that she could not end it and would not pretend to.**

He said: "Then it can wait until after."

"**It cannot wait until after. If you go into that building on Thursday with a thread you cannot end running out of a room in Bower Street, and the room is on a shelf, and I have not got anybody to ring, and the one thing I have is you and your mobile and a date.**" She read her own diary out loud to a man of fifty-one in his own front room. "That is the whole of what I am. There is no post above me and there is no form and I am the telephone number."

---

Marek Senn was in that corridor at twenty past twelve and he was not on a job and the building does not have a visitor book on that floor, and he came up because a woman of twenty-nine had telephoned the depot and been put through to him at ten and had said the number forty-one twice and then said the name of a man who is going into a hospital on Thursday.

He did not go into the room. He stood in the corridor for nine minutes, and he did not touch the plate, and he did not take the leaflets out of the rack, and he did not take the pad, and he did not say the sentence about thirty feet, and he did not say the sentence about a shape not telling you who cut it.

He asked the woman of twenty-nine what the four hundred leaflets were, and she said they were the leaflets, and he asked who printed them and she said the district in the spring and there is a code on the bottom of the stack and a name in the district's own index that nobody has ever read out loud in that room.

And at ten past one he said the only useful sentence he had, and it was eleven words, and it was not about the room.

"**Nothing in this city can end a share except a person who knows which plate it went through.**"

Nobody argued with it and nobody could do anything with it and he had said it about nine times in nine rooms in three weeks and it had never once been a thing anybody could act on until about forty-one people were standing behind it.

Then he went down the stairs and telephoned a district nurse of thirty-four with a clipboard who is not disciplined and has a file with her name at the top of it, and she said, in nine words, that she had eleven rooms on her own list and that she had been thinking about the forty-one since half past ten, and that she was not going to do it on her own and was not going to do it in a district, and that she would want to talk to a courier.

---

*137. Tuesday of week 38, half past ten at night in a repair shop in Lattice Ward, and this entry is about me, and it is about me because this is the one where forty-one people are on a thing that cannot be ended, and because I stood in a corridor for nine minutes and did not go into the room.*

*What went out, and when.* **A man of thirty came up on the Wednesday of week thirty-seven at ten past two in the afternoon, in four minutes, on a booking that carries a job and not a name, and put a bigger plate on the wall in the building's typeface, and it says 2-14, and the room is 2-07.** There is a plate under it with 2-07 on it and a clean rectangle in the dust two inches wider than the new one and a photograph of the wall taken by the only person who has taken one. **Four hundred consent leaflets are in the rack and every one of them is printed with 2-07, and the schedule has a room field, and the room field says 2-14, and he can see 2-14 and cannot see 2-07 anywhere on anything he has.** Nothing broke. **A number went onto a wall and forty-one people are now on a link that nobody can end, because where the plate cannot be confirmed the share does not end, and the room is not out, the room is on a shelf.**

*The number, and the count, and the refusal.* **A woman of twenty-nine counted the fronts of four hundred leaflets in about forty minutes before half past nine and got forty-one dates that are not leaflet dates, and wrote the number on a pad, and said so on the telephone having said her name first.** One leaflet for the twenty-second of last month has two names on the front in a different pen. One for the eleventh has three. One for the fourth of the month before has nine. **And the number is refused as a rate before it is used, in the correct words: there is no sample, and a number without a sample is a complaint, and a complaint gets a form and a form gets a return, and there is no four days.** She said leave it on the pad.

*The good news, said out loud, standing in front of the rack.* **There is no card holder in this building. There is no man in a lanyard in this corridor whose word anybody is waiting for. Nobody in this room is going to be told to hold on because a person with a sleeve is standing in a doorway, and she has wanted to say that sentence for four years.** And then, to a girl of twenty-nine who asked why that is the hard part: **in about four years there is going to be a document that says a room went out on the Tuesday of week thirty-eight, and the first thing anybody will ask is who was holding the line, and there is not anybody, and I have been trying to write *nobody was holding it* into a sentence a person reads in about four years and does not immediately turn round and look for somebody to blame.**

*The man of fifty-one and the Thursday.* **He telephoned the clinic on Monday at ten past ten and read off the back of a leaflet: he is fifty-one, he is going in on the Thursday for a procedure on a leg that has been bad since the spring, he would like the thread off his side before he goes, he has had it eleven days and never knew, he read about himself in four thousand leaflets, nobody has rung him once.** A girl of twenty-nine promised him a telephone call on Monday and a form, and he said the girl said there is no form and said it like it was her fault. Nobody rang him on Monday. On Tuesday at half past ten she rang and gave him the true thing in one sentence and then said she could not end it and would not pretend to. He said it can wait until after. She said it cannot, because he would go in on the Thursday with a thread he cannot end running out of a room in Bower Street, and the room is on a shelf, **and the one thing she has is a mobile number and a date and she is the telephone number.**

*What I did in a corridor for nine minutes.* **I did not go into the room. I did not touch the plate and I did not take the leaflets out of the rack and I did not take the pad.** I asked who printed the four hundred and it is the district in the spring with a code on the bottom of the stack and a name in an index nobody has read aloud in that room in four years. And then I said the sentence, and it is eleven words and I have said it nine times in nine rooms in three weeks: **nothing in this city can end a share except a person who knows which plate it went through.** Nobody argued and nobody could do anything with it, and it had never been a thing anybody could act on until forty-one people were standing behind it. And then I rang a district nurse of thirty-four with a clipboard who is not disciplined and who has eleven rooms on her own list, and she said she has been thinking about the forty-one since half past ten and that she is not doing it on her own and not in a district, and that she wants to talk to a courier. Entry 137. The book is at forty-four lines. Forty-one is on a pad in a room whose plate is wrong, and nobody is waiting for a man in a lanyard anywhere in this city, and that is the first good news of this movement and it is the reason it is hard.
