# Chapter 142 — Nine, Ten And Eleven

A Friday is a Friday. There were eleven jobs on this one and nine of them were in date, and he said the eleven parts eleven times, and the word came at nine, at ten, and at eleven a minute between ten past nine and two o'clock, and the count is on the back of a docket in the inside pocket of a jacket over a chair in a repair shop in Lattice Ward, next to a piece of card about two inches by three, which is not on the docket.

**Nobody asked him about a fifth line. Nobody asked him whether it was safe. Nobody asked him who they should be waiting for. Nobody said the word.**

He had waited for it about eleven times by a quarter past one and it had not come, and he had been on grade three since eight in the morning.

---

Marguerite Okpara is twenty-nine and she has been a lift engineer for six years and she is on the Friday list because her employer pays for the card, and the card says eleven and it is about nine, and in the week of the thirty-first she told a man with a lanyard that she would keep the number in her head and to nowhere else, and told him that it would stay in her head.

It has stayed in her head.

What has not stayed in her head is a different number, and she has been keeping that one since the spring without deciding to, in the way of a person who writes things on the back of a hand.

"You have not asked me a question," she said.

"I have not."

"**Then I am going to say a number and you are not to write it on anything,**" said Marguerite Okpara, "and I want it said out loud in a canteen and not in a corridor, because a number said out loud in a room is a number other people heard, and I have been carrying one in my head since the week of the thirty-first and I am eleven weeks older than I was and I have never once been asked."

---

They had a canteen off the Meridian corridor with nine tables and a servery and a woman of fifty-eight behind it who has worked there nineteen years and does not clear plates.

Marguerite Okpara put a tray down and did not sit on it.

"**Eleven a week,**" she said. "**Seven of them are on the rota and about four are not, because a company does not hold a course on a Friday and has not held one for eleven years, and so there are four people a week standing in about four rooms in this city waiting for a person who is not on the rota and is never coming, and they are not being taught anything by anybody because there is nobody there to teach them.**"

He had a hand on the table and he did not use it.

"**Eleven a week is eleven people and not a rate, and I have got the eleven in a list and the list is on the back of my own card and it is not on a form anywhere and I have not shown it to anybody since the spring.**"

"That is a number with a sample behind it."

"**It is a number with one Friday behind it and one other Friday in the same month,**" said Marguerite Okpara. "**It is not a rate. Do not call it a rate. I have watched a woman of thirty-one refuse a number as a rate in about nine words and she was right and I am not going to be the second person in this case to hand somebody a complaint with no sample in it.**"

"Agreed. It is not a rate."

"**It is eleven people a week in this district,**" she said, "**and in about four years somebody is going to put it in a return, and the return is going to be about a levy, and the levy is going to be eleven thousand premises, and the eleven is going to be a column, and the column is going to have a heading, and the heading is going to be about eleven people a week and it is going to be true, and there is not going to be one person in this city who can say where the eleven came from except the two of us and a woman of thirty-one who has told a girl of twenty-nine in a car park to leave a number on a pad.**"

---

Then she put her handbag on the table and took a leaflet out of it, because about four thousand had gone out with the last levy note and she had one on her because everyone has one.

"**Read that out.**"

The front of it had a stylised open hand a millimetre apart and *fee paid*, and a session title, and a job title, and a date, and about four hundred leaflets in a stack at a trestle table, and two boxes near the bottom that said *name of the person this concerns* and *contact for enquiries*.

Both of them had two words in a man's handwriting in them.

*No name.*

"**The sentence on that page is the third line,**" said Marguerite Okpara. "**The coverage of a supervised confirmation where the holder is not a named party to the share. It is nine words, it is funded, it was minuted on a Wednesday afternoon in a room above a print works by a committee of nine, and it is the only sentence on the leaflet that anybody is going to read, and it has got no name on it, and no date of authorship on it, and no name of the man who wrote it, because a woman of twenty-six said she was not going to invent one and a man of forty-four wrote no name in the box with his own hand so that it would not be hers.**"

Nobody in that canteen said anything for four seconds.

"**And I have read it four times,**" she said, "and it is the only sentence in this whole term that anybody has paid money to say out loud, and there is not one name attached to it anywhere in this city, and I am twenty-nine and I have been in about nine hundred lifts and I can tell you exactly what that sentence is going to do in about four years and it is going to make somebody in a room wait for a person instead of putting it down."

---

He did the eleven jobs and at half past two he went out to a car park off a service road with a job sheet in his hand and stood next to his own van for eleven minutes with nothing to do.

He was aware, the whole eleven minutes, of not being needed.

Nobody came. Nobody telephoned. Nobody stood at the driver's window with a card in their hand and a question about the fourth beat, because the crisis in a building in Bower Street had lasted nine minutes on a Tuesday, and there is no card holder in that building and there is no man in a lanyard in that corridor whose word anybody is waiting for, and the third door belongs to a courier who has a van and nine stops and does not go to car parks.

A woman of twenty-nine had said that to a girl of twenty-nine in a car park off a service road in the week of the thirty-eighth, standing in front of four hundred leaflets, and she had wanted one true thing to go round before anybody frightened arrived.

Nobody has printed it. There is no form in this city that carries a true thing going round, and the only thing that carries a true thing going round is a woman saying it out loud in a canteen to one man, and she had done that at eleven minutes to one in the afternoon and had gone to catch a bus.

---

Marguerite Okpara caught the bus.

He wrote nine words on the back of the docket at half past four and the nine words were the count and not a sentence, and then he crossed out four of them and left the date, because a number without a sample is a complaint and he had said so himself to a woman of twenty-nine in about nine seconds.

There is nothing in this city that can turn eleven people a week into a man nobody can find.
