# Chapter 125 — Nobody Is Waiting

A Friday is a Friday and this one had eleven jobs on it and about nine of them were in date, and the four that were not were not because anybody had let them lapse, they were not because the district runs its course on a Monday and a Tuesday and has for eleven years. He said the eleven parts eleven times and the word arrived at nine, and at ten, and at eleven a minute, and the count is on the back of a docket in his own pocket.

On the seventh job, in a yard in Nine Bridges, he answered a question four seconds before the man who asked it had finished asking it, and the man waited, and then said *no, I meant*, and Marek Senn said go on, and the man said the thing, and Marek Senn had to ask him to say the first part again.

He apologised. He has apologised three times in a fortnight for that and it has not been corrected once.

---

The Quiet Quarter has four printers on one street and three of them have a sign that says the same word twice, and the fourth has a shutter and a bench outside it and about nine leaflets on the bench for a charity that gives away bread.

The fourth is a shop about four feet wide with a counter and a machine and a boy of nineteen who is on the till and who has been on the till for about four months.

Sunniva Bellhouse is fifty-eight and she has run it for about nineteen years and she was doing a job at the back when he came in at half past two, and she came out, and she said her name, and she said it before he asked, which is the second time this week that has happened to him and the first time was a woman of thirty-five who did it on purpose.

"You have a job sheet in your hand," she said. "**If you have come about the card, say so and shut the door, because the boy on the till has a name and I have had one enquiry about this in six weeks and it was from a woman who was very rude about it.**"

"**I have come about the card.**"

She looked at him for four seconds and then she went and turned the sign.

---

"I am not going to tell you I have not heard of it," she said, "**because I have printed four hundred and thirty-two of them and I have got the docket and I have got the money in an envelope and I have been sitting in this shop for about nine days waiting for somebody to come, and nobody came, and I want to say that I have been waiting for somebody to come and not that I have been frightened, because those are not the same and I have thought about the difference quite a lot in nine days.**"

"**Four hundred and thirty-two.**"

"**Four hundred and thirty-two. Two hundred on the first run and two hundred and thirty-two on the second, because the first lot went in about eleven days and I had a sheet left over and I did the second on a Tuesday.**"

"**Six weeks.**"

"Six weeks and about four days." She went to a drawer under the counter and took out an envelope and put it on the counter between them. "**Eleven payments. Eleven pounds each. One hundred and forty-one pounds. In a mailbox four streets away, in coins and then in notes, in a slot, at four in the afternoon, on a Monday and then on a Monday, every Monday.**"

---

Marek Senn looked at the envelope and did not touch it.

"**You kept the money.**"

"**I kept the money and I kept the docket and I have not spent either of them, and I have had a cash box in this shop for nineteen years and I have never once in nineteen years not banked a Friday, and I did not bank two of these, and I would like that written down somewhere before anybody starts.**" She pushed the envelope an inch towards him and he did not take it. "Take it. It is not evidence, it is a hundred and forty-one pounds that I did not know what to do with."

"**I am not taking your money.**"

"**I know you are not, and I would rather you did not take it, because you have got a face on you that says you are going to try to give it back to me later.**"

"**How do you know that.**"

"Because you have had one." She put her hands flat on the counter. "I am fifty-eight and I have had a shop on this street for nineteen years and I am telling you what I am going to say next and I have said it to a mirror." "**I have had a hundred and forty-one pounds in a drawer for about nine days from a person I have never met, who has never given me a name, who paid in small sums, and there is no mechanism in this city that I can ring, and I have thought about that every day, and the thing I have decided is that if the person is frightened then a name is the worst thing I could do, and if the person is not frightened then a name is the best thing I could do, and I have not been able to find out which, and that is the whole of nineteen years of me running a shop on this street.**"

---

"**The file.**"

"**The file is behind you. It is a brown envelope and it has got a W on it in biro and it is the only one I have made in six weeks with a letter on it, which your man in the café could tell you is not usual, and I will tell you myself because I would rather you heard it from me.**"

She did not go and get it. She looked at him.

"**I wrote a W on it. I did not know what for. I have thought about that for nine days and the answer is that I was not writing it for anybody, I was writing it because it is what you do when you do not know who something is for, and I would like that in whatever you are going to write, because it is the least impressive thing about me and it is probably the truest.**"

---

She went and got it.

Inside the envelope there was a sheet of A4 with about nine job tickets stapled to it, and one of the tickets was a first run, and stapled to that, separately, a sheet of plain paper with a corner torn off it, and the corner was the corner of a card that had been cut off a bigger sheet.

On the front of the corner, a photocopy. On the back of the corner, a fifth line in a hand.

"That is it," said Sunniva Bellhouse. "**That piece of card is the master. Everything I printed is off that. Four hundred and thirty-two of them off one piece of card about two inches by three.**"

He did not pick it up.

"**Photocopy,**" he said.

"**Photocopy of a photocopy, if you want the whole of it. The first one came to me as a print on a sheet, and it had a second copy on the back of the same sheet, and the second copy is the one with the writing on it, and that is how it came, and the customer said the card is one side only and the fifth line is part of the card.**"

"**You asked about the fifth line.**"

"**I asked one question about the fifth line, once, on the first run, and I am going to tell you the question and the answer, and the question was: is that on the front or is that on the back. And the answer was: it is on the card.**"

---

"**And you believed that.**"

"I did not believe it. I wrote it down. Those are different and I have got nineteen years of knowing that." She turned the master a quarter turn on the counter. "**What I want you to understand is that the two halves were already cut apart when it came to me. Somebody had a card. Somebody had a copy of a card. Somebody wrote a line on the back of the copy. And they came to me with two pieces of a thing that had been through at least two people, and I printed it, and I did not put a name on it, and every one of those four decisions was reasonable and I made all four of them in nine minutes and I have thought about them every day for six weeks.**"

---

"**The four steps.**"

"I have never seen a company's stock. I want to say that on the page and I want it in whatever you write, because everybody who has come in here in six weeks has asked me whether I have got the steps right and nobody has asked me where I got them from." She nodded at the counter. "**They came off a leaflet. Page two of a six-sided thing that came round with the market levy note. There is a photograph on it of a card in a plastic sleeve on a lanyard, and the four steps are in the photograph, and the photograph is about the size of a postage stamp and the steps are legible, and I enlarged it and I checked each one of them against the other three, and they are correct. All four. I have checked them nine times and I am a printer and I know what a wrong step looks like when it has been typed by a person who was in a hurry.**"

---

Marek Senn had to sit down on the bench outside the door, and the bench is four feet wide and it is outside a print shop in the Quiet Quarter, and nobody in the shop made a thing of it.

The photograph is on page two of a six-sided handout that went round with about four thousand leaflets, and the four steps on the card in the photograph are the four steps that everybody in this city has been arguing about for a term and a half, and they are legible in a picture about the size of a postage stamp, and about four thousand people have got the page, and **the picture has no key-holder in it anywhere, because a photograph of a card in a sleeve on a lanyard cannot have a key-holder in it. A key-holder is a person in a room. A photograph is not a room.**

"Say that again," said Sunniva Bellhouse from the door. "**I have been listening to you not say it for four minutes and I would like you to say it where I can hear it.**"

---

He said it again, and he said the rest of it, and it took forty seconds, and it is the fourth time in a fortnight and the first time it has not been about him.

"**The four steps came out of a photograph of your own card, on page two of a handout about four thousand people have got, and the photograph is where a key-holder cannot be, and so somebody cut a card up into two pieces, wrote a line on the back of the copy, and took it to a printer, and the four steps were right for free.**"

"Right for free," said Sunniva Bellhouse. "**Do you know that is the first time anybody has used that phrase about anything in this case.**"

"No."

"**It is the correct phrase. I have printed four hundred and thirty-two of the best thing I have ever printed and every step in it came out of a photograph of a postage stamp, and if you had asked me in the spring whether I could do that I would have said no, and I did it in nine minutes in a Friday afternoon and it is the only thing in nineteen years I am proud of and I do not know whether to be proud of it, and that is what I have been sitting with for six weeks and not for nine days.**"

---

"**The fifth line.**"

"I did not write it." She said it fast. "**I want that said first and I want it said in whatever you write, because there is going to come a day when somebody stands up in a room and says a woman in the Quiet Quarter wrote the line about withdrawing, and I did not write it, and I have got the master and the master has been in this drawer for six weeks and it is the only proof in this city and it is a piece of card about two inches by three.**"

"**I believe you.**"

"**You should not believe me. You should look at it.**"

He looked at it.

It is four printed steps and a fifth line in a hand that got smaller towards the end, and it says: *If the word does not come, withdraw and report.*

"**The hand is not yours.**"

"The hand is not mine and it is not a printer's and it is not a company's. I have run a shop for nineteen years and I can tell you it was written by somebody in their forties who was writing fast and who was not frightened of spelling and who has not got a good reason to be careful." She put one finger about an inch from the writing without touching it. "**That is a hand of a person doing an ordinary job. That is the whole of my profession. I am paid to tell you that a hand is an ordinary hand, and I am telling you, and it is the most frightening thing anybody has said to me in six weeks and I have had two police in here about a debt.**"

---

"**The customer's name.**"

"No."

"**I have not asked you what is in the file. I am asking you for one thing.**"

"And the answer is no, and the reason is better than the refusal, and I have been rehearsing it for nine days because I worked out on about the fourth day that somebody was going to stand in front of me and ask me that." She took her hand off the counter. "**If I give you a name, then in about four years there is a person attached to a fifth line. And a fifth line with a person attached to it is a technique. And a technique with a name on it is a thing somebody can be shown to a frightened room, and the whole difficulty of this case — and it is the difficulty, and I have understood the whole of it in nine minutes standing behind a counter — is that **a fifth line with a name on it is the original one. So I am going to refuse you, and I am going to give you the slip, and the slip is the handwriting, and the handwriting is the only thing here that anybody can use."

She put the corner of card on the counter, face down.

"**That is yours. Take it off my counter and do not put it in this building again.**"

---

He took it off the counter and put it inside his jacket, in a pocket, and the pocket already had a docket in it with a count on it, and a drawer in a repair shop in Lattice Ward still holds five things and this is not one of them.

"One more thing," said Sunniva Bellhouse, "**and then I have got a job at half past three and I am not going to be late for it because I have been the woman with the shop for nineteen years and I am not going to start being the woman with the story.**"

"**Go on.**"

"The five pounds." She nodded at the money in the drawer. "**A hundred and forty-one pounds is eleven weeks of a gas bill and it is a hundred and forty-one pounds, and a levy officer in Crown Terrace paid a man of fifty-one a hundred and forty pounds and travel to stand up in a room and say a true sentence out loud, and he was owed nothing and he said the number out loud in the room, and I read about that in a handout.**" She shut the drawer. "One pound. That is all that is between the two of them. There is no connection. I want that on the page and I want it said by somebody other than me, because if it is said by me it is a story and if it is said by you it is an arithmetic, and it is an arithmetic, and it is the only arithmetic in this whole thing that I have been able to sleep on."
