# Chapter 121 — Two Kinds Of Paper

The box by the door in the Saltmarket market office had a lid on it by the Monday morning of week thirty-five, and the lid had a rubber band round it, and the rubber band had been round it since about the Wednesday.

The handback form had come on the Friday with the district internal post. It was one side of A4 with four boxes and a reference and a date, and the fourth box was the one anybody in this case has ever cared about, and it wanted the name of the person who collected the items.

Fenna Rudd had written **holding officer: market office** in it at ten past five on the Friday afternoon, after the fish, and she had not rubbed it out.

She is thirty-one and she is a market clerk and she has been one for about six years and she wrote the notice on the board herself in the middle of last week because four men were in her office before nine in the morning and every one of them was frightened of a piece of card.

---

He came through the door of that office at a quarter past nine, on a Monday, at grade two, in a work jacket, four days a week, with a job sheet in his hand because he had been at a customer site at seven and the sheet says what the next thing is.

She put the handback form face up on the counter between them and did not offer him the chair.

"**Before you ask, and so that you do not have to look like a man who is about to ask,**" she said, "you are not getting the two."

"I had not got as far as it."

"**You had got as far as putting the bag down.**"

He put the bag down. It had a spare meter in it and a pen-torch and a coil of three-core and nothing else, and it was the bag he carries on a Monday, and she had been looking at it for four seconds.

"And before you say the next thing, which is that you are not asking as a person," she said, "**I know you are not. You never are. You are asking as a person with a lanyard and a job sheet and a company's card, and the last time you were in this office I refused you a sheet with eleven names on it and I was right, and I have thought about it every day since and I am still right, and I have got a district letter in the drawer under this counter that says the same thing in nine words.**"

"**No requirement at this stage for a record of individuals holding unofficial materials.**"

"**You have read it.**"

"**I have read it four times.**"

"Then you know what I am." She turned the handback round. "**I am the requirement. I made the sheet. The stage is this stage. And the handback wants a name in the fourth box and I have put the office in it, and the office is not a person, and if I put a person in it then in about four years there is a document that says a woman called Fenna Rudd personally held eleven practice cards in a market office and transferred them, and the eleven people are not in that document and I am in it for the rest of my life.**"

The office door was open behind her and nine people were walking past it with boxes on shoulders, and a man of sixty came in, put two hundred grams of something on the counter, and went out again, and none of that was for him.

---

"What I have come for," said Marek Senn, "**is not the two. I have come for the sheet, which I am also not getting, and for one thing that is not on anybody's list, which is whether the two are on the same paper.**"

Fenna Rudd looked at him for four seconds.

"**Which two.**"

"**The two of the nine that are copies. The same fifth line, the same hand, the same pen.**"

"**On different paper.**"

"**I know.**"

"**Do you know why.**"

"**No.**"

"**I have known since about the Wednesday and I have not told anybody and I have been sitting on it for four days, and I am going to tell you now because you are going to go and look at it in a car park and get it wrong, and I would rather you got it right out of my mouth than out of a photocopy.**"

---

She took the lid off the box and did not take the rubber band off, and she did not take the two cards out of the box.

She put her hand in to the second knuckle, brought out two pieces of card, and laid them on the counter under a strip light that has been flickering since the spring.

"**Hold them up. Not over the counter. Up, to the light.**"

He held them up.

"**Now look at the left-hand edge on both of them, and tell me what you see, and do not tell me what you think it means.**"

"**The top one is paler along the left edge than the bottom one, in a band about a centimetre wide, running the length of it.**"

"That is a second-generation copy," said Fenna Rudd. "**That is a copy of a copy. One has been off the machine twice and one has been off it once. It is not a fault and it is not a fault you would see on a screen. You see it on a wet day with a strip light and a bad bulb and a woman who has been cutting fish on that counter for six years.**"

He looked at it for a while.

"**How do you know.**"

"Because I buy the paper." She turned the top one over. "**The bottom one is the back of a wage packet. Everybody in this city has the back of a wage packet, it is a company thing, it is on a machine in a room with a door, and it comes out of a drawer in a pack.**"

"And the top one."

"The top one is the back of a stall price list." She put a finger on it. "**They are printed two-sided on a stock that comes off a van in reams, and the bottom half is the fish and the top half is the shell, and the ream is cut by hand down the long side, which is why the edge is straight on three sides and not quite straight on the fourth. I have been buying the same list off the same printer for about six years. It comes on a Wednesday.**"

"**Which printer.**"

"I do not know which printer." She said it flatly and did not soften it. "**I know which van. I have never been in the building. I know that about eleven stalls in this market buy off that van and about nine of us have been doing it since before the fish.**"

---

And then she said the thing she had been sitting on, and she said it while she was putting the two cards back, one at a time, and not while she was looking at him.

"**Now the other half of it, and this is why I have not told anybody and this is why I have been sitting on it for four days.**"

"**Go on.**"

"I counted the box on the Wednesday. Eleven. I counted it again on the Friday when the form came, and it was eleven, and I have got the count in my own hand on the back of the notice." She put the lid back on. "**I counted it on the Wednesday before I put the lid on for the first time. And this one, the pale one, the copy of the copy, was in the box on the Wednesday, and the Wednesday is the morning I put a notice on that board, and the Friday before that nobody in this building knew there was a box.**"

The strip light went off and came on again.

"So it was in here before I opened the door," she said. "**Which means the box is not where it came from. Which means the box was never where it came from. Which means the thing I have been doing for two weeks, which is holding it, is not holding the start of anything, it is holding the middle of something that was already going, and I have been the person in charge of the middle of it since about the Wednesday of last week and I did not know and I still do not.**"

---

"**Who put it in.**"

"**I do not know.**"

"**You said the office opens at four.**"

"**The office opens at four and there are nine people in this building between four and half past five and every one of them is a stallholder or their lad, and on the Wednesday morning the box was not there until I put it there, so nobody in this building put anything in it.**" She looked at the lid. "Which is the good news and it took me four days and it is not a good piece of news, it is just where the box is not."

"That is a relief in a way."

"**It is a relief in exactly the way that a thing being somebody else's problem is a relief, and I have had that feeling twice this month and it has cost me more both times.**"

---

The woman of forty-four came in at ten past ten and she came in the way people come in who have been thinking about something since the previous week and have not slept about it, which is quickly, and with her coat already half off.

She is agency. She has been on the west berth on Fridays for nine years. She put a card in the box at twenty past nine in the morning of the Monday of week thirty-four without being asked to, and it had the most writing on it of the eleven, and she asked whether hers was the dangerous one and was told no, and then said that no was what she had done on the Friday and it was what put her in the box.

"They are taking the box," she said. "**I saw the form on the counter. My card is in the box. I want my card.**"

"They are taking the box," said Fenna Rudd, "**and the box is mine and not yours, and the notice says cards will be returned, and returned to the office that took them, which is this one, which means returned to me, which means you will have to ask me for it in about a fortnight and I will give it to you then and not before, because the second time a person asks a person for their own card in a fortnight that is a different conversation and it is a worse one and I have seen it happen twice.**"

"**A fortnight.**"

"**A fortnight.**"

The woman of forty-four put her coat back on and then took it off again, which is what people do.

"Are you going to ask me again," she said to Marek Senn. "**About whether mine is the dangerous one.**"

"No."

"**Because I have decided something about it and I want you to know that I have decided it, because you said the word keep working to me in this office nine days ago and it is the four least useful words anybody has ever said to me.**"

"**I remember.**"

"I am going to keep working," she said. "**I have thought about it for nine days on about nineteen buses and I am going to keep working, and I have said that to two people this week and I am saying it to you, and none of the three of us is going to be able to say in about four years whether it was the right call and that is what a decision is.**"

Then she went out, and she had not asked, and it had taken her nine days and about nineteen buses to arrive at a sentence that four words had made necessary.

---

The district rang at twenty to twelve and it was a woman of thirty-four called Rosalind Toomes who said her name first, which he had not expected, and who read out the reference on the handback form before she said anything else.

"I am not asking for the names," she said. "**I want to be clear that I have not asked for the names and that I am aware of what your district's letter says and it is in the same drawer as this form, and I have read that one too.**"

"**Then what are you asking for.**"

"An itemised list." She said it the way people say a thing they have been told to say. "**Eleven items, a count by description, and a signature at the bottom, and the form has a box that says *received by* and the box wants a name in it and I am the person who will be receiving them, and I have given my name, because I am a person and I have a job and that is what the box is for.**"

"And the fourth box on the handback wants the name of the person who collected them."

"Which is a different box," said Rosalind Toomes, "**and I am not going to argue with you about which of the two is correct, and I would like you to know that I have looked at that box for nine seconds and I do not know what it is for either, and I have been in this job four years and that is the first box I have not been able to fill in.**"

---

He said thank you and put the phone down and stood in a market office in the Saltmarket with a bag at his feet that had a spare meter in it.

She watched him not do the thing.

"**You are not going to ask me for the sheet.**"

"No."

"**Say why.**"

"**Because you were right last time and you have got a district letter in the drawer that agrees with you, and because a count is not a list and you told me the difference in four minutes in this office nine days ago and I have not got an argument that beats it, and because I have been refused eight times in a fortnight and every single one of the eight was correct, and I am not going to be the ninth man in a corridor telling a woman of thirty-one that her own sheet is the problem.**"

Fenna Rudd wrote something on the back of the notice with a biro and did not show him.

"**That is nine,**" she said.

"What is nine."

"Refusals." She put the pen down. "**You counted them out loud in my office and I have been keeping them, and I am a market clerk and I do not keep anything, and I have got nine in a column on the back of a notice that is going on a board in about four days, and I am going to put the nine on the board with the box, and I am going to be asked by a man in a district office why a market office is counting refusals and I have not got an answer for him and I have decided not to have one.**"

---

The handback goes on the Friday at four, and she will carry it herself, in a plastic wallet, in a carrier bag, on a bus, eleven cards and one side of A4, and the fourth box on the form is going to say **market office** again, and the district is going to call that a refusal, and the district is going to be right, and about four years from now somebody is going to read a document that says eleven items and no names and is going to assume that was a decision somebody made on purpose.

And in four seconds on a counter under a strip light, a woman who sells fish put a copy of a copy back in a box and said out loud that the box was the middle of something rather than the start of it, and the only person in this city who could have asked her the next useful question was standing in front of her with a bag of meters, and did not ask it.
