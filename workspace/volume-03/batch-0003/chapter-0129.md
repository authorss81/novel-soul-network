# Chapter 129 — A Job On A Programme

There were about forty folding chairs set out in rows in a room in Crown Terrace on the Thursday evening of the week of the thirty-six and about nine of them were empty, and a microphone on a stand at the front with a chair beside it, and a trestle table by the door with about four hundred leaflets on it in a stack, and a man of about fifty-eight in a fleece at the door with a list, counting, because that is what a committee sends a man to do.

The fourth of eleven sessions. *Supervised confirmation and a technician's own judgement.* Admission free. **Fee paid.**

The panel was five and all five of it was on the front of the leaflet, listed by job, and three of the five were jobs and two of them were people printed as a job as well, and there was no way of telling from the leaflet which of them was which.

*District practice assurance officer, in the chair. District service manager. District practitioner, release callout. Independent practitioner. Independent practitioner.*

Marek Senn was one of the two people, and the reason he was one of the two people was that he was the only person on every job sheet in the week of the thirty-fourth, and nobody chose him, and a man of fifty-two had said so in a corridor and had stopped counting the number of times he had offered *it checked*.

He raised it in the corridor at half past six, before the doors, which is the correct time to raise it and which cost him the whole of the first four minutes of his own evening.

"**I have a condition on a share and it says I may not demonstrate, teach, assess or assist at any practice in that department, on its premises, under its insurance, at any time, unlimited by hours. Is this that.**"

Corin Vasska read the panel on the leaflet and then read it again.

"No," he said. "**This is an evening session about a question. It is not a practice, it is not on your department's premises, it is not under your department's insurance, and nothing in this room is going to be assessed and I will put that in the minutes in those words if you want it in the minutes.**"

"Then I will sit on it."

"You will sit on it, and I am going to say the other half, and I am saying it because I would rather you heard it in a corridor at half past six than worked it out at ten past eight in front of forty people, which is what is going to happen otherwise." He put the leaflet down on the table. "**The condition stops you doing the thing. It does not stop you being asked about the thing by a member of the public in a room with a microphone, and there is no mechanism in this city that can tell the difference between the two, because the mechanism would have to be a rule about who may be asked, and a rule about who may be asked is a rule about who is findable, and four of the five people on that panel are findable.**"

---

The other person printed as a job was a man of forty-nine from a firm in Ashfields who had driven in and who said his name once and then did not say it again.

Peter Amado, about fifty-one, grey jacket, notebook, glasses he took off and did not clean, was in the third row with the notebook open on his knee and a pen in it, and he had four lines in it and three of them were crossed out, and he had crossed them out himself and in his own car.

The chair read section four of the specification out loud, in the room, to the people it is about, which is the practice of that office and which is the only thing about that office anybody in this city has ever been able to say is decent.

Then he asked for questions and nine people put their hands up and he took three.

The first was about the fee, and he answered it with the number, because the number is in the specification and he is not going to be the officer who makes the number smaller in a room.

The second was a man of forty-six who wanted to know whether attendance made anybody competent, and the answer is no, and it is printed at the bottom of the leaflet in a type about a millimetre smaller, and it has been at the bottom of the leaflet since the spring, and there were nine people in that room who had never turned the leaflet over.

---

The third question was from a man of thirty-three who was not counted at the door because the man of fifty-eight in the fleece counts everybody, and who stood up at ten past eight and said his name and did not give a surname.

He had a card in his hand. Four printed steps and a fifth line in a hand that got smaller towards the end.

"I got these off a table in a café," he said, "**and I have given out about nine of them and I have not been paid by anybody and I do not know who printed them and I have got one in my kitchen drawer and my wife asked me on Sunday why it is in there with the takeaway menus.**"

"**What is your question,**" said the chair.

"That is my question," said the man of thirty-three. "**Which of the two is safer. Yours, or that.**"

---

The room did what rooms do.

The chair said thank you and said the panel would take it and the man said that is fine and sat down, and eleven people in the second and third rows turned round to look at each other, and the man of fifty-eight in the fleece stopped counting for four seconds and then started again.

The chair said: "**That is a question for the panel. I will take it in order. District service manager.**"

Wyn Dunleavy, fifty-two, said he was going to pass, and he said why, and it was that his office held no list of anything and that the honest answer to the question is that the panel cannot compare a document it has not seen with a document it has issued, and that he would like it minuted that he said the panel cannot.

The man of forty-nine from Ashfields said he had not seen the card either, and that his firm had eleven people on a Friday and that he could not answer for any of them and would not try.

Which left two.

The chair looked down the table and said: "**District practitioner, release callout.**"

---

Marek Senn had four seconds and he had been in about four hundred rooms in a term and a half and he had never once not known what the next sentence was.

What he knew was this. That he had a piece of card about two inches by three in an inside pocket and that it was the master of four hundred and thirty-two of them. That he was forbidden to assess, and that a room with a microphone and forty folding chairs and nine people taking notes is an assessment in everything except the word, and that the word is the only thing protecting anybody in this room, and that it was a word in a form in a company's system. That the answer to the question needed him to say what the word was, out loud, in a room, and that he had said it once in front of four hundred and six people and had not been believed and had said he would not say it twice, and that he was twenty-two and had a Friday rota and eleven people a week who were going to be taught to wait for a man in a lanyard.

He also knew that the answer to the question was: he did not know, and that the true answer was longer than four seconds.

He took nine.

"I am not going to answer that as the panel," he said, "**and I am going to say why in forty seconds and then somebody else can do it, and I would like the chair to stop me if I am talking over somebody because I have been told four times this fortnight that I do it.**"

The chair said: "**Go ahead.**"

---

"**The card he is holding has four correct steps and a fifth line in somebody's handwriting, and the fifth line says if the word does not come, withdraw and report, and there is no name and no version and no company on it anywhere, and about nine of them went out in six weeks in one district and there is no mechanism in this city that can withdraw a thing nobody has admitted to issuing.**"

He looked at the man of thirty-three.

"**I know where it was printed. I was in the shop on Friday. I know the payment and I know it was a hundred and forty-one pounds out of a mailbox in eleven instalments, and I know the woman who printed them has been in that shop for nineteen years and she did not write the fifth line and she has kept the master and she is not going to be the one in this story and I have asked her for nothing.**"

"**How do you know that,**" said the chair.

"Because I have got it in my pocket," said Marek Senn, "**and I have not decided what to do with it, and if somebody in this room would like to know what is on it then they can have it, and it is a piece of card about two inches by three and it is the only copy in this city and the woman who printed four hundred and thirty-two of them has kept it in a drawer for six weeks in case somebody came.**"

Nobody put a hand up. Two people at the back looked at each other.

Then he said the rest of it, and it was the best sentence he has said in a room in a term and a half and it was not about himself and it was eleven words long.

"**The two are the same shape and only one of them is a name.**"

---

And then a man of sixty-one stood up in the fourth row, and he had been in the room for about an hour, and he had a bit of pale card in his hand that he had had in his pocket since the summer.

He is Steve Akers. He has been at a barrier on a contract in another company's building for four years, and he wrote out nine words on a card in the middle of last term, and a man in a grey jacket came to the gate and told him he did not need a copy, and he has not been back in nine weeks, and a room of seventy people were told about that on the Friday of week thirty-three and the man in the lanyard said from the back of the room that the man has still got it.

Nobody knew that. Nobody in that room knew that the man in the grey jacket was three rows in front of him.

"**I have got one,**" he said.

---

And Marek Senn kept talking.

That is the whole of it and it is in the record and it is in this book, and it took four seconds. He had nine words in him about a fifth line and a shape and a name, and a man of sixty-one in the fourth row had said *I have got one* in a room of forty people, and Marek Senn said the next four seconds of what he had been saying, and the room heard two things at once and one of them got covered.

He stopped. It took him a second and a half to stop and four seconds to work out that he had stopped.

"**I am sorry,**" he said. "Go on."

The man of sixty-one said: "**It is all right. I have got about nine words on it and I have had it since the summer and I have never shown it to anybody and I have been told I did not need one.**"

"**Who told you that.**"

"**A man in a grey jacket. About nine weeks ago. At a gate.**"

The chair said, "**The panel is going to have to take that in writing,**" and the man of sixty-one said, "No," and said it immediately, and nine people in that room did not know what to do with that.

He said: "**No, and I will tell you why, and then I will sit down. I have been at that barrier for four years and I have a bit of card in my pocket that a man in a grey jacket told me I did not need, and if I put it on a table in a room in Crown Terrace then in about four years there is a document with nine words on it in it, and a man of sixty-one is the only person in this city who can be found from it, and I would like to keep being able to walk past a barrier for another four years.**"

He sat down.

---

Peter Amado read his notebook at twenty to nine, and he had asked for the microphone and he had not been asked to and he did not ask anybody's permission, and he read the fourth line and the other three were crossed out in his own hand in his own car.

"**I have got four lines in this book and I have crossed out three of them, and they were worse than the line in the specification and I was paid nothing extra for them, and the one I have not crossed out is the only one I have ever said that I actually believe, and I have been standing at a microphone for four Fridays looking for a fifth one and there is not one.**"

He put the notebook down.

"**It says that a thing cannot be safe because a lot of people have been given it, and a thing cannot be dangerous because a lot of people have been frightened of it, and those are the two things everybody in this city has been arguing about for a term and a half, and I have looked for the third thing every Friday since the middle of the summer and there is not a third thing and I have been paid a hundred and forty pounds and travel for a line in a specification, and I would like somebody in this room to tell me which of the two I said in a market in the week of the twenty-four, because it was one of them, and I have not got a note.**"

Nobody told him.

Then he said the sentence that is going to be true in about four years and that nobody in that room could hear, and it was not written down:

"**And I am not going to find out in a room like this one, and I knew that on the first Friday, and I have come four times anyway.**"

---

Oren Vey stood up at ten past nine and he was in a suit and he was not on the panel and he was owed nothing and nobody thanked him.

He was right about the card. He said it in about nine words and he said it as an operations director and not as a man with an opinion, and nine people in that room wrote it down, and the words were: four hundred and thirty-two, four correct steps, no version, no name, nine places in six weeks, and no recall mechanism exists for a document nobody admits to issuing.

Then he was wrong about the other thing, and he said it out loud, and he said it in the same breath as the first half, and there is a sentence in this case about a man of forty-four who did that on purpose.

"**And I have been saying since the middle of last term that they are the same, and I was wrong about the part of it that matters, and I do not know what the part is, and I am not going to be asked for it and I am not going to pretend to know.**"

The chair said, "**You have said a company is going to describe a card it has not seen as the same family as a practice on a company's own material. Do you want that minuted.**"

"**I want that minuted word for word,**" said Oren Vey.

Then he said the third thing, which is the only unasked fact anybody has been given in six weeks, and he said it standing up, in a room, with his name and his job attached to it, and it took thirty seconds.

"**Page two of the six-sided handout that went out with the leaflets in the spring. There is a photograph on it of a card in a plastic sleeve on a lanyard, about the size of a postage stamp, and the four steps are legible in it, and it is ours, and about four thousand people have got that page, and I found out about it on Friday morning from a technician in a repair shop, and we have been telling the trade since the spring that the sequence is issued on a card and on nothing else, and that is no longer true and it stopped being true in the spring.**"

Corin Vasska stood up.

"That is the second thing in nine days," he said, "**that a man has said to me in this series that I have to take to a committee, and I have been an officer of a levy for four years and I have never once had to take a thing to a committee that I did not put in the specification myself, and I want to say the other half, and I am going to say it now while there are forty people in this room and not in four weeks in an office.**"

He said: "**A programme with a name on it can be removed. My series is a job on a piece of paper. On Thursday of week thirty-four nine hundred rooms got a leaflet with my series on it and no name on it, and on Wednesday of this week a district form put a name in a drop-down for a group because the form would not submit without one, and that name is the name on the leaflet, and there is no document in this city that joins those two things and I have looked for one for about four days.**"

The room did not go quiet. About nine people said something at once.

---

Marguerite Okpara was in the fifth row and she was twenty-nine and she works Fridays and she has a card and a bus to catch and she was not on any list in that room, and she came on her own time on a Thursday, and she did not ask a single question, and Marek Senn could not work out why she was there and it is the only thing in the chapter that frightened him.

At half past nine she caught his eye across the front of a room and put one hand flat on the back of the chair in front of her, once, and then took it away, and neither of them said one word.

The session ended at about ten. About nine chairs were still empty. The fee was paid to two people and one of them had produced nothing and one of them had produced a card and a five-letter number and eleven words he had not been allowed to finish.

At the door a man of fifty-eight in a fleece was still counting, because he had not been told that the count was over.
