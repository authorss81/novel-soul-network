# Chapter 114 — The Tenth Time

He took a bus and then walked the last mile so that he would not arrive nine minutes past, which is a thing he has done about four times in two volumes and which is the only private discipline he has got left.

The gate asks for nothing.

That is the first fact about it and it is the fact that everybody who has ever been here remembers, and it is the reason about nine men who are not the company's work here, and it is the reason a man of twenty-two can walk in off a public road on a Thursday afternoon in a jacket with a bag and nobody has to be told anything. **The shutter is up to about a foot, then a chain, then a card reader with a light on it that has never in about four years been tested. Then a hut.**

The hut had a kettle and a chair and a rack of leaflets for a charity giving away bread, and a man of sixty-one in a chair who said his name before he was asked, and then said he was going to write it down, and wrote it down.

"**You have not changed your mind about the card on the post.**"

"**No,**" said Steve Akers.

"Then it has not been taken down."

"**Nobody has taken it down. Ask Bryn. Ask the foreman. He is in the office with the door propped and he has been in the office with the door propped since about the spring.**"

---

The prefab office had a desk, a chair, a heater, a kettle and a filing tray, and the tray was the reason he had come, and the tray was on the desk with nine other things in it and the printout was on the top of all of them because it was in the way.

The foreman was fifty-eight and he had a pencil behind his ear and a site diary open on the desk with a beer mat holding the pages down, and he had been at this site for thirty-one years and he had been in this office with the door propped since about the spring.

"**You are the boy who came in the summer,**" he said.

"**I am.**"

"**With a lamp.**"

"**With a lamp.**"

"**That lamp is on that wall and it is a different lamp,**" said the foreman, "and the ballast came off the same wagon as that gate, and the ballast was eleven months old and it was going, and I want to say that first, before anything else, because you have come on about four occasions with about nine excuses and I would like the record to be accurate."

"It was going."

"**It was going, and I would have replaced it in the summer, and your boy is the reason it got replaced in week twenty-two, and I am not going to pretend to you that that was charity. If you had not turned up it would have gone in about six weeks and it would have gone dark in a gate hut at about eleven at night, and I would have found out from a man in a van.**" He took the pencil from behind his ear and put it down on the diary, not in his hand, which is a thing a man does when he wants both hands visible. "So that is the lamp and that is accurate, and now you are going to tell me what you actually came for, and I am going to let you get about a third of the way there before I stop you, because I have got nothing until two."

---

Marek Senn said the first third, which was that there was a printout in a tray on a desk in a prefab office and that he had read page nine of it four times.

"**In the spring,**" said Bryn Maw. "You stood where you are standing and you had it in your hand and you read the bottom of the last page and then you put it back on the top of the tray, and I watched you do it, and I have not asked you once, and I want to tell you that I noticed and I have not asked, and both of those are on purpose."

"You noticed."

"**I have been on this gate for thirty-one years and I have watched about nine hundred people put a paper down and pick it up again, and about nine hundred of them were doing exactly what you were doing, and nine of them came back a second time, and there is one of you standing in my office.**"

He reached over and moved the top of the tray four inches, and did not take the printout out, and left it face down on the desk with the ninth page under his own forearm.

"**Go on then, and read it, and do not pretend to me that you are reading it for the first time, because I have been in this trade for thirty-one years and I have never once seen a man read a page for the first time with a pencil in his pocket.**"

---

He read it.

Nine pages, top of the tray, brought out because it was in the way, and the upstream field blank, and about two inches below the field the words *Meridian consolidation point, lot onward* in a stores clerk's hand on a venue line, and two inches below that a box number written in biro in a different hand by a different person about two years ago, because a man on a wagon asked where the thing came from.

**He read it a fifth time. He did not write it down a fifth time.**

He had read it in the summer and twice since and once on the Friday of week thirty, and the number had been in his head since the summer, and there was one number in this entire case that had never been on a piece of paper that anybody else could hold, and the reason for that was not integrity and had never been integrity.

"The clerk typed a lot onward on a venue line."

"**On a venue line,**" said Bryn Maw. "I do not know what a venue line is. I know what I have got in front of me, which is a page of a printout with half of it blank and a number on it, and that number is a box, and boxes are not places I can send a van to, and I have asked twice and both times I was told it is not a site and not a supplier, and the second time I was told it politely, which is how I know somebody had been told that before."

"You have asked twice."

"**I have asked twice in two years and I did not write either answer down, and I have not written this one down either, and that is not because I am careful, it is because there is nowhere here to write it. There is a tray. The tray goes at the end of the year.**"

"When is the end of the year."

"**Thirteen weeks,**" said Bryn Maw. "And then the trays get emptied into a container outside the office and the container goes on a wagon and the wagon goes, and I have never once been told where."

---

"Write it down," said Bryn Maw.

Marek Senn did not have a pen and Bryn Maw had given him a pencil off the desk without being asked and put it flat on the printout, and the pencil was a carpenter's pencil with a flattened lead and it had a foreman's grip on it, and it was the only object on that desk that was not company property.

"**No,**" said Marek Senn.

"**No,**" said Bryn Maw, taking the pencil back. "That is the correct answer and I want to say why it is correct, because in thirty-one years I have had about nine people say no to me at this desk and about four hundred people say yes, and the nine are the only people I have ever had to think about, and every one of the nine was a man who knew that a number written down is a number somebody can ring."

"You have four of them here."

"**I have nine men on this site who are not on any company's books, and not one of them is on a book at this gate, and the reason there is no book at this gate is that I decided in about year nine of being in this office that a book at a gate is a list of people, and a list of people is a thing that can be asked for in about four years by somebody who wants to know who was here on a Thursday.**" He tapped the desk once with the flat of the pencil. "So you can sign in if you want. I will not stop you. There is nothing in this office that stops a man signing in, and if I put a book on this desk then in about four years a district office or a company or a man with a clipboard can ask me who was on this site on the day, and I will have to answer with nine names, and four of those nine are not on anybody's books and one of them is fifty-eight years old and does not want to be found in a file by a stranger."

"I did not ask to sign in."

"**I know you did not, and I would like it on the record that you did not, and I would like it on the record that I offered, because a man who has been refused a thing and a man who was never offered a thing are two different men at four years, and I have been in this office long enough to have seen one of them turn into the other.**"

---

They walked out to the shutter at twenty past two because the foreman said the kettle was on and Marek Senn said he had to be somewhere, and neither of those was true.

The control post is about six metres inside the shutter and there is a plastic horn in a cage on it, and on the face of the post there is a laminated sheet gone cloudy with age, held on by two cloudy cable ties, one of which has a buckle somebody put there, and it has nine words on it and no name, no date, no company and no version.

*RELEASE IS GIVEN BY ANOTHER VOICE. DO NOT RELEASE ON YOUR OWN JUDGEMENT.*

Bryn Maw stood in front of it with his hands in his pockets.

"**I will tell you why that is still up,**" he said. "I have had nine conversations with people about that card in two years and I have never once taken it down, and every one of those conversations has been somebody telling me it is a hazard, and one of them was a man from a company and one of them was a woman from a district office, and both of them were wrong, and I have got nine words for why I am right and you are going to think they are stupid."

"Go on."

"**It is the only document on this site.**" He said it the way a foreman says a thing that has been checked. "Everything else on this site is gear and pallets and a kettle and a container that goes on a wagon. There is not one other piece of paper in this yard that says a person is waiting for a person. There is not one other piece of paper in this yard that says what a person is supposed to do if the waiting goes on. And I have been standing in this hole for thirty-one years and I know exactly what I am doing and I could not tell you, in a kitchen, in words, what I am doing, and I have got nine entries in this diary in pencil and every one of them is the same entry."

He took the diary off the beer mat, turned it round and put it on the corner of the post so that it was open at a page with ten short lines in pencil, and each line had a date and a number and one word.

"**Ten times this year, a load stood on a hook and there was nobody to say the word, and the word is on that card, and on the eleventh I put the load down myself and I went home and I told my wife I had a headache and she said you have had a headache since the spring.**" He turned the diary back. "That card is the only thing on this site that says what the tenth time is. Nine words and no name and it is the only document I have got, and I have got no name to ring and no company and no version, and if I take it down then I have got nothing at all and I am a man of fifty-eight deciding on my own in a yard, and I have done that once this year already and I have got a diary about it."

Marek Senn stood in front of a laminated sheet with two cloudy cable ties on it and worked out, in nine seconds, that he had spent a term and a half proving that a card with no name on it is a hinge, and that a foreman with a pencil has kept the same nine words for two years because they are a brake, and that both of those are true and that he has no argument that reaches both.

He did not say any of that.

What he said was: "**A man of fifty in a grey jacket read that card out to Steve in the hut. He wrote it out on a bit of card off his own pad and photographed it and then told Steve he did not need a copy of it, because it was on the company record.**"

"**That is what Steve has told me,**" said Bryn Maw. "And Steve has told me that for about two weeks, and before that he told me nothing, and I did not notice the change, and I am going to be honest with you about that, because I would rather be a man who did not notice than a man who made a speech."

Marek Senn looked at the card on the post.

"**Do you think it is on the company record.**"

"**I have got no idea,**" said Bryn Maw, "and I have not asked, and I am not going to ask now, and if you are about to tell me then I would rather you did not, because I have a diary with ten lines in it and I would like to go on being a man whose diary has ten lines in it rather than a man whose diary has eleven because a boy of twenty-two told me something in a yard."

---

Steve Akers had come out of the hut by then and was standing nine feet off, with a card in his hand that had not been in the hut in week thirty-one, pale, with nine words on it in biro and a date on it that was a Saturday.

"**You told me not to keep a copy,**" he said to Marek Senn. "Did he."

"No. He told you."

"**I know he told me,**" said Steve Akers. "I am asking whether you told me, and there is a difference and I would like somebody to hear me say that there is a difference."

Nobody said anything for eleven seconds, and the shutter went up about a foot higher on a bus that was not there.

"**No,**" said Marek Senn. "I have not told you and I am not going to tell you, and the reason is not that I do not know. The reason is that you are asking me to take a thing off a man of sixty-one who has been asked for it once, and the only reason I would take it off you is if you asked me, and you are not asking me, you are asking whether he was telling the truth, and that is a different question and it is the one I cannot answer, and I have been asked the other one by four people in a term and a half and I have said no four times."

"**Right,**" said Steve Akers, and put the card in his pocket, and went back into the hut, and did not say anything else for the rest of the afternoon.

---

He got the last bus at half past three and he had a folder with a job sheet in it and the folder was lighter than it had been in the summer.

On the seat in front of him, folded in four, were two dockets. One of them was four lines written in his own hand about a foreman of forty-four who wanted one name to ring. The other one was four lines about a man of thirty-one who had signed nothing.

In the summer he had come here and invented a fault to have a reason to come back, because a company technician who goes to a site and comes back without a reason is a fault somebody hears about, and he had found a ballast with eleven months on it and booked it and it had been a real fault, and the real fault had made the false reason true, and he had never told anybody which of the two had been the reason.

**On the Thursday of week thirty-three he did not write the number down a fifth time, and the reason he gave, in a notebook in a pub, to a man of forty-four who asked him why not, was arithmetic.**

"Because the tray goes in thirteen weeks, and if I write the number in a book, then in thirteen weeks the tray goes and the number is in a book, and a book in a shop is a copy somebody can be found by, and a number in a box is a person's mail. **And the other half, which I did not say to him, is that I am the only person in this city who has it, and I am twenty-two, and I am the most findable person in this city, and there is no version of a document in which those two facts sit next to each other and stay separate.**"

---

*117. Thursday of week 33, eleven at night in a flat above a shop in Lattice Ward, and this entry is about a foreman of fifty-eight with a pencil and a carpenter's pencil with a flattened lead, and about a man of sixty-one with a bit of pale card in his pocket, and about a number on page nine of a printout in a tray that goes in thirteen weeks.*

*The site.* **A gate that asks for nothing: a shutter to a foot, a chain, a card reader with a light on it that has never been tested in about four years, and a hut with a kettle and a chair and a rack of leaflets for a charity giving away bread.** Nine men who are not the company's. A prefab office with a desk, a chair, a heater, a kettle and a filing tray, and a door propped since about the spring, and a foreman who has been at this gate for thirty-one years and has been standing in the same hole since he was twenty-seven, which is what a foreman of fifty-eight has to say about himself in this city.

*The tray, and the fifth time.* **Nine pages on the top of it because it was in the way. The upstream field blank. Two inches below it *Meridian consolidation point, lot onward* on a venue line in a stores clerk's hand. Two inches below that a box number in biro, a different hand, a different person, about two years ago, written because a man on a wagon asked where the thing came from.** I read it a fifth time. **I did not write it down a fifth time, and I have now read that page five times in thirty days, and the number has been in my head since the summer, and the reason is not integrity.** A lot onward is not a place a van can be sent to and he has asked twice and been told twice, and the second time he was told politely, which is how he knows somebody had been told that before. **The tray goes in thirteen weeks and then the trays go into a container outside the office and the container goes on a wagon and he has never once been told where.**

*And the book, and the pencil.* **He gave me a carpenter's pencil off the desk without being asked and put it flat on the printout, and I said no.** That is the sixth refusal of a term and a half and it is the only one I have ever had handed to me by a man who did not want anything. **A book at a gate is a list of people, and a list of people can be asked for in about four years by somebody who wants to know who was here on a Thursday, and four of his nine men are not on anybody's books.** He offered. He said the offer out loud and said that a man who was refused a thing and a man who was never offered a thing are two different men in about four years. **I did not ask to sign in and I have written that down because it is the only clean thing I did in about ten days, and a man of fifty-eight prepared a thing for it that I did not want, and that is not a thing I am going to be able to put anywhere.**

*The card, and the tenth time, and the thing I have got wrong.* **Nine words, no name, no date, no company, no version, two cloudy cable ties, one with a buckle somebody put there.** I have spent a term and a half proving that a card with no name on it is a hinge, and a foreman with a pencil has had it on a post for about two years because it is a brake, and both are true. **It is the only document on that site and everything else on that site is gear and pallets and a kettle and a container that goes on a wagon, and there is not one other piece of paper in that yard that says a person is waiting for a person, and that man cannot tell me in a kitchen what he is doing, and has got ten lines in pencil in a site diary, and every one of them is the same entry, and on the eleventh he put the load down himself and told his wife he had a headache.** **That card is the only thing on that site that says what the tenth time is.** He is right and I am right and I have got no argument that reaches both, and I have had a term and a half to find one, and I did not find it, and I am not going to find it in a yard.

*And the bit of pale card.* **A man of fifty in a grey jacket read that sheet out to a man of sixty-one in a hut, wrote it out on a card off his own pad, photographed it, and told him he did not need a copy because it was on the company record. He was on the site list. He wrote the date on the card and the date was a Saturday and the Saturday is somebody's mother's date to the day.** Steve Akers asked me in front of the foreman whether he was telling the truth, and I said no, I have not told you and I am not going to tell you, and the reason is that you are asking me to take a thing off a man who has been asked for it once, and you are not asking me for that, you are asking me whether a stranger was truthful, and that is the other question. **He said: I know he told me, I am asking whether you told me.** And I said no, and that is a no I am going to be asked about again, and the only person in this city who can say what that man said that day is a man of fifty-one on a stage on a Friday evening who does not know that I know, and I am not going to be the one who tells him.

*And the bus, and the two dockets.* **The folder was lighter than it was in the summer and the ballast was real and the reason was not.** The lamp on that wall is a different lamp and the ballast came off the same wagon as the gate and it was eleven months old and it was going, and I invented a fault to have a reason to come back and a fault turned up inside four months and made the false reason true, and I have never told anybody which of the two was the reason and I am not going to. **I gave a man of forty-four in a pub the arithmetic and kept the other half, and the other half is that I am the only person in this city holding this number and I am twenty-two and there is no document in which those two facts sit next to each other and stay separate.** Entry 117. The book is at forty-three lines and will not be opened for three weeks, and a tray goes in thirteen, and a number goes with it, and there is one thing I got wrong today that I am not going to be able to argue my way out of, and it is that the nine words on that post are the ninth time somebody has been right about a card and I have been wrong about it, and both of those are about the same nine words.
