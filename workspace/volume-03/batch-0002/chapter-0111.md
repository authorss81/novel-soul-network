# Chapter 111 — Eleven Minutes A Day

The first job of the day was declined at four minutes to seven in the morning by a dispatcher of forty-one called Jo Fisk, on a phone, in a corridor, to a man who had driven eleven miles for it.

*Roller door, jam, cold store, Nine Bridges. Broken down.*

"**Who is the attending contractor on that one.**"

That was the whole of it. That was the sentence, and it was in a system, and the system had a field on it that used to be a name and was a job, and Jo Fisk read the field out because that is what a field is for.

"**Sayer and Sons, gate three. They are on it from seven.**"

"Then I cannot send anybody."

"**I cannot send anybody,**" said Jo Fisk, "and I could not send anybody if the job said your name, because the clause went live at midnight and your name is not a reason, a name is not a reason, only a booking is."

The man on the other end of the corridor was fifty-eight and had a van at the gate and eleven miles in each direction before breakfast, and he said, "**Right,**" and put the phone down, and it was not Jo Fisk's fault, and Jo Fisk knew that it was not her fault, and Jo Fisk had a pad in front of her on which she wrote the time and the reference and the reason, and the reason she wrote was *attending contractor*, which is what the field said.

By eight o'clock there were four of them on the pad and by ten there were eleven, and the eleven were not the same eleven every Monday, and none of them was anybody's decision.

---

The muster at a quarter past eight is four minutes long and has never in nineteen years been five. Nine mechanics stood in the yard in the cold with their hoods down because a foreman of thirty-four once told a man from Wales that hoods up at a muster is a man who cannot hear you.

Nobody said the thing.

**Everybody in the yard had read the notice on Friday afternoon, and everybody in the yard had read the second thing, which was not on the notice, which was a leaflet in the corridor at the depot with a stylised open hand on it and a thumb and a forefinger about a millimetre apart and eleven session titles and a word *technician* in the fourth one.** Nobody said either of those things either.

What a man of thirty-one said at four minutes to nine, to nobody in particular and to Marek Senn in particular, was: "**So the district's off customer sites.**"

"That is the clause."

"**That is the clause and it is a fortnight old on the wall and it starts today.**"

"Then it starts today."

"**Right.**" He turned his back on it. That is the end of that conversation and it is the ordinary way a yard handles a thing nobody can fight.

---

Duncan Muir was not in the yard.

Marek Senn had a foreman's habit of knowing who was in a yard and who was not, and Muir had been the foreman of a cold store on a distribution road for nineteen years, and Muir was the only man in that yard whose job was a customer's site, and at ten past eight on the Monday of week thirty-three there was no Muir in it.

He rang at four minutes to nine from a depot two hours away.

"**Is the district attending Cold Street today.**"

"No."

"**And was the district attending Cold Street last Friday.**"

"Yes."

"**Right,**" said Duncan Muir. "Then somebody down there has put the Saturday roster out and my kid is on it, and I am not going to be there Saturday, and I would like somebody to tell me who to ring about that."

"Who do you normally ring."

"**The supervisor. On the site. I have never rung anybody else in nineteen years because there has never been anybody else.**"

Marek Senn looked down the corridor at the board with the eleven names on it and the Monday list and the Friday list, and the board did not distinguish between grade two and grade three and had never distinguished between anything.

"There is no one else," he said. "That is what the clause does. It does not take the job away. It moves who is allowed to be the one who turns up."

"I understand what a clause does," said Duncan Muir. "I have signed nine hundred of them."

"Then you know I cannot do that."

"**I know you cannot. I did not say you could. I said I would like somebody to tell me who to ring, and you have told me, and that is four words and they were correct, and I am going to have to go and tell a girl of seventeen that there is nobody to ring, and I have got to do it in a kitchen tonight, and I am going to have to do it in about nine words, and I have not got nine words.**"

The line went.

---

He thought about it for the rest of the morning, and he got it right eventually, and the way he got it right was to stop trying.

**Nobody decided to do this.** A service level of nine pages had been rewritten in the middle of last term by a procurement officer in a district office who was not a monster and had done it in a fortnight because the alternative on her desk was eleven men being on eleven sites for a company that had not bought the cover. **A man in a district jacket said in week twenty-one and again on the Thursday of week twenty-eight that in about three weeks somebody was going to be paid to say a true sentence out loud, and a man of fifty-one in a grey jacket is going to do it on a Friday evening in about four days, and neither of those men is the reason Duncan Muir cannot get to a canteen on a Saturday.**

He wrote it on the back of a job sheet at half past ten in his own hand with a date on it, and then he did the other thing, which was the thing that had cost him a term and a half and had not yet cost anybody else.

He wrote: *Duncan Muir, 44, 19 yrs, not in the yard on the first Monday. Wants one name to ring. There is no one else and there will not be one.*

---

At ten past ten he was at Civic Spine 7 with a plate in a bag for a Friday job, and there were eleven men in the corridor instead of the four who had stood there eight days ago, and Sera Quill came out of the back with her tool braid in one hand and read the notice from about eleven inches without touching it, the way she had read it eight days ago.

"**I am going to ask you for nine words,**" she said. "You are going to give me four again, and I am going to do the rest of it, and I am going to be quick about it, and then you can go and do your day."

"Nobody decided to do this."

"**Four, and they are the same four, and I said them on the Monday of last week and you have not added to them in eight days, which I am taking as a compliment and as a failure.**" She put the braid down on the bench. "And I am going to say now the thing that is worth something to a man of forty-four in a kitchen tonight, because that man has not got a schedule and that man has got a Saturday, and the four words are worth nothing to anybody who is not already in the room." She did not raise her voice, and eleven men in a corridor heard it, which she had not planned and did not mind. "**His attendance at that cold store is a subcontracted service and the subcontract is with the customer and the customer's own people are not the district's. A schedule is not a thing anybody votes on. Nobody in this city can be shown the person who changed it, and I cannot either, and the four people I can show it to are me, you, a dispatcher called Fisk and a clerk in a district office, and that is the entire audience, and about four people in this city have heard me say it, and one of them is a man who is going to be on four thousand leaflets and, if the district does what the district does, on about nine hundred of our own doors.**"

The nine men in the corridor had gone quiet in a way that Marek Senn could hear from nine feet away, and then one of them said, from the doorway, in the voice of a man of twenty-six who had been in that doorway eight days ago:

"**Is that us or is that them.**"

"**That is a schedule,**" said Sera Quill, "and you are a mechanic, and if you can get a schedule to answer for itself then you have got a week of my life back that I have not had since the spring."

---

He got the plate, and he checked it, and it was fine.

He also had a day that was supposed to be a day.

At twenty past one the second job on his sheet was declined, and this one was his, and Jo Fisk said the sentence about the attending contractor twice because the first time he asked her to check.

"**Check it.**"

"**I have checked it. The booking says the attending contractor and the attending contractor is a company, and there is no box on this system for a district technician, and there never has been, and the reason there never has been is that for thirty-one years nobody needed one.**"

"So my day is half a day."

"**Your day is half a day,**" said Jo Fisk, "and I am sorry, and I have written the reason, and the reason is the field, and the field is not mine."

A half day on a Monday at grade two is a day and a half of money on a Tuesday and a Wednesday, and there is no box in a company's system for that either, and he took it, and he went home at half past two, and at four o'clock he was on a distribution road in Nine Bridges collecting a district bench and a district lamp from a store room, because the district is still responsible for the district's own kit, and that part of it did not move.

The van from Sayer and Sons was in the yard.

There were four men in it and none of them had a district jacket on and one of them was thirty-one and was being told, patiently, by a site supervisor of thirty-eight with a clipboard, that the Saturday roster had gone out on the Monday morning and would not be coming back.

"**I am not saying Saturday is finished,**" she was saying. "I am saying Saturday is somebody else's now, and they bring their own two, and I have put the two names on the sheet, and I have not told either of them yet because I did not want to do it on an email."

The sheet was on the tailboard of a bench and Marek Senn read four names on it from nine feet away while he waited for a man with a key, and the third name on it was **Neve Muir**, and she was seventeen, and she had been doing the Saturday goods-in count at that cold store for two years, and it had paid for a course.

The supervisor saw him looking.

"**They are all right with it,**" she said. "The two that are leaving are not upset. They are going to a bigger site. I just do not do this on an email."

"Understood."

"**You are off the district, are you.**"

"Today."

"**Ah,**" she said, and looked at the van, and then at him, and then at the van again, and she was doing arithmetic about nineteen years in four seconds and she got the answer she got because the van was in the yard and he was in it with a lamp.

---

He put the lamp and the bench in the district's own van at twenty past four and drove back across the city with the radio off.

He did not ring Sera Quill, because she had said the whole of it to nine men and four people had heard it and one of them was going to be on about nine hundred rooms of post.

He did not ring Wyn Dunleavy, because Wyn Dunleavy had offered him *it checked and I cannot do anything about it* twice this term and once in a corridor, and offering it a fourth time in a week would be a man asking to be let off something.

He did not ring a woman four miles away, because it was Monday and because she had told him in week twenty-four to ring her on a Tuesday.

**What he did instead, at about ten past seven in the evening, in a repair shop in Lattice Ward with the shutter up and the lamp on over the bench, was write four lines on the back of a docket and fold it in four and put it in his own inside pocket, which is a thing he had never done with a docket in his life and did without deciding to.**

**The drawer under the bench still held five things and he did not put a sixth in it, and the reason is not integrity: a docket in a drawer is a piece of paper somebody else could come across, and a docket in a pocket is a piece of paper about a man who is already the most findable person in this city, and there is no version of that which is better.**

And then he sat there, because the thing he had learned in a term and a half is that there is no version of this where the next sentence helps, and there was nothing on the wall and nothing in the tool roll and nothing in the box of washers on the shelf where a grey card stood in front of the whole room, and about eleven people a week were still going to be taught that a release waits for a man in a lanyard, and about eleven thousand premises were still paying a levy on a fund that had bought one line and was going to buy a second.

He thought about a girl of seventeen on a piece of paper on a tailboard in Nine Bridges, who had told everybody at school for two years that her father was the district man, and who on Monday had acquired, from four adults who were all being reasonable, a sentence that was not true: that the district pulled out.

And the sentence could not be taken back, because there was no meeting at which to take it back, no letter to issue, no form to complete, no committee until the week of week thirty-seven, and the four people in this city who could prove in about nine words that nobody pulled out were four people in a corridor on a far side of the city, and one of them had a tool braid and had already said it out loud to nine men, and one of them was him.
