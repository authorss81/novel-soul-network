# Chapter 116 — A Fifth Line

The stoppage started at twenty past eight in the morning and by nine it had cost the market about four hundred pounds an hour and nobody could have told a committee what it was about.

There is a gantry on the west berth at the Saltmarket and a wet bay under it and about nine men who work pallets and ice on the Friday and the Saturday and the Sunday. On the Friday evening a man of fifty-eight called Fenn Pryce, whom the whole berth had called the other way round for thirty years, had a load on a hook in a wet bay and a man of thirty-one on the other end of a bar and no card in the room, and he put the load down in nine seconds and said *nobody is coming, I am putting it down, tell somebody*, and about nine people saw him do it, and it was correct.

He has a card. It is a piece of card with four printed steps and a fifth line, and the fifth line is in his own hand, and it says, in ballpoint, on the back of a printed thing: *If nobody comes, put it down and say so.*

On the Friday night nine men in that berth asked him for a copy and four of them wrote the fifth line out on the backs of their own cards, and one of those four wrote it badly, and by Sunday there were thirty of them in about six places in this city and nobody knew where they had come from, because that is what a fifth line does. It does not need a movement. It needs a wet bay and a man of fifty-eight who is not going to wait.

---

By nine on the Monday the office of the Saltmarket market had a notice on the board and a cardboard box by the door, and the notice was reasonable, and the notice was the single most expensive thing anybody in that building did all week.

*NOTICE. Following the district practice assurance evening session and the leaflet now in circulation, any practice card, sequence card, cue card or hand sheet held in this building that was not issued by a company or a training provider is to be placed in this box. Receipt given. Cards will be returned. — Market office.*

The market clerk was thirty-one and called Fenna Rudd and she had a clipboard and a rubber band and about nine leaflets on the counter, and the leaflets had come round with the quarterly levy note, and she had put one on the board with four drawing pins because nine people had come in and asked her what it was.

"**I have read it,**" she said, when Marek Senn came in at ten past nine on the Monday morning of week thirty-four, "all of it, twice, and I have rung the number on it, and the number is a fund and the fund is honest, and the fund does not know anything about a box in my office."

"Who told you to do the box."

"**Nobody. I did the box.**"

She turned the clipboard round.

"**There were four men in here before nine and every one of them was frightened, and not one of them was frightened of a person. They were frightened of a piece of card, and there are nine of these leaflets in this city in about four thousand pockets, and seventy people went to a session on Friday night that was advertised on them, and half of that room does lifts.**"

---

The box filled by about ten.

**Eleven cards. Eleven names on eleven cards, in eleven hands, and a sheet of A4 with a date against each one and where it came from and whether it had a fifth line, and Fenna Rudd wrote the sheet in four minutes with a rubber band on the top of the page.**

"I have not asked anybody their name," she said. "I have read eleven names off eleven cards, which is not the same as asking, and there is a difference, and I would like somebody to be able to check that I know the difference."

"May I see it."

"**No.**"

That was that, and she gave the reason without being pushed, which she had clearly prepared on the way to work.

"**Because you are a technician with a lanyard and I have had a leaflet on my board since Monday morning telling me that unofficial practices and the people who use them are the danger, and there is a job on that leaflet, and your job is on it, and if I show a sheet of eleven names to a man with a lanyard on then in about four years there is a document that says a man with a lanyard was given the names of eleven people at this market, and that document is about eleven people and not about the lanyard.**"

That was correct, and Marek Senn conceded it out loud, and it was the seventh time in a fortnight that a stranger had refused him something in nine seconds and been right about both halves.

---

Then she read the fifth line out loud, which she did because she had to, and it took a minute, and it is worth doing properly because it is the whole of the chapter and the whole of this week.

Nine of the eleven had a fifth line.

*If nobody comes, put it down and say so.*

*If I am not there, do it and tell me after.*

*Ask first. If you cannot ask, do not.*

*Wait ten. If nobody has said anything, put it down.*

*The release is mine. I am the one holding the bar.*

*If the word does not come I am not holding a load, I am holding a piece of card.*

*Ring the office and if the office is shut put it down.*

*Nine. Not eleven. Nine.*

*If I do not come back, do it anyway. It is only a pallet.*

Marek Senn stood in a market office in the Saltmarket and read nine fifth lines in nine different hands on the back of nine cards, and eight of them were true, and every one of them was written by the person whose name was on the front, and the ninth belonged to Fenn Pryce and had been copied out four times on the Friday night, and two of the four had been copied with the same pen.

"There," said Fenna Rudd. "That is what the box is for. That is the danger on the leaflet, that is the bit where somebody is being told they do not need anybody, and now they are all in one room on my counter with their names on them."

---

And here is the thing that nobody in that room could get past, and Marek Senn got it at about eleven and could not get anybody else to.

**Two of the eleven are copies of the same sheet with the same fifth line in the same hand, in the same pen, on paper that is not the same paper as the other nine, and nobody in this city can say whose box they came out of.** Eight of the nine fifth lines were written by their owners and cannot be traced to anybody. Two came off a sheet. And the shape of a fifth line does not tell you which is which, and there is no question you can ask a fifth line that will answer it.

"That is the whole of my trade," said Fenna Rudd. "I sell fish. I can tell you whether a crate came off a boat or off a van and I cannot tell you who cut a label off a bottle."

"No you cannot."

"**So what am I supposed to do with a box of eleven cards and a sheet with eleven names on it.**"

---

The district's answer came at about two in the afternoon and it was a letter, and the letter was correct, and it is the whole of this week's cost in ninety words.

*Thank you for your assistance with the district practice assurance leaflet distribution. Please continue to hold any cards collected. The matter has been referred to district practice assurance and will be addressed through the committee's scheduled review. There is no requirement at this stage for a record of individuals holding unofficial materials. We are grateful for your cooperation.*

"Somebody wrote that," said Fenna Rudd, "and they have written nine words in the wrong place, and the nine words are *no requirement at this stage for a record of individuals*, and I have got a sheet with eleven names on it and I made it myself, in four minutes, off eleven cards, and I am the requirement, and the stage is this stage, and the individuals are in my hand."

"Can you put it in a drawer."

"**I have put it in a drawer, and the drawer is in a market office and the market office is open from Monday to Friday from about four in the morning, and there are nine people in this building between now and half past five, and any one of them can open a drawer.**"

---

Two things happened in that building that were not about the box.

The first was at half past nine, before the box was even full, and it was the foreman of the market, a man of forty-five called **Harlan Vessey**, standing in the middle of bay two with a leaflet in one hand, telling nine men that they were not to work a lift of over about two hundred kilos on their own card until the district had said something, and that this was for their own protection, and that he had no authority to stop anybody and was not using it.

Nobody worked a lift on their own card for two hours, and eleven men lost about nine hundred pounds between them, and in about four weeks the man who lost most of it would be asked to explain the gap on a timesheet, and he would not be able to say *a leaflet*.

**He said the right thing. That is what made it expensive: he said the right thing, and the right thing was that nobody should be the fourth one on a card that says a stranger handed round in a car park, and he was right, and there is no version of this where he was wrong, and the fifth line is the reason nine men stood in a wet bay for two hours doing nothing.**

The second was at eleven, when Harlan Vessey asked Fenn Pryce who had told him to put the load down on the Friday.

"**Your card did.**"

"**Whose card.**"

"**Mine. It is my card. It is a card I made out of a company one and I have had it about four years and the fifth line is mine and my wife wrote the words for me because I cannot spell.**"

"**Right,**" said Harlan Vessey.

And that is the whole of it, and it took nine seconds, and there is a foreman in a wet bay in this city who went back to work that morning with a question and got an answer that is true, and the answer does not help, because a man asked for a name and the name was the man who asked.

---

At half past eleven Marek Senn was in the office and Fenna Rudd had a woman of forty-four in there with her, agency, nine years, who had worked the west berth on Fridays for nine years and who had put a card in the box at twenty past nine without being asked to.

"**Are you here about my card,**" she said.

"Yes."

"**Then is it the dangerous one,**" said the woman of forty-four. "Because it is the one with the most writing on it, and I have got a lot of writing on it, and I have never once been asked to show anybody, and if that is the one then I have nine years and a Tuesday and a bus and I would like to know now."

Marek Senn looked at the card on the counter, and it had four printed steps and a fifth line in a hand that got smaller towards the end, and it said: *If the word does not come I am not holding a load, I am holding a piece of card.*

**It is true. He could not say that. He could say nine words about it and all nine would be a mechanism, and a mechanism given to a woman of forty-four in a market office at half past eleven in the morning on a Monday is a man performing a thing, and there is a nineteen-year-old in this city who worked out in week twenty-eight that the man who explains things in rooms is the man who stops the room, and she was right, and it took her five weeks on her own and I let her.**

"No," he said. "It is not the dangerous one. I cannot tell you which of the eleven is and I am not going to try, because the only way I would know is by telling you four words on a laminated sheet behind a gate on the Meridian corridor, and I am not going to name a gate, and that is the seventh time in a fortnight and I have stopped apologising for it."

"Then what do I do."

"**You keep working.**"

"**That is what I did on Friday and it is what put me in your box.**"

"Yes," said Marek Senn. "That is the difficulty and I am not going to improve on it."

---

He left at ten past twelve and the stoppage had lifted and the bay was working again and nobody had died and nothing had been injured and about eleven men had lost a morning.

On the wall by the office door there was a leaflet with four drawing pins in it, and the first line was the specification's line, and there were eleven session titles, and *fee paid*, and a name on it: **the Quiet House**.

A man of thirty-one at the ice machine had been reading it for four minutes and said, to nobody in particular, as Marek Senn came past:

"**Paid. There is a fee on it. Somebody is paying somebody.**"

"**That is the fund's fee for the session on Friday,**" said Marek Senn.

"Is it."

"It is."

The man of thirty-one looked at him for four seconds and then went back to the ice.

**Marek Senn stood in a doorway in the Saltmarket for nine seconds and let that stand, because the alternative was a speech in a wet market about a woman with a notebook at a repair shop in Lattice Ward, and because a man of thirty-one with a fee on a leaflet is not going to be argued out of a fee on a leaflet by a man with a lanyard, and because the leaflet is true about a group of nine people who do say leave, and it is true that somebody has tidied up about two hundred names onto a letterbox page, and the tidying is not theirs and they have never said it was, and he knew all three of those things and said none of them, and the reason he gave in his own book later that night was that the three of them together are nine minutes and a market office at half past eleven on a Monday is not the place, and that this was the fourth time this term he had used that sentence and the first time he believed it.**

I have said that to a man in a yard, to a foreman in a corridor, to a woman of thirty-one in Bower Street and to a woman of forty-four in a market this fortnight, and I have said it because it is what is true and because it is what a technician can say, and it is four words with no cost in them at all, and I cannot think of a single practice that would make those four words cost something.**
