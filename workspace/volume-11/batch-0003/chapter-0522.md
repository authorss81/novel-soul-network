# Chapter 522 — A Man With Nine Flats And A Notice In His Coat, And A Chair That Is Not His

**The Wednesday was a bench in Lattice Ward, a counter, and a trestle table in a shop front where somebody said one thing out loud for about nine minutes, and the shape of it was a man from a block of four storeys who heard about a room in a corridor and came to find out whether nine flats belonged in it, and was told what the room is, and was not on it.**

---

Eleven booked for the Wednesday, eleven of them with a date against their name, and eleven turned up, the last at about ten past six. The bench ran to two, the counter from two to six, and the four rooms behind the service road from half past six. The rate is nine and ten, wrong, and stayed where it is. **Heat in the tenement over the road is on and has gone up again, four people on that lease are each paying more than they were, and the fortnight one cook covered out of her own wages is still sitting with nobody.** Behind the service road the room stands at eight hundred and ninety-one days, one hundred and twenty-seven weeks and two days, warm throughout, four rooms and one behind.

The card in the rail by the counter is eight hundred and ninety-five days old, which is one hundred and twenty-seven weeks and six days, four days older than the walls around it, and the rail takes two. The trestle table in the shop front is neither of those, and the coat with a sheet in its inside pocket is a third thing again.

---

The corridor of that block of four storeys has a bulb out of every third fitting and has had since before anybody moved into the flats above the shop.

The man of about thirty-eight was carrying a plastic sleeve in the inside pocket of his coat and had been carrying it since the spring, and the sleeve has a printed sheet in it of nine lines with a box at the foot printed for a signature. He keeps the rota for those flats and keeps a key to a meter cupboard on the second landing, and the man of about forty-four who keeps the stair, the bins, the bulbs and the notice board has never once asked to see the sleeve and would not understand it if he did.

Two men were working on a landing at the end of that corridor and one of them said, to the other, that there was a room off a wide road with nine chairs in it and that it was for people who had been asked to do something and had not known what they were agreeing to.

**"Is that the thing with a name on it."**

**"No, it hasn't got a name. I heard it hasn't got a name."**

**"Everything in this city has a name by Thursday."**

---

He was at the counter at about half past three with the sleeve not out and then with it out, and the man behind the counter read a counter and did not look at the sleeve for about four seconds, which the man of about thirty-eight noticed and did not mention afterwards to anybody.

**"Are you the one who wrote that address."**

**"I wrote the address."**

**"Then can you tell me whether my block should be in it. Nine flats, four storeys, and I've a notice with nine lines on it and a box at the bottom for a signature."**

**"I am not the person who may answer that, and I am not going to answer a part of it, because a part is how it starts. There is an office and it is above a line on a green stair and I will give you the address and you will go up it yourself, and the four lines are also on the door of the room in your own hand and not mine, and if you read those four lines to your nine flats you will be quoting me and not her."**

He wrote the address on a job ticket and did not write anything else on it, and the man of about thirty-eight took it and put it in the same pocket as the sleeve, and the two pieces of paper in that pocket were for two entirely different buildings and neither of them knew about the other.

**"You could have come up the stair with me and said it once."**

**"If I come up with you, then in about a fortnight there will be a man at that door who says the man at the counter said it, and in about a month there will be two, and none of them will be able to take anything back."**

---

The office on the green stair has a table, one chair on the visitor's side, a window that opens about four inches, and no sign on the door.

She read his nine lines from the plastic sleeve without taking the sleeve, and put them back the way they were, and then she did the thing that ended it.

**"You are the person asking. This room is for the person who did not understand. Those are not the same chair, and no version of this room puts you in hers."**

**"But I could bring one of them. I've nine flats and there'll be one, there's always one, they don't read it and they sign it because there's a man from the landlord's office sat in the room and it's quicker."**

**"And when that person comes here, what will they have brought."**

**"A signature."**

**"That is the thing they did, not the thing they did not understand. If you want the second one you have to ask them for it, in words, in your own stair, and there is no paper in this city that will ask them for you."**

**"There is. It's the sheet in my coat."**

**"Then that is the piece of your month I can help you with and I am going to do it, so listen, because it costs you something and you should have it in writing of the mouth."**

She turned the chair about so that it faced him and she did not sit down in it.

**"Nine people, one sheet, one box at the bottom. That is not nine answers. That is one answer written nine times, and the second person only signs because the first one did, and by the ninth you have a sheet that tells you a thing that nine people never said. A sheet like that is worth nothing at the end of the month and it is worse than nothing, because it looks like an answer and it is a copy."**

---

He sat on the visitor's chair and did not say anything for about nine seconds.

**"I have a sheet with a box on it and I cannot get my name taken off it. It came back in the spring after I asked for it to come back. And they have me down for an inspection at the end of this month and I am going to be asked whether that block is in order."**

**"And whether you can say it is."**

**"I can say the stair is in order and the bulbs work and the bins go out. I cannot say nine flats are in order because I have not been in nine flats in a fortnight and I do not know what goes on in them, and that has been the case for about a year and nobody has ever asked me about it."**

**"Then you will be asked a question you cannot answer and this office does not hold the answer, and I am not going to pretend to you that it does, and I would rather say that to you today than let you walk down those stairs thinking there is a room in this city that will have you."**

**"There isn't."**

**"There is a room with nine chairs in it and one line on a sheet, and what is on that sheet is not a remedy and it is not a building and it is not a hearing. If you were hoping for one of those three things, and I know you were, then I have just cost you about four days of hoping and I would do it again."**

**"I know what it is,"** he said, and put the sleeve back in the inside pocket, and buttoned it, and the buttoning was the end of the conversation.

---

**He did not offer to go up and down that block with him and write nine lines in his own words for the board, and he has not written them on a second occasion either, and the rota man went alone.** The cost of that decision is not theoretical: on the Friday the rota man came past the counter and said that he had spoken to four of the nine and that two of the four had said the sheet was fine and one had said it was fine in a voice that meant the opposite, and that he had said nothing at all to the other five, and Marek asked him the one question that would have helped, which was what the fifth said, and the rota man said that he had not got to the fifth, and the fifth had left a note about the bins.

That is twice now that nine lines have gone unwritten, and the second time it was not restraint on a stair, it was a Wednesday afternoon he wanted, and the wanting is the thing he is not proud of.

---

On the stairs of that block at about ten past four there was a woman of about fifty-three sitting with a folded paper flat on her knee and a handbag on the step below, and the man of about thirty-eight went past her twice.

On the way out he said, without stopping, that he wished he knew what the box at the bottom of a sheet was for.

She said that it was for the man who wanted it filled in.

**"That is not what a box at the bottom of a sheet is for."**

**"No,"** she said.
**"It is what it is used for."**

He did not stop again and he did not learn her name and he thought about it for about a week, which is longer than he thought about most of the things that happened that week.

---

The man of about fifty-one was against the north wall with his bag down beside him and had gone four hundred and ninety-seven days without speaking, which is seventy-one weeks to the day. The man of about thirty-three who holds four buildings and about nine hundred doors is five hundred and twenty-four days into it, seventy-four weeks and six days, in another city, with no stamp on it and nobody's name attached to it and no question asked of him since a spring neither of them will name.

The ask is five hundred and eighty-one days, which is eighty-three weeks to the day, and is counted from the day it was asked and not from anything said since. The newest of the nineteen lines on two nails is five hundred and eighty-seven days, which is eighty-three weeks and six days, and still the only one of the nineteen that has ever carried a zero. The post at the corridor end is four hundred and thirty-nine days old, sixty-two weeks and five days. The nine copied fronts of a page are four hundred and fifty-seven days along, which is sixty-five weeks and two days, and what is between him and the office in another district is two hundred and seventy-one days, which is thirty-eight weeks and five days, and neither of those two numbers moved while he was on a green stair.

Nobody thanked anybody. The shutter came down at about ten.

*Nine flats and one box, as a record of an afternoon on a green stair.* **Where: a second-floor office above a line, reached by a stair with a green handrail, holding a table, one visitor's chair, a window that opens four inches and no sign on the door. Who came: a man of about thirty-eight keeping the rota for four storeys and nine flats, carrying a plastic sleeve holding a printed sheet of nine lines with a signature box printed at the foot, and he had been carrying that sleeve since the spring. How he came to it: two men on a landing in his corridor had said that a shop front off a wide road has nine chairs in it and is for people asked to do something without knowing what they were agreeing to, and that as of the previous day it still had no name, and his reply was that everything in this city has a name by Thursday. What the man at the counter did: confirmed he wrote the address, refused to answer the question or any part of it, gave the office address on a job ticket with nothing else written on it, said the four lines are on the room's door in her handwriting and not his, and gave the reason he would not walk up with him, which is that a man at a counter who accompanies becomes a man at a counter who is quoted, and after about a fortnight there would be two of them and neither could take it back. What she read and did not take: his nine lines, out of the sleeve, and back into it. What ended the conversation: that he is the person asking and the room is for the person who did not understand, and that no arrangement of the room puts him in her chair. What she refused and how: that he bring one of the nine, on the grounds that a signature is the thing a person did and not the thing they did not understand, and that obtaining the second one requires asking in words on his own stair, and that no paper in this city will ask on his behalf. What she gave instead, and named the price of: that nine people and one sheet and one box is one answer written nine times, that the second signs because the first did, and that such a sheet is worth nothing at the end of a month and worse than nothing because it resembles an answer and is a copy. What he named: the sheet he cannot get his own name removed from, its return in the spring, an inspection due at the end of the month, and the fact that he has not been inside nine flats in a fortnight and cannot testify about them. What she refused to do: pretend the office held the answer, and she said she would rather tell him that on the day than let him leave believing a room in this city wanted him. What she said it had cost him: about four days of hoping. Owner: a plastic sleeve, a fourth landing, and a button done up.**

*Two windows dressed in a town, and a woman on a stair.* **Where: a block of four storeys in this city, one bulb out of three in the corridor, and a kitchen in a town four days of bus travel away. The first window: a flat on the second landing where a man of about fifty-three keeps a table for two and a list in a hand nobody has read, and a note pinned above it, and the note has been repinned eleven times. The second window: a flat above a launderette in the same town where two people sleep in shifts because one of them washes up and the other does not and neither can be there when the other is. What each of them did on that Wednesday: nothing, and neither of them wrote to anybody, and the launderette's owner does not know about the note and the man with the list has never read the note and neither of them is on the nine lines and neither of them has been asked. What they cost: in the first window, about four pounds of heat a fortnight that is not in anybody's bill, and in the second, a night each way every third day for about two years, which is a year of somebody's life and is not a figure anybody has ever written down. Owner: two kitchens, one note, and a bus four days each way.**
