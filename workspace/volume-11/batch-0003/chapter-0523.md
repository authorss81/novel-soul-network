# Chapter 523 — A Name Given On A Corner By A Woman With Figures On Her Arm, And A Box By A Meter

**The Thursday carried a bench, a counter, nine chairs and a name that four streets had by the middle of the afternoon, and the shape of the day was a paragraph on the back of a sheet being quoted correctly in a laundry stairwell by people with nowhere to put an objection to it, and one woman deciding on the strength of that to go and look.**

---

Eleven entered on the sheet for the Thursday and eleven in the date column, and eleven were through the door, the final one at about five in the afternoon. Bench to two, counter to six, the service-road rooms at half past six. The charge remains nine and ten, which is wrong, and which was mentioned to nobody at all. **The heating on that stretch is running and costs a family more than it did at the turn of the year, and four names carry it between them on one tenancy, and the cook's fortnight, put out of her own money in the winter, is still on nobody's list.** The rooms off the service road number eight hundred and ninety-two days, one hundred and twenty-seven weeks and three days, and are warm, and are four rooms and a further one behind them.

The card standing in the rail by the counter has been there eight hundred and ninety-six days, which is one hundred and twenty-eight weeks to the day, four days beyond the room, and the rail is a rail of two.

---

It got the name on a corner at about eleven in the morning, between a lock-up shop and a place that sells buckets, from a woman of about thirty-one who reads meters for a firm that has an office she has never been inside.

She has forty-four figures written down the inside of one forearm in a hand small enough to be read at arm's length by nobody but herself, and they are readings, and they are kept in a column, and the column is the reason she can tell a landlord's meter from a company's without looking at the number plate.

Two men were arguing on that corner about whether a room with nine chairs in it was a clinic, and one of them said it has no name, and she said it has.

**"It hasn't."**

**"It has. It's the room you said yes in."**

**"That's not a name."**

**"It is on a street. You've had it for about four days and you've not called it anything, and if you don't, then in a month it'll be the room, and the room is a different thing, and the room is where you go when you already know."**

---

By the middle of the afternoon it had shortened. What went round the four streets near the shop front was the yes room, said the way a thing is said when people have decided it is funny and slightly shameful and are also going to use it about nine times a week.

Nobody who said it had been inside. Nobody who said it knew what was on the four lines of the sheet on the door. Two people said it as a joke about a woman they had never met, and one person said it as though it were a door in a hospital, and none of those three people were wrong about the door, and all three would have been wrong about what was behind it.

At the counter that afternoon a man of about twenty-six asked Marek whether it was true, and he said that he did not know, in his own shop, on his own counter, and it was the second time in a week he had said it and the first time he had said it about something he had been asked about four times.

**He did not write the name on the wall list, and that was the cost of the Monday.** The wall list is the one piece of paper in this district that about eleven people a week read, and a name on it would have taken four days to get into every shop in four streets, and he has wanted for two years to be the person who is asked, and he stood there with a pencil and did not do it, and what he gave up was the only thing anybody offered him that week and it was his to take and it cost him nothing to take and that is the part he has not got used to.

---

The four hundred competent people were in the back stairwell of a laundry at about two in the afternoon, and they were four of them and they had all done the same thing without arranging it.

The paragraph is printed on the back of a sheet of paper. It is about the length of a receipt. Everybody who has ever been asked to agree to anything in this city has been handed a copy of the front of that sheet at some point, and nobody in the four districts has ever seen the front.

**"It says a person may withdraw at any time and that withdrawal is free and does not require a reason and does not affect any other part of the arrangement."**

That was a woman of about thirty-seven who ran the flat machines and had said it about four hundred times, and it was correct, and it is the reason a great many people in this city sleep.

**"And if I want to object to the paragraph itself."**

That was a man of about twenty-six on a roof and he had asked it to the stairwell and not to anybody in particular.

**"Which paragraph."**

**"The one on the back. I can quote it. I cannot write to the person who wrote it, because there is no person, and I cannot put an objection anywhere, because there is nowhere."**

**"No,"** the woman of thirty-seven said.
**"There isn't."**

**"So it's true and it can't be disagreed with."**

**"It can be disagreed with by everybody. It just doesn't have a place. That's a different thing and it took me about nine years and a laundry to work out which one it was."**

A woman of about fifty-one in a school office said that she had put a question to a man in a lanyard in a company building about a default setting and had been told it was a technical matter, and that this had been in the spring, and that the paragraph and the default were in different documents and that she was aware they were in different documents.

**"Then they're two problems."**

**"They're one problem with a nicer name on one half of it."**

Nobody wrote any of it down. It is the one thing in that stairwell that has been true for about nine years and it is not in the paragraph, and the paragraph would not be improved by it.

---

The woman of thirty-one went at about four on the Thursday, and she went because of the stairwell, and she had not been in a room in this city for anything except reading a meter since a spring she does not discuss at work.

She is not the woman who sits in the chair nearest the door in a warm room four rooms along. The two have never been in a building together, and the woman with the column on her arm has no idea that the other one exists.

What she brought was a small grey box by her meter on the second landing, and the box was fitted in about four minutes by a man with a drill who was very polite, and what she had been asked to do was let him in and sign a piece of paper, and what she had not understood was what the box was.

**"It reads the meter,"** the woman of twenty-four said, before the woman with the column on her arm had got to the end of it.
**"It does not talk to anything yet. That is a fact and not a reassurance, and I am not going to tell you it is a good thing or a bad thing, because I do not know, and the person who fitted it is not in this room."**

**"Is it one of theirs."**

**"It is a box. Whether it is theirs is not a thing I can tell you from a chair."**

**"Then what is this room."**

**"It is a room where a person may bring something they were asked to do and did not understand what they were agreeing to. What you have brought is exactly that and you were right to bring it and I am going to write it down in about nine seconds in my own hand and then I am going to hand it to the chair of the committee, and the chair reads it out at a sitting, and that is all the route there is."**

**"And then I will know."**

**"And then you will know that it was said."**

**"That is not the same as knowing."**

**"No. It is not, and I am not able to improve it, and anybody who tells you in this city that a thing written down has been dealt with is selling you the part of it that is easy."**

---

She wrote it in about nine seconds and turned it round and pushed it across a trestle table, and the woman with the column on her arm read it, and it said what she had said and not what she had meant, and she made the woman of twenty-four change one word, and the word was changed because she was right about the word and not about anything else.

Then she said the thing that she had come four days' worth of thinking to say and it was not about the box.

**"There is nowhere to put an objection either. You have just given me somewhere to put this one, for about four days, and then it is read out in a room I am not in by a woman I have not met, to about nine people, and I will never know which of them heard it, and none of the four hundred people who can all say the paragraph can be told they were wrong by any of them."**

**"That is a different room and it has not been built."**

**"No,"** she said.
**"It has not. And I would like it on the record that I know that, and I would like it said by somebody who is not me, because in about a fortnight this will be a story about a woman with a column on her arm who wanted a special room, and the box by my meter will be gone and the story will be here."**

He said that he would say it, and did not write it down, and that was the second time in four days he had been asked to be the person who gets asked and had answered the same way, and he has begun to think of those two refusals as a shape rather than as two.

---

The woman of about fifty-three was at the back of that room for about forty minutes and said nothing in it, and afterwards, in the street, a man of about twenty-one asked her whether she was a person who worked there, and she said that she was not, and he said sorry, and she said it was all right, and neither of them learned anything.

The man of about fifty-one at the north wall had been silent four hundred and ninety-eight days, seventy-one weeks and one day. The man of about thirty-three with four buildings and about nine hundred doors is at five hundred and twenty-five days, seventy-five weeks to the day, and this is the first figure in this paragraph that is a whole number of weeks and it was reached without anything having happened to cause it.

The ask is five hundred and eighty-two days, eighty-three weeks and one day. The nineteen lines on two nails run from five hundred and eighty-eight days at the newest, which is eighty-four weeks to the day, and it carries no zero. The post at the end of the corridor is four hundred and forty days, sixty-two weeks and six days. Nine copied page fronts are four hundred and fifty-eight days into the run, which is sixty-five weeks and three days, and the two hundred and seventy-two days between him and her are thirty-eight weeks and six days, and a name that goes round four streets does not move either figure by a day.

Nobody thanked anybody. The shutter came down at about ten.
