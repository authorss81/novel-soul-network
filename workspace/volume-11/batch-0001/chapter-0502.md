# Chapter 502 — Four Seconds, And A Man Who Says The Cost Out Loud

**The Friday was a bench, then a counter, and then a room with a man in a lanyard standing at the front of it, and the shape of the day was one conversation that lasted less time than either man wanted it to and one sheet of paper that got torn in half at the end of it for a reason that had nothing to do with the paper.**

---

Eleven on the docket and eleven in date; eleven attended, the last of them about six in the evening, and his day was a bench in Lattice Ward until about two, the counter until about five, and a room with a shuttered window and a man in a lanyard from about half past five until he was let out. The rate was at nine and ten, and he read that line last, standing up, with a cable in his hand. **The heating was on, dearer than it had been in the winter, four names on one lease went with it, and the fortnight a cook laid out was still on nobody's account.** The room off that service road is eight hundred and forty-four days old and is warm, and on the Friday night five people came in for the temperature and none of them was asked anything.

The card in that rail is eight hundred and forty-eight days old, four days more than the room, and the rail holds two and has never held three. A question in grease pencil on the back of a working man's hand is a fifth object in this entry and is on nothing at all, and nothing in this entry joins it to any of the other four.

---

The man came in at about ten past two, which is before the queue, and he had a jacket on for a job indoors and his hands were cold, which meant he had come from outside and had not been inside long enough to warm them.

He was about forty-four and he cleaned and checked buildings in the second of the four districts, and he had written a question on the back of his left hand in grease pencil, and Marek could see three lines of it upside down from the middle of the shop.

**"Are you the one who's with the new woman?"**

**"No."**

**"Right."** The man took the cap off and put it back on.

**"Right, so — there is a woman on the second floor of my building, she's seventy-one, and on Wednesday a lad from the company came round with a form about a relay and a default setting, and she signed it because he said it was for the fire panel and she said she'd heard that before."**

**"And you want to know whether that is one of the things she does."**

**"I want to know whether she's a person she can go to about it, and I've been standing here working out how to ask that for about ten minutes and then I wrote it on my hand, which is not the same as knowing how to ask it."**

Marek looked at the hand again. Two of the three lines were not about consent at all. They were a company's name and a postal district, and the second one had a box drawn round it with a number in it, and he understood in the space of about four seconds that a man had come to a repair counter with a practical question wearing the coat of a different question, and that the coat was the reason he had come and the question was the reason he had stayed.

**"I can't tell you what she does,"** Marek said.

**"I wasn't in the room and I wasn't asked and I don't have a copy of anything, and if I stood here and guessed at it and you went up there on my word, you'd have taken my word for hers. That's not a small thing to hand a person."**

**"Aye."**

**"There's a Friday afternoon slot. She wrote it on the back of a ticket."**

**"Right."**

**"That's all I've got. That's not enough and I'm not going to stand here and dress it up."**

The man turned his hand over and looked at the grease pencil as though it had been unhelpful, which it had.

**"Right, that's the honest answer, and I've had a lot of not-honest ones this month."** He rubbed a thumb across the three lines.

**"Here's what it costs me, since you asked and since you didn't.** I finish here at four and I'm on the depot at seven and I'm on again at four in the morning. If I go up to her on Friday afternoon I lose the Saturday, and the Saturday's the one I've been putting the daughter's on, and that's a day's pay off. And if I don't go up to her Friday, then it's next Friday, and next Friday is when the company's lad comes back with a different form."
**"Here's what it costs me, since you asked and since you didn't.** I finish here at four and I'm on the depot at seven and I'm on again at four in the morning. If I go up to her on Friday afternoon I lose the Saturday, and the Saturday's the one I've been putting the daughter's on, and that's a day's pay off. And if I don't go up to her Friday, then it's next Friday, and next Friday is when the company's lad comes back with a different form."

Nobody in that shop said anything for about four seconds, and the parts cage man turned a radio down rather than off.

**"I could write your question down,"** Marek said.

**"Properly. On a ticket, with both of us on it, and it'd be on a record at my bench and you could take it up."**

**"No. Don't."** The man said it fast enough that Marek stopped.

**"I'm not having her name next to my building on a sheet in a shop. She signs things. That's the whole of it, that's why I came and not a lawyer, because a lawyer writes things down and she'll get told a lawyer's been at it."**

Marek had the ticket in his hand before the man finished the sentence. The back of it had four words on it in a hand that was not his, and a stair with a green door, and a slot on a Friday afternoon, and there was no second half of this ticket to give anybody.

He tore it down the middle, which took him a second try because the perforation had gone brittle, and gave the man the half with his own handwriting on it, which was a job number and nothing else, and kept the half with hers.

**"That's a job number for a bracket you didn't bring in."**

**"It's a piece of paper with a conversation on it,"** Marek said.

**"It'll do."**

The man put the fragment in his jacket pocket, not the one with the tools, and went out, and the parts cage man turned the radio back up.

**What Marek wanted to say, at the counter, after the man had gone, was that he could take the two practical lines and have them answered by Monday without going near any of this. He did not say it. He had a pen in his hand and a half ticket in the other one and the specific address of a company depot in his own handwriting on a scrap of paper, and nobody had asked him for any of it, and he put the pen down in the roll with the fourth pocket and did not take it out again that week.**

---

The man of about fifty-one with a tool bag took his part at about four and left without saying anything, which is what he does, and Marek looked at the wall where he had been standing for two years and worked out from the shape of the dust on the floor that the man had a rota somewhere, and that the rota had him somewhere else on Monday, and that nobody in that building had ever asked him to say so out loud.

He decided not to say it. That was the whole of the decision and it took about two seconds and it cost him nothing that anybody could price, and he has never been able to work out why he still counts it.

The grease pencil stayed on the man's hand all afternoon. Marek saw it four more times before six, on a forearm reaching across the counter for a change, and every time he saw it he thought about the three lines, and two of which were a company's name and a box drawn round a number, and neither of which had anything to do with consent, and both of which were the reason a man of forty-four had stood in a repair shop for ten minutes working out how to ask.

**That is the thing the man could not have got from the office on a second floor in Crown Terrace, and he got it from a counter by accident, and Marek has thought about that more than he has thought about the appointment, and has not decided yet what to do with the thinking.**

**At about five he did a thing he has done perhaps four times in two years, which is to go out into the lane at the end of a working day and stand there for about a minute as though he had forgotten something.** There was nothing to go back for. He did it anyway, and the reason is that in two years he has worked out that a man standing in a lane at the end of a day is a man who could be spoken to, and that a great many people who could not have come into a shop at ten past two on a Friday can speak to a man in a lane at five, and that the two of them are not the same man and the second one is not available on a Saturday.

Nobody came. There were nine people in the shop, a bus went past, and the shutter came down at about ten, and the lane was empty for the whole minute.

**What he wanted, and did not say to anybody including himself, was that the man of about forty-four with the grease pencil would come back. He will not come back. He said on the Friday that he would not lose a Friday, and a man who has said that about a Friday has made a decision that does not get revisited because a person in a lane makes it feel revisitable, and that is the entire difference between asking a person for something and being a person who is easy to ask.**

Nobody in that shop knew that on the Thursday a woman of twenty-four had been given a piece of paper in a room on a second floor, and nobody in that building knew that a man of forty-four had written her office down on his hand in grease pencil and had then decided not to have it written down anywhere else, and the two of them never met and were not going to meet, and one of them is on a list of about nine people and the other one is on nothing.

**He had split the only copy of an address in half at about ten past two on the Friday and kept the half that had her handwriting on it, and it is on a hook under a wall list in a plastic sleeve, and it is not a record, and there is no second copy of the stair with the green door because he has not asked her for one and is not going to.**

The man of about fifty-one with a tool bag was at the wall on the Friday afternoon and his part was finished and he did not take it and was not asked to.

The woman of about thirty in the chair nearest the door was in that room on the Friday night and was not asked a question, and her page is the eighth of eight things in a ring binder on a shelf and was not read.

A man of about thirty-three who holds four buildings and about nine hundred doors with no stamp, no reference and no date was not in this city on the Friday and his hold ran on for sixty-eight weeks and one day without anything being asked of him and without anything being given to him.

Nobody thanked anybody. The shutter came down at about ten.
