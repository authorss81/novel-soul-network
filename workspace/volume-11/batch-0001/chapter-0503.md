# Chapter 503 — Two Numbers In One Kitchen, And A Step Neither Of Them Sat On

**The Monday was a bench, a counter, and a room, and the shape of the day was a shop left alone for a while with a sheet of paper that had gone off a bench with no explanation, and a bus that takes most of a day going out and coming back with one sentence in it that nobody acted on.**

---

Eleven on the docket and eleven in date; eleven attended, the last of them about six in the evening, and his day was a bench in Lattice Ward until about two and the counter until about six and a room from about half past six, and none of those three places is in any of the four towns and none of them has a bus to it. The rate was at nine and ten, and he got to it after the rest of the sheet, which is the order he reads that sheet in. **The heating was on and the four names on that lease were still paying for it, and a fortnight of one cook's own laying out is still owed to nobody and is not going to be settled on a Monday.** The room off that service road is eight hundred and forty-seven days old and is warm, and seven people came in on the Monday evening for the temperature and none of them was asked anything.

The card in that rail is eight hundred and fifty-one days old, four days more than the room is, and the rail holds two. A rent book in a shop in the first of the four towns and a chopping board in a kitchen in the second are two more objects, and a bus is a fifth, and nothing in this entry puts any two of the five together.

---

The sheet had been on that bench for five weeks. The first column was printed and the second had a line ruled in it in pencil and nothing whatever above that line, and it had gone.

Marek noticed it at about eleven, between a kettle job and a bracket he did not have parts for, and he stood there with the bracket in one hand for longer than the bracket required. The cage man had his back to the room and had had it there since a quarter past seven. The man at the wall had come in at about nine with a tool bag and had not sat down and had not spoken, and the window onto the lane was the only way in or out, and the man at the wall had gone out through it at about ten.

**He wanted to ask the cage man. He had wanted to ask the cage man about something else twice this year and had not, and the reason was not politeness and was that a man who works with his back to a room all day has arranged his working life around not being asked, and walking up behind it at eleven on a Monday is a way of saying that arrangement is finished for him.**

He put the bracket down. He did not ask.

What he did instead was take the twenty minutes he had been going to spend on it and give them to a woman of about thirty-four who had brought in a lamp with a switch that worked and a cord that did not, and he did that job for nothing, and the switch came off in his hand at about four and the seam of his right glove went with it, and he worked the rest of the afternoon through the split with two fingers of it bare and did not stop to sew it, and that is what the twenty minutes bought and what it did not.

---

The bus went at about seven in the morning from a stop on a road that does not go anywhere anybody goes on purpose and came back at about nine in the evening, and the man who rode it out was about thirty-five and kept the only written record in the first of the four towns, and he had a plastic bag with a rent book in it that he had not opened since the shop shut.

He had written the nine lines out by hand in about nine minutes and the fourth one says that a room which has not sent anything in nine days has not said no. He had never read the fourth line out loud to anybody, and there are three other copies of nine lines in three other towns and he has never been told that they exist.

The kitchen was warm and had four tables in it and half of them were full, and the woman of about forty-three was at the pass with her sleeves up, and she did not stop what she was doing when he came in, and he did not expect her to.

He put the plastic bag on the counter and did not take the rent book out of it, and after a while she came over and looked at the bag and not at him.

**"You're the one with the shop."**

**"I'm the one with the shop."**

**"You've come on the bus."**

**"I've come on the bus."**

**"Well, sit down if you're sitting down."** She wiped the counter.

**"Go on. Say the line."**

**"Nine."**

**"Four."**

He had a whole afternoon's worth of the sentence ready and it went out of him somewhere between the four and the look she gave him.

**"That's not the same number."**

**"No."**

**"Which one's the right one?"**

**"Now look at me."** She put the cloth down.

**"I've got a number in my kitchen that I copied onto a board off the side in about nine minutes about a month ago, and you have got a number in a bag, and neither of us wrote the thing we copied, and neither of us can go back and ask who did. If you sit down on that step you're going to tell me yours is correct because it's the one in front of you, and I'd be doing the same with mine, and then in a month we'd both be telling people that the other one is wrong."**

**"I'm not comparing them,"** he said.

**"I want to be clear that I have not come here to compare them."**

**"Good. Neither have I."** She picked the cloth up again.

**"What I have been doing for about three weeks is waiting for somebody to stand in that doorway so that I could hand somebody four and be finished with it. You've made it a great deal worse and I'm glad you came, and I want both of those true at once and I expect you to be able to hold them."**

**"I can hold them."**

**"Then say what it cost you, because you've had a bus each way and this is a Monday and your shop's shut."**

**"A day,"** he said.

**"And the day isn't paid and there's nobody to claim it off, and I'd do it again next Thursday, and I want you to know that I know exactly what I'm doing."**

She wrote four on the corner of a receipt pad and turned the pad round and pushed it across the counter to him, and he looked at it and did not take it, and she took it back and put it in the bin, and neither of them said that that had happened.

He was back on the bus at about half past six. He did not open the rent book. He has never opened the fourth line of it in front of anybody, and the woman of about forty-three has not read the fourth line of a chopping board out loud to anybody since, and the two of them know a number about each other and there is nothing written down anywhere with both numbers on it.

---

The lamp woman came back at about two to ask whether the switch would hold. It would, and he told her so, and she asked him what he would charge and he said nothing, and she said that was a stupid way to run a shop, and he said he knew, and she left, and he was aware that he had spent the twenty minutes he was not spending on the missing sheet and had got a switch out of it, which is the exchange rate he has always got and has never been able to explain to a person who is not him.

At about half past three he read the wall list, which he does not need to do, and under it the job ticket with four words on the back of it in a hand that was not his, and he stood and looked at the plastic sleeve and worked out the number of people who would see it in a week.

The number was not about four. The number was every person who came to that counter with something, which is most of them, and the sleeve is at eye height, and the handwriting is small, and there is nothing on the sleeve that says the paper is not for reading.

**He did not move it. That is the decision, and it was made in about eleven seconds, and the reason it is worth writing down is that the reason was not tidiness. The reason was that a man of twenty-two who has spent ten years being the person people are told things by would have taken it down to protect a woman from a consequence of his own making, and that is the same man in a different coat, and the coat was the whole of the difference.**

He put the ticket back in the sleeve and finished the day. The sheet of paper with the pencil-ruled box is still not on the bench and is not on the floor and is not in the bin, and it went where somebody's hand took it at about ten on the Monday morning.

**The woman whose lamp he fixed came back on the Wednesday and put a second lamp on the counter and said it was for a room in a building where three people work nights, and asked whether the switch on this one held, and he said it would, and charged her, and she paid, and neither of them said anything about the first one being free. That is a fair arrangement and he has never been able to stop noticing it: the first one was free and it made the second one a sale, and he is the only person in this transaction who knows the sequence, and the knowing is worth about four pounds and costs him something he has not been able to name in two years.**

**The nine hundredth figure on the corridor end is not going to be replaced by anybody. The post by the corridor end of that building has been bare for three hundred and ninety-five days and there is nobody whose job it is, and the wall list in a plastic sleeve in a repair shop in a different district is not a notice and cannot become one.**

**Nobody in that shop on the Monday knew where the delivery note had gone, and nobody in that kitchen on the Monday knew that a sheet of paper with a pencil-ruled empty box on it had left a bench in a repair shop the same morning, and the two facts were about four days of bus travel apart and are not going to be joined by anybody this month.**

**He had let a piece of paper out of the room without finding out who took it, and he had made that choice in about four seconds with a bracket in his hand, and the cost of it is a glove with a split seam in it and the twenty minutes of somebody else's lamp, and the part of the cost he did not pay and would pay later is that the box is now in a pocket in this city instead of on a bench where at least he could see it.**

The man of about fifty-one with a tool bag was at the wall on the Monday night and had said nothing through all of it.

The woman of about thirty in the chair nearest the door was in the room from about half past six and was not asked a question, and her page is the eighth of eight things in a ring binder on a shelf and was not read.

A man of about thirty-three who holds four buildings and about nine hundred doors with no stamp, no reference and no date was not in this city on the Monday and his hold ran on for sixty-eight weeks and four days without anything being asked of him and without anything being given to him.

Nobody thanked anybody. The shutter came down at about ten.

Who came: a man of about thirty-five who keeps the only written record in the first of the four towns, with a rent book in a plastic bag that he did not open, having come on a bus that takes most of a day each way.
