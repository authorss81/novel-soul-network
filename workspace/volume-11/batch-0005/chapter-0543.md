# Chapter 543 — A Man Who Opens And Shuts A Public Hall Is Asked To Run Its Door On The Day And Says Yes In About Nine Seconds, And Nobody Told Him What He Was Agreeing To And He Did Not Ask

**The Thursday was a bench in Lattice Ward until about two, a counter until about six, and a cupboard under the stairs of a public hall from about half past four, and its shape was a man being asked one question by a person with an ordinary job and answering it faster than the asking, and then four days of finding out what the answer had been.**

---

Eleven on the Thursday, eleven dated, eleven came, and the last of them at about ten past five. Bench in Lattice Ward until two, the counter two to six, the rooms off the service road from about half past six, and a cupboard under some stairs from about half past four in the afternoon. Nine and ten is the bench rate, it is wrong, and it was said to nobody this week. **Heating in that flat is on and dearer, four names on the lease are carrying it, and a fortnight a cook laid out of her own wages is owed to nobody and is still going to be owed to nobody at the end of this month.** The service-road rooms are nine hundred and thirty-four days, one hundred and thirty-three weeks and three days, four of them and one behind, and they are warm.

The card in the rail on the front of that counter is nine hundred and thirty-eight days, one hundred and thirty-four weeks to the day, four days more than the room, in a rail that takes two.

---

The man is about thirty-four and he has opened and shut that hall for about six years and his job on the paper is caretaker, and the job is the heating and the lights and the two doors and the bin store and the water stop, and about nine people know him by face who do not work there.

**The woman of about thirty-six who lets the hall out for a charity that owns the building found him in the cupboard under the stairs at about half past four on the Thursday, holding a torch he had no use for, and she asked him one question and he answered it in about nine seconds.**

**"Will you run the door on Monday."**

**"Yes."**

**"You have not asked me what that is."**

**"No."**

**"I am going to tell you anyway and I am probably going to tell you the wrong part of it first, and you are going to take it the wrong way, and that will be my fault and not yours, and I want that said out loud now while there are no people listening."**

He put the torch in his pocket. He did not say anything for about nine seconds, and the nine seconds were the longest part of the afternoon.

---

**What he thought he had agreed to, and he has never changed his mind about this and he is not going to: that he would unlock the two doors at about nine, unlock the two doors again, stand to one side of them so that people could get in, and lock up at about ten.**

**What had actually been agreed to, as it turned out over four days, and this is the whole of what he found out and he found it out on his own because nobody was going to tell him: he was to be the man at the door who counted people in and people turned away, on a sheet with two columns, and who shut the two doors when the business began, which is a signal he cannot see from where he stands.**

The first thing he found out was on the Friday morning, and it was a sheet.

The sheet was on the sill inside the north door, folded once, with about nine days of dust in the fold. It was a printed grid with two columns and a line at the top for a date and a line under the columns for a name, and the name line was empty, and both columns were empty, and the dust said that nobody had stood at that door with it in eleven days.

**The second thing he found out was on the Friday afternoon, in about four hundred people, and it was that the doors on the Friday had been left open, all day, by a decision taken in about nine minutes by a woman of about thirty-six, and that the decision was that the doors were shut on the Monday, and that the Friday had been given over to the second of the two things, and that about four hundred people had come and about nine of them had been turned away with nowhere to stand because there was no room left in the hall with the doors open and nobody in it had been told to leave a gap.**

He asked the woman of about thirty-six one question about that on the Friday evening at about six, standing in the cupboard with the torch still in his pocket.

**"Why shut them on the Monday when Friday was fine with them open."**

**"Because Friday was a thing being explained and Monday is a thing being decided, and about nine hundred people deciding something is not the same as about four hundred people listening to something, and the hall is not big enough for both and the council has one hall."**

**"And the two hundred and sixty from last Monday."**

**"Are outside on Monday."**

**"Say that again."**

**"I am not going to say it again. You are the man who is standing at the door and you are going to be the one who shuts it and I would rather you heard it from me once and then worked it out yourself than heard it from me every morning for four days."**

---

He worked it out on the Saturday, on his own, sitting on the third step of the stairs in a hall with no lights on because the heating had a timer and the timer was not his to change.

Nine hundred chairs. About nine hundred people in them at about eleven last Monday. A queue that had not come down to fewer than about a hundred and forty by half past two. A door that shuts when the business begins, and a man who cannot see the room and therefore cannot see the beginning of the business and therefore cannot know the moment and therefore has to be told it by somebody.

**And on the Saturday, at about four in the afternoon, in the dark, on the third step, he said out loud, to a hall with nobody in it, the thing that has been the cost of his week, and there was nobody there to hear it, and it is printed here because it was said and because a cost that is only ever thought is not a cost anybody can be said to have named.**

**"I am going to be the man who shut that door and nobody is going to be able to say I was told to shut it, because I am not going to be told, because nobody can see me and I cannot see them, and on Monday morning about eight o'clock I am going to stand at a door and wait for a signal from a room I have never been inside during a meeting, and when it comes I am going to shut two doors on about two hundred and sixty people, and if it is wrong then it is wrong and it is mine, and there is not going to be any way afterwards of working out whose hand it was."**

---

On the Sunday he did the thing that no clerk in this city has ever done and that is the only reason anybody knows the shape of the problem, which is that he counted the chairs himself, twice, at about eleven in the morning and again at about four in the afternoon, and got the same figure both times, and wrote it on the back of the folded sheet in pencil under the word MONDAY.

**Nine hundred and six chairs. Nine hundred and six. Four of them are stacking chairs and a man has told him twice that if he stacks them he will lose about four hundred seats and that he should not stack them, and he is not going to stack them, and he has not told anybody that he has written the number anywhere, and if he is asked on Monday morning he will say that he has not written it anywhere.**

He found the bin store at about half past four and it is locked and the only thing that opens it is on his own ring, and behind the door there is about nine years of other people's meetings in the sense that there is a stack of about nine hundred folded chairs' worth of nothing, and a bucket, and a coil of cable, and a notice from a council about a fire exit that was printed in a different decade from the rest of the building.

**On the Sunday at about seven in the evening he walked to the letting office of the woman of about thirty-six, which is about nine minutes on foot, and stood at her desk for about eleven minutes before she finished a thing with a lease, and asked her one question, and it was the first question he had asked anybody in four days and he had been asked none, and what he asked was whether the doors had ever been shut on that hall on a day that anybody was still angry about, and she said that she had been in this city for about nine years and did not know, and that it was not her business because the hall was let, not run, and a person who lets a building is not a person who governs it.**

**"That is the correct answer and it is the first correct answer anybody has given me since Thursday."**

**"Then I will not be insulted by it."**

He walked home at about a quarter past seven. He had the building, and a folded sheet with a word at the top of it and two empty columns, and about nine hundred and six chairs, and four days, and a woman of about thirty-six who lets a building and does not govern it, and he did not watch anything and slept about four hours.

**What the man of about twenty-two wanted, and did not say, on the Thursday, on the Friday, and on the Saturday, and gave a reason for on the Friday, is the third thing on this page and the third thing is that a man of about thirty-four is going to carry four days of a thing alone and shut a door on Monday morning because of what a man of twenty-two did not tell him.**

**He worked it out on the Friday at about six, standing in the same cupboard, and he is right about it, and it is this: if he had said it on the Thursday then the man of about thirty-four would have had four days to decide whether to do it, and a man who has four days can say no, and a man who has four days who says no puts a man of about thirty-six in a cupboard with nobody else to ask, and a man of about thirty-six has a building.**

**So he said nothing, on the Thursday and on the Friday and on the Saturday, and the cost of saying nothing is a decision of his, taken in a corridor, in about nine seconds, that will on Monday be the reason about two hundred and sixty people are outside a shut door, and the man who will shut it will have decided nothing at all and will have been handed a signal, and a man of twenty-two will have been upstairs in a bench in Lattice Ward at about eleven at night, and the whole of the doing will be the man of about thirty-four's.**

He told the woman of about thirty-four who reads meters and works nights about none of it, on the Thursday, at the counter, at about ten past five, when she came in and asked him whether the hall would be full on the Monday and he said he did not know and meant it.

The bench in Lattice Ward had the nineteen lines on two nails above it and had had them for years. The newest of them is six hundred and thirty days, ninety weeks to the day, and it is the only one of the nineteen that has ever closed on a zero. The post at the end of the corridor is four hundred and eighty-two days, sixty-eight weeks and six days. The hold of the man of about thirty-three in another city is five hundred and sixty-seven days, eighty-one weeks to the day. The man at the north wall has been at that wall five hundred and forty days, seventy-seven weeks and one day, with his bag on the floor, and he has said nothing on any day this month. The ask is six hundred and twenty-four days, eighty-nine weeks and one day. Nine copied page fronts are five hundred days, seventy-one weeks and three days, eight of the nine unfinished, the first disagreement in the fourth line not found. The room off that service road is nine hundred and thirty-four days and the card in the rail is nine hundred and thirty-eight, and the two are not the same object and have never been the same figure on a day. The gap between him and the woman of about twenty-four is three hundred and fourteen days, forty-four weeks and six days, and it is not shorter and it is not ended.

Nobody thanked anybody. The shutter came down at about ten.
