# Chapter 541 — A Notice Goes Onto A Shuttered Wall In A Hand, And About Two Hundred And Sixty People Are Still Outside It When The Business Begins

**The Monday had a morning in it that the other Mondays do not have, and its shape was about four hours on and around a public hall off the market street, a bench in Lattice Ward until about two, and a counter until about six, and the hall was the reason a man who keeps normal hours was out in the middle of the day for the first time in about nine years.**

---

Eleven jobs are listed for the Monday, eleven of them carry a date, eleven people came over that counter, and the last of them at about ten past five. The bench in Lattice Ward ran from about eleven at night until about two, that counter stood open from two to six, and four hours of the middle of the day went on and around a public hall. Nine and ten is the rate for an hour at the bench and it has been wrong since before the winter and it was said to nobody. **The heating in that flat is on and dearer than it was, four names are on the tenancy carrying the rise, and a fortnight a cook laid out of her own wages is owed by nobody and will be owed by nobody at the end of this month.** The rooms off that service road are nine hundred and thirty-one days, one hundred and thirty-three weeks to the day, four of them and one behind, and they are warm.

The card along the rail on the front of that counter is nine hundred and thirty-five days, one hundred and thirty-three weeks and four days, four days on from the room, and the rail takes two and has taken two for eight years.

---

The hall stands over a shop that has stood empty to let for two years and the school had it for the morning, and nine hundred chairs went in at about nine, and about four hundred of them came up from a drill hall on a road that goes nowhere on purpose.

The printed notice was in a plastic frame behind glass by the door and the glass has been scratched at eye height by about nine years of people standing at exactly that height, and from the pavement the whole of it was four grey shapes.

**A man of about thirty-six who keeps the shutters up on a shop that is let empty, and who copies notices for landlords when a landlord wants a notice copied, came out at about half past eight with a brush and a pot of the same white the frame was made of, and painted the whole of it flat onto the brick beside the door at about shoulder height, so that anybody standing where he was standing could read all of it while they waited.**

The man at the counter read it from the doorway and then read it again with his chin up against the wall the way he had read about four hundred other notices in his life, and the second reading took him about nine seconds, and the thing he was checking for was not on it.

**It said when the doors opened. It said when the business began. It said that anybody who came might speak, and that speaking would be taken in the order it was asked for. It said two things would be decided. And it said, in the last line, that the doors close when the business is begun.**

It did not say why, and it did not say what the business being begun would look like from a person in a queue, and both of those were on the same line in the frame inside the glass where nobody standing outside could read them.

---

By ten past ten the queue went from the door past the shuttered shop and round the corner into a street it had not been designed for, and there were about two hundred and sixty people in it, and about nine hundred people in the hall, and a man of about twenty-two was standing at the front of the queue with his hands in his pockets because he had come out at about half past eight for no better reason than that a man had said on the Friday that there would be a queue.

**He had wanted, and did not say, and did not write on anything, to be asked to speak. That is the whole of what he had come for, and nobody asked him, because a man of twenty-two at the front of a queue on a pavement is not anybody, and he had known that since about nine years old.**

The woman in front of him was about twenty-five and she worked Fridays and Sundays at a canteen on a corner and washed up, and he knew her the way he knew about four hundred people in this city, which is to say she had been in the shop and he had not been the one who spoke to her.

**"Are you going in."**

**"I do not know yet."**

**"They are going to shut the doors."**

**"They have said so."**

**"They have said so about a half hour ago and there are two hundred and sixty of us out here and the hall has nine hundred seats in it, and I have counted about four hundred of them going up the stairs in the last twenty minutes, and one of those seats is going to be mine, and I am the one who washes up."**

**"Then it is a chair you have paid for in time of your own."**

**"It is a chair I got here at nine for."**

**"That is a different thing and you are right to want it."**

The painter of the notice was coming back along the wall with the pot in one hand, about four steps from them, and the man at the counter could see the brush was still wet at the ferrule and had not been rinsed.

**He did not go over and say it to him. That was the decision he took on that pavement and he took it in about four seconds and nobody saw him not take it, and the reason is that the man of about thirty-six paints notices because landlords pay him to paint notices, and a man who has painted one true notice this morning and is paid by the man who owns the wall is going to be told what to paint on the Tuesday, and the telling will be done by the owner and not by him, and if a man of twenty-two stands next to him on a pavement and explains the last line of the notice then the next notice will be in a hand that anybody in this city can trace back to a counter in Lattice Ward.**

**The cost of not saying it, named exactly: that man is going to paint on the Tuesday and is going to be paid twice, and the second notice is going to have the same last line on it, and about two hundred and sixty people are going to be outside it again, and none of the three of those things is going to be anybody's fault, and the fourth thing, which is that a man of twenty-two could have said nine words on a pavement in about four seconds and changed the last line, is going to stay in a man of twenty-two and is going to be in him at about two in the morning for the rest of the month.**

---

The man of about thirty-six said it, to nobody, while he was rinsing the brush.

**"Doors close when the business is begun. That is what it says. It is not mine, it is on the printed one, I have only copied it."**

The woman of about twenty-five who washes up turned round and said it back, exactly, the way a person says a thing they intend to remember.

**"Doors close when the business is begun."**

She said it to a queue of about two hundred and sixty people at ten past ten on a Monday morning, out loud, with the words in the order they were printed, and about nine of them turned round, and about eleven of the nine said it after her in the next half minute, and by about a quarter past ten the end of the queue on the corner had it too.

Nobody thanked her for it and she was not on anything and the words were not written down anywhere, and by about ten past eleven the doors shut and about two hundred and sixty people were outside them and about nine hundred were inside them, and a man of about thirty-six rinsed his brush and put the lid back on the pot.

---

**A man of about thirty-one with a notebook came along the queue at about twenty past ten and asked about nine people a question, and the ninth of them was the man at the counter, and the question was what he thought would happen, and he said nine words and no more of them than that.**

**"I think the room will be full by eleven and I am outside it."**

**"Write that down, then, because you are the first person all morning who has said where he is standing."**

**"You can write it down yourself. You have a notebook."**

She wrote it down herself. He did not say anything else to her that day, and she used the four words in a piece she filed on the Wednesday, and the piece was about a hall and a queue and not about him, and he read it at the counter on the Friday of the following week with about four people standing behind him and he did not tell any of them.

**Nobody in this city asked a man of twenty-two in about four years what he thought about a thing, and on the Monday morning of this hall he was asked, and he gave four words, and the price of the four words was that a stranger now knew which pavement he was on, and about four hundred people in this city know his name, and one of them is a woman with a notebook, and none of them is going to use it to ask him anything, and that is what the four words bought and it was not expensive.**

---

**At about eleven he went as far as the north door and stood on the step with about four other people who had been let out for air, and from the step he could hear the room and could not see into it, and about four hundred and eleven people were in there making a noise that was not applause and was not talking, and he has heard that noise in about nine other rooms in this city and has never once been able to put a name to it.**

The man of about thirty-six was at the wall with his pot.

**"You are not to paint over that."**

**"I am not painting over the notice. I am painting over the frame, because the frame is the landlord's and the notice is on the brick and one of the two has to go."**

**"That is a landlord's answer and not a reason."**

**"It is a fact about a wall and I will do it because it is my job. I will be back on it at about half past eight tomorrow morning with the same letters and the same last line, and I will be paid the second time as well, and I would like to say one thing about that before you decide what you think of me."**

**"Say it."**

**"I have painted about nine notices in this city in about nine years and every one of them said what the person who handed it to me wanted said, and this is the first one I have copied that I could read, and there is no way on this earth for me to change a word of it, because it is a form of words and I am a man with a brush."**

He put the lid back on the pot and crossed the road and ate at about one, which is late. The man of twenty-two said nothing to him at all at about half past two, and that was the correct thing to say to a man with a brush, and it cost the two of them a conversation that neither of them will report.

The queue did not go away at eleven. It went to about four hundred, and then it went to about a hundred and forty by about half past two, and the man of twenty-two stood in it for all of that because there was a bench in Lattice Ward at about eleven that night and a counter at two the next afternoon and a flat at about half past six, and standing on a pavement for about four hours in a coat is a thing a body does not enjoy and does remember.

**At about a quarter past two a woman of about sixty-one came along the line, looked up at the notice on the brick, and said one thing to nobody, and he heard it because he was the next person in the queue, and the thing she said was that the frame is the landlord's and the words are nobody's, and that a person should know which one they are about to read.**

**"I have been coming to that door since it was a bank."**

She looked at him, and then she said that he was not going in either.

**"No."**

**"Then you are the only person in this queue who is going to be able to say what happened in there, and I am not going to ask you, and I would like you to know that I have decided not to ask you and that you have about eleven minutes before I get on that bus."**

**He had said nine words to a woman with a notebook that morning and four words to a woman of about twenty-five who washes up, and he had said nothing to a man of about thirty-six with a brush, and the woman of about sixty-one gave him about eleven minutes of not asking and he has not decided yet whether that was a gift or a test and he is carrying both readings, and the bus came at about ten past three and she got on it.**

Nothing was decided on the Monday. Nothing was signed, nothing was adopted, no list was made, no minute was kept, and about four hundred competent people in this city could each say a paragraph about how a meeting ought to be run and not one of them had anywhere to put an objection to it.

The man of about thirty-three who holds four buildings and about nine hundred doors is five hundred and sixty-four days into a hold, eighty weeks and four days, in another city, with no stamp, no reference and no date and nobody's authority, and he was not asked anything this week.

The ask is six hundred and twenty-one days, eighty-eight weeks and five days, from the day it was asked and not from any sitting since. The newest of the nineteen lines on two nails is six hundred and twenty-seven days, eighty-nine weeks and four days, and it is the only one of the nineteen that has ever ended on a zero. The post at the end of that corridor is four hundred and seventy-nine days, sixty-eight weeks and three days. Nine copied page fronts are four hundred and ninety-seven days, seventy-one weeks to the day, eight of nine unfinished, the first disagreement in the fourth line not found. The gap between him and the woman of about twenty-four is three hundred and eleven days, forty-four weeks and three days, and it is not shorter and it is not ended.

The man at the north wall was at the wall and had been five hundred and thirty-seven days, seventy-six weeks and five days, with his bag on the floor at his feet, and he had said nothing on any day of that week, and the room a woman of about thirty-one read meters has was shut and a woman of about thirty sat in the chair nearest the door and was not asked and was not thanked and nobody has said sorry to her, and the eighth of the eight leaves in a ring binder on a shelf was not read.

Nobody thanked anybody. The shutter came down at about ten.
