# Chapter 964 — A Sixth Column On Somebody Else's Form

**A Thursday was a bench until two and a counter until six, and the woman of about forty-three works behind a counter in a second district where about nine people were waiting on that Thursday, and one of them put a form of his own on the desk where everybody could see it.**

---

Nine callers went through that shop on that Thursday, the last at twenty past nine, and the shutter came down at ten.

**The four converted units off that service road stood at one thousand seven hundred and eighty-one days, two hundred and fifty-four weeks and three days. The request in the drawer in a second district was thirty days old on that Thursday.**

---

**He had ruled the column by hand with a straightedge and a biro on a copy of her form, and about four people at that counter have said since that he held it flat with two fingers the whole time he was explaining it, and that it was a different sheet from the one she had turned down before.**

He said: you turned the last one down and I put it back in my coat and I have carried it since the winter. This is a different one and this is mine.

The woman of about forty-three said: it is still my sheet, whatever coat it has been in.

He said: I know whose it is.

She said: **then you have put a sixth column on a sheet I made to give somebody a day and ask them nothing at all.**

He said: the sheet has a column called told on it now.

She said: a column is a question with a box round it. That is what a column is.

**Nobody at that counter said anything for about nine seconds, and about four people in that queue have said since that they did not look up from their own papers, because four of them had been in that queue more than once and knew what a raised voice at that counter costs.**

He said: then tell me what a column cannot do.

She said: it cannot say whether anybody was told anything. It can only say that somebody wrote that they were.

He said: that is all anybody can say about anybody.

---

**And then Marek asked the question that turned the afternoon, and he asked it from behind the rail where he cannot be reached.**

Marek said: what is the column for.

The woman of about forty-three said: **for somebody in four years to open that tray and ask who knew, and be able to point at a line.**

Marek said: and point at whose.

She said: whoever filled it in.

Marek said: and nobody will have to point at the nine.

The woman of about forty-three said: no. That is the whole of what it does. It moves the question off nine people and onto one person with a pen.

**About four people at that counter have said since that the man of about fifty-two did not answer that, and that he put his thumb on his own ruled column and left it there for the length of a bus arriving outside.**

He said: then who fills it in.

She said: that is not my question. I made the sheet.

He said: **it is the only question there is, and you are the only person in this city who could put a name under it, and you have just told me why not.**

---

**Nobody at that desk said one word for about nine seconds, and Marek has said since that he watched the man of about fifty-two work out that he had lost, and that he worked it out slowly, and that Marek could not have helped him if he had tried.**

**And then the man of about fifty-two asked her to take the copy and keep it, and about four people at that counter have said since that this was the last thing he did that day and that he said it in nine words.**

He said: you take it. I cannot hold what I have written on it.

She said: I said no to the form. I am not saying no to your copy.

He said: what are you saying.

She said: **leave it on the desk. I will put it in the tray with the others and I will not rule it out, and I have had a tray with nothing in it for four years.**

He said: that is holding it.

She said: no. It is not holding it. Holding it is a thing a person says yes to.

**That is how a second column, thirty-one days after the word first appeared in a margin, ended up in a tray in a building in a second district, in the handwriting of a man who had been told no twice and had left it on the counter anyway, and nobody at that counter asked him to take it back.**

---

**A shop, a stair and a landing took the rest of that Thursday, four jobs in about two hours and no time over.**

A stair light on that landing had been fixed with its earth taken off the conduit, so that the fitting had no return.

He put the earth back where the fittings met and proved it from the landing.

"**Eight pounds,**" he said. "**A stair light with no return is a stair that works until it does not. Four of those fittings in that block are earthed off the conduit, and one of them has been a landing somebody has stopped coming up after dark for four years.**"

A light switch on that landing had been put in upside down, so that down gave more light and up gave less.

She turned it round and proved both ways with a lamp.

"**Seven pounds,**" she said. "**A switch upside down teaches a landing the wrong thing in about four days. Four of those switches in that block are back to front, and one of them has been a corner somebody has had to shout across for four years.**"

A socket in the shop had a blanking plate fitted over it and the plate was live behind, so that a screwdriver across it would have felt it.

She took the plate off and deaded the box and proved it with a tester.

"**Nine pounds,**" he said. "**A blanking plate over a live box is a hole in the wall waiting for a screwdriver. Four of those plates in that row are on live boxes, and one of them has been a bench somebody has put a knife down on for four years.**"

A light in that shop had a lamp holder with no shade ring, so that somebody working over it had felt hot air on their hand.

She fitted the ring and proved the fitting with the shop lit.

"**Ten pounds,**" she said. "**A holder with no ring is a hand that gets warm doing a day's work. Four of those holders in that row have no ring, and one of them has been a bench somebody has turned away from for four years.**"

**Thirty-four pounds is what those four came to on that Thursday, exact.**

---

**That Thursday the fourth of those four rooms was shut from half past six with a woman of about thirty behind that door, and the binder stayed at the back of its shelf, and nothing is offered here about what it holds.**

*967.
Thursday of week 322, at ten. That is the two hundred and ninth day of this stretch of days. That Thursday brought nine callers into that shop and put nine names onto that day's book, the last of them at twenty past nine.
**Nothing sat in on that Thursday and no figure was said out loud in any room in this city. A man of about fifty-two brought a sixth column ruled by hand to the counter of the woman of about forty-three and she said a column is a question with a box round it. *What the day did not settle, and the rest of it.* **A passage under this one runs through ten objects and never brings two of them into one sentence together. A rail with two places cut in it. A door propped by a card creased once. A board hung on two nails. Nine torn corners on nine copies of a front. A tin with a lid that is shut. The shutter. The green book. A binder at the back where nobody reaches it. A dated slip with nobody's hand on it. The pencil in that margin.
The cage that stands down one side of that counter is outside the ten, and so is the paper lying in a drawer in a second district with one name crossed and one name underneath. A page of nine names is none of the ten, is not added to any figure here, and is not printed on this page.
A refusal of a form is not a refusal of a man's handwriting. A tray is not a holder.
A dated rule stands across a question that is somebody else's, and the records behind it are written down and open to anybody who comes and asks.
Nobody thanked anybody that Thursday and nobody forgave anybody. One man ruled a column, one woman told him what a column is, and a piece of paper went into a tray that had been empty for four years.**
