# Chapter 212 — Twenty-Two Minutes With Four People In It

On the Tuesday morning of week seventy-one four people made a room ready in about twenty-two minutes, and it is the first thing anybody has ever done in that building that one person could not have done alone, and the four of them were a cook, a man who works on lifts, a printer and a woman with a diary.

He was in the room the whole time. He did not take part and he did not comment, and the reason he did not comment was said out loud by the cook and not by him.

---

They had a Wednesday to get ready for. There were nine people coming at about two in the afternoon because a woman of thirty-eight with four rooms of folding work had offered to show anybody who turned up how she folds a sheet, and nobody had said yes to her out loud yet, which is a different thing and is the subject of the next chapter.

Asha Reed put it on the counter on the Monday night in a hand that got smaller towards the end of it, and it said: *the room, ready, twice a week.*

That is the whole of the thing. It is not on a wall. It is not a rule and nobody agreed to it.

---

The first person in was the cook, at about ten, because she was on her own time and the market she cooks in does not open until the afternoon.

She went straight to the plug socket that works and put the kettle on it, and then she washed her hands, which is the order she does things in.

The second was the man of twenty-nine who works on lifts, who is on his employer's list for Fridays and had come in on a Tuesday for the first time and had said nothing about why.

He took the nine chairs out of the third room and brought them round in two trips, because he could not do it in one, and put them in a curve with the ends turned in, and stood back and looked at the curve and moved the two at the ends about four inches each.

The third was the woman of thirty-one with a key and a diary, who had a cloth in her bag and did the counter, and the sounder, and the hardboard by the door, which she turned on its two nails so that the writing faced into the room instead of along it.

The fourth was the printer of fifty-eight with a shop four feet wide, who had been in that room twice and had said about nine words both times, and who did the door, and the bell-push on it, and the outside of the shutter.

And Marek Senn stood with a docket under his arm and watched, and did not do any of it, and was not asked to, and did not offer.

---

It took twenty-two minutes and Asha Reed timed it on her own wrist and wrote the number on the back of her hand and then had to rub it out because she could not rub a biro out, and rubbed at it with a thumb for about a minute while four people did not say anything to her about it.

She said later, in the same room, that the rubbing out was the most useful thing anybody did that morning, and nobody disagreed with her, and she did not explain it and was not asked to.

And here is what the four of them found out, and it is the finding of this chapter and it is not about the man with the docket.

At about eighteen minutes the printer said "where do you want the—" and the cook said "on the strip," and the man on lifts said "it goes round the corner," and the woman of thirty-one said "it goes on the strip, and it goes there because that is where it went on Friday."

And the man of twenty-two, who has spent a term and a half noticing the shape of things in other people's work, said nothing at all, and about nine seconds later the four of them heard it, and it is the first time in this whole case that four people in a room have heard their own arrangement at the same moment.

Each of the four has been doing her quarter of that room on her own, on her own day, for three weeks, and every one of them thought she was doing a favour.

---

And the cook said the reason, and she said it with her hands in the sink and she did not stop what she was doing to say it, and she said it once.

"**You have been standing in here for twenty minutes with a docket under your arm and you have not offered to do anything once.**"

He did not say anything.

"**That is not modesty. I have worked behind a man at the front of a kitchen for nine years and I know what it costs, and a room that waits for the man at the front is a room with a man at the front of it.**" She rinsed a bowl. "**You are the only one of the six of us that anybody in this city can go and find, and if four of us cannot get a room ready on a Tuesday without somebody checking whether we did it right, then in about four years there is a man at the front of this room with a key and a wall with his name on it, and I am not having it in a kitchen and I am not having it in here.**"

He said, in about four seconds: "That is the whole of it and I am not going to improve on it."

"**Good. Go and stand on the service road for ten minutes.**"

He went and stood on the service road for ten minutes with a docket and a word that had been at nine all morning, and it was at nine when he came back in and there was nothing to do about it then or at any other time.

---

At about half past ten the four of them stood in a room that was ready and it was not obvious to anybody that it was a thing.

The cook said: "**Kettle.**"

"Last," said the woman of thirty-one.

"**Always last. If the kettle goes on early then somebody is standing about waiting and the room is not ready, it is being got ready.**"

That is the release, and none of the four of them had written it anywhere, and it is the fourth beat of a thing that has four beats and a boundary and a stop, and it is the first time in this manuscript that anybody has put one of those together in a room with a door in it.

---

And then the four of them wrote the page, and this is where the room's rule was actually kept rather than repeated.

The rule is that a thing is recorded in the words of the person who does it. It is on a piece of hardboard the size of a tea tray in six hands and it is the fourth of the nine lines and it was not said by anybody who is not in the room.

Nobody handed Asha Reed a pen.

She put the binder open on the counter and put a clean sheet in it, and then she put the pen down beside it, and she said: "**You four write it and I will hold the page flat, and if I write any of it then it is my words about your thing and not your words about your thing, and that is the whole of the thing the wall says.**"

It took about half an hour and there are four hands on that one sheet and the four of them argued about two words on it for about nine minutes.

The man of twenty-nine wrote the shortest part and his is about nine words. The printer wrote hers and then read it back and took a word out of it. The woman of thirty-one wrote hers in a hand that got smaller towards the end. The cook wrote hers last and it is four words long and it is the last line on the page.

Asha Reed held the page flat for half an hour and did not write one word of it, and afterwards she wrote a line on the inside cover of the binder in her own hand that said *four hands on this page* and nothing else.

---

And she had a fight with herself about one thing and it took her about four minutes and she did it in front of the room.

"There ought to be something on that page saying you were in it," she said, to nobody. "**You were in it the whole time. If somebody finds that page in about four years they will not know whether it was four people or one.**"

"No," said the man of twenty-two.

"**I am not saying put your name on it. I am saying there should be a line that says the room was not empty.**"

"There is a line that says the room was not empty," he said, "**and it is the one you are holding flat.**"

She left it. And the reason she gave for leaving it, out loud, in the room, and nobody argued with it, is that a page which says who was standing in a room is a page which describes a room, and she has been losing that argument to two other people for three weeks and has just found out that she loses it to a page as well.

---

At ten that night he was in the front room with the shutter half down and the binder on the shelf with its four hands in it and a line on the inside cover, and there were nine chairs in a curve in a room that had been empty of this entirely four days before, and about four thousand of those decisions a day happen in this city with no form for one of them.

He wrote one thing.

*215. Tuesday of week 71, ten at night on a counter in the front room of a converted shop unit off a service road in the Lower Wards, with the shutter half down, and this entry is four people, twenty-two minutes, and four hands on one page.*

*The composite thing, as a set of actions and not as a technique.* **Turn a unit with four rooms and a back one into a room nine people can walk into, twice a week. Four people, about twenty-two minutes, timed on a first-year's wrist and then rubbed at with a thumb for about a minute because a biro does not come out of skin. Four roles and each of them belongs to one person and to nobody else: kettle and strip first, at the plug socket that works; nine chairs out of the third room in two trips because one person cannot carry nine, curved with the two ends turned in and then moved about four inches each after standing back; counter, sounder and the hardboard turned on its two nails so the writing faces into the room; the outside of the shutter, the door, and the bell-push on it tested from the service road. The fifth beat of the thing is that it is not finished until the kettle is on, and it is not started until the kettle's place in it has been agreed, and the reason the cook gave for the order is that a kettle on early means somebody is standing about waiting and the room is being got ready instead of being ready.**

*The finding, and it is four people hearing their own arrangement.* **The four hands involved are a cook of twenty-seven on her own time from a kitchen that does not open until the afternoon, a man of twenty-nine who works on lifts and who came on a Tuesday for the first time, a woman of thirty-one with a key and a diary, and a printer of fifty-eight with a shop four feet wide who had been in that room twice and had said about nine words on both occasions. At about eighteen minutes the printer began a sentence about where something went and three other people finished it, and the arrangement is as old as the room, which is about three weeks, and the man of twenty-two, who noticed it and said nothing, is the reason this entry can report that the four of them heard it themselves nine seconds later. Each of the four had been doing her own quarter of that room on her own day for three weeks and every one of them thought she was doing a favour, and the difference between four people and one is not twenty minutes of less work: it is that a room ready on a Friday is not the same room as a room ready on a Wednesday, and nobody had tried it twice.**

*Why he did not take part, in the cook's words and not his.* **He stood in the room for about twenty-two minutes with a docket under his arm and did not offer to do anything once, and was not asked, and the reason he did not comment was said out loud by the cook with her hands in a sink and was said once: it is not modesty, it is that she has worked behind a man at the front of a kitchen for nine years and knows what it costs, and a room that waits for the man at the front is a room with a man at the front of it, and he is the only one of the six of them anybody in this city can go and find. He agreed in about four seconds, said he was not going to improve on it, and was sent out to stand on a service road for ten minutes, which he did, and the word was at nine when he came back in.**

*The page, as four hands and not as six.* **One sheet in a ring binder, four hands on it, about half an hour, two words argued over for about nine minutes. The man of twenty-nine's part is about nine words and is the shortest. The printer read hers back and took a word out of it. The woman of thirty-one's hand got smaller towards the end. The cook wrote last and hers is four words long and is the last line on the page. A first-year of nineteen held the page flat for half an hour and wrote none of it, put the pen down beside it rather than in her hand, and gave the reason in the room: if she writes any of it then it is her words about their thing and not their words about their thing, which is what the fourth line on the wall says. She then argued, out loud and for about four minutes, that the page ought to say the room was not empty, and was told there is a line that says the room was not empty and it is the one she was holding flat, and she left it, and gave the reason: a page which says who was standing in a room is a page which describes a room, and she has been losing that argument to two other people for three weeks and has now lost it to a page.**

*Held out of this entry.* **No count of who was in that room, no rota for it, no list, and nothing written down about the twenty-two minutes beyond four hands on one sheet and a line on the inside cover of a binder; the arrangement on the back of a first-year's own hand was rubbed at with a thumb and is not there any more, and nobody kept a second copy of it. He taught, demonstrated, assessed and assisted nothing and nobody in that room asked him to; the reason is the one a cook gave at a sink and the reason on his own condition is a different one, and both are true and neither is a doorway. No share, no relay, no practice, and nobody taught, demonstrated, assessed or assisted anything. The hardboard with nine lines in six hands, the binder, the folder, the two keys and the card in a rail twenty-six days old are five objects with five owners and do not stand together in a sentence here. The condition is not quoted and not paraphrased. The standing problem is not said, and no rule about who may be asked was invented, and the licensor is unnamed and the field is blank and no message came from anybody. Nothing went over the door and no title, office or post went on anybody. The master did not leave the pocket. Nobody asked him about the room. Entry 215.**
