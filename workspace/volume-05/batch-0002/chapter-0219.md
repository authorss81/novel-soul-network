# Chapter 219 — One Stroke

On the Monday morning of week seventy-three, in a room off a service road in the Lower Wards, with about nine people in it and the shutter up, the first person ever recorded in that room asked to be taken out of it, and it worked, and the man of twenty-two who was standing at the back with a docket had to do it and cannot undo it.

Nobody stopped it. Nobody was asked to.

---

She said it at about eleven, at the sink, before the kettle went on, which is the order she does things in.

"**Take the eleventh one off.**"

It took about four seconds for the room to work out what she meant and it took Asha Reed about two of them.

---

The cook of twenty-seven has eleven things in a ring binder and the eleventh of them is a person's own name, spelled the way its owner spells it, and she asked somebody else to write it in the second week of this volume because she cannot spell it.

It is on the first page. It was the first thing anybody wrote down in that room and it was written three days after a letting clerk of thirty-nine stopped a pen, and it has been on that page for a month.

"It is my sister's," said the cook of twenty-seven. "**It has been on that page for about a month and I put it there, and I asked a girl of nineteen to write it because I cannot spell it, and I want to say the next part out loud in a room instead of saying it at a sink on my own, because that is what I do in this room and you all know it.**"

The room waited.

"**My sister rang me at work on Thursday. She has been in a trade of her own for about eleven years and she rang me and said her name is on a bit of paper in a building and to take it off. That is the whole of what she said and I am not going to improve on it in a room with nine people in it.**"

---

And then she said the part that is the reason, and she said it about a piece of paper and not about herself, and she was right.

"**So I want to be exact, because everybody in this room is going to assume it is about me and it is not. It is not about me. I am the person who put it there. I put my sister's name on a page because I thought a name was a courtesy, and it is not a courtesy, and I have been on the other end of a name on a page twice.**"

She dried her hands.

"**This is about a bit of paper.** It is the only name in this room that is not mine, and it is the only one of the eleven things on that page that I did not have the right to give away, because the other ten are mine. And a name on a page in a binder on a shelf in a room with a door and a bell-push on it is a name anybody can look up, and there is no form in this city that takes a name off a page, and the only way this comes off is if somebody takes a pen to it."

---

The ring binder came off the shelf.

Asha Reed opened it to the first page and turned it round so the woman of twenty-seven could read it upside down, and then she put the binder down flat and did not put a hand on it.

"What is the procedure," said the cook.

"There isn't one," said Asha Reed.

"Then I do not know what to do with my hands."

"**Then we will have to work it out in a room, which is the only thing this room has ever done.**"

---

And it was worked out in about four minutes, in front of about nine people, and this is the shape of it and none of it is secret.

The first problem was that the name was not in the cook's hand. It was in a second hand, and it was in a second hand because she had asked for it, and so the person whose name it was was the only person who could strike it and she was four miles away at work and had not been asked to come and would not come.

The second problem was that about eleven of the people in that room could hold a pen and nobody in it had standing to pick one up, and the reason for that is the fourth line on a piece of hardboard, and it is the sentence that has been on that wall for eighteen days and that the cook wrote.

"**Somebody write it out first**," said Asha Reed, "**and then we will all agree to it and that is a page and we have agreed not to have those.**"

The third problem was the man of twenty-two.

He had been at the back with a docket for the whole of it, as he has been at the back of about nine things in this room, and he had not said anything, and nobody was going to ask him, and Asha Reed said the true thing out loud because she is nineteen and it is her job.

"**There is one person in this room anybody in this city can telephone,**" she said, "**and it is him, and everybody in here knows it, and he said it himself on this floor on the Thursday he signed that wall.**"

"I did say it."

"**So we are not choosing him. We are admitting that we have got exactly one.**"

---

He put the docket on the counter.

He had a biro in his pocket because he has a biro in his pocket. He did not take it out for about four seconds.

"**Say the question out loud,**" he said, "**so that I am not the one deciding it.**"

Asha Reed said: "**Can the room take a name off a page when the person whose name it is has asked for it, and is the person who asked in the room, and the person who asked has not got a copy and nobody else has one either?**"

"**I cannot answer that as a rule**," he said, "**because a rule about that is a rule that is not enforceable against my sister, who is not in this room, and I have been in this room for a month and the only sentence on that wall with my hand near it is the one about the promise.**"

"Then you are not going to do it."

"**I am going to do it because there is nobody else and I have been telling this room since the Thursday I signed that wall that the only person anybody can find in it is me, and that is the argument, and the argument is not true until somebody uses it on me.**"

---

He took the pen out.

He opened the binder to the first page and he did not read the whole page and he did not touch the other ten items and he did not go round the page. He put the tip of the pen on the name and drew one line through the middle of it, top left to bottom right, in about a second and a half, and he did not go back over it and he did not make it neater than it was.

The woman of about thirty was in the third chair from the door and watched it and was not asked anything then or afterwards.

Nobody in that room said one word for about eleven seconds.

"**Right**," said the cook of twenty-seven.

---

And then somebody asked the question that is the finding of this chapter and it was a woman of nineteen and it was not a cruel question.

"Can you undo it."

He looked at the page for a while.

"**No.**"

"Rub it out with a finger."

"**A finger will take the ink off and leave the paper**, and that is not the same as taking a name off a page, and if I do it in about four months then in about four years somebody opens that binder and finds a name with a ghost through it and no sign that anybody ever asked, and this page will be a page about a man of twenty-two striking a name out in a room in the Lower Wards on a Monday, and about a hundred years from now the only record of a woman asking to be taken off her sister's name is a smudge."

"Asha." Talia Venn said it from the back, and it was the first time she had spoken in about half an hour.

"I am not going to argue with him. **I am going to argue with the fact that he is the only one who can be asked, and that is a different argument and I do not have it.**"

---

Nobody stopped it. Nobody was asked to. Nine people watched a man of twenty-two put one stroke through a woman's name in a ring binder, and about four of them had known it was going to happen before the pen came out, and about four had not, and none of the nine said anything about it afterwards in that room, and it is not a thing the room will not do and it is not a promise and it is a line on a wall from that evening.

He gave the reason for his own cost and he gave it out loud because he is the only person in that room who has ever given a cost out loud without being asked, and it is the fifth time in three weeks that somebody has done it.

"**There is a box in a building in Bower Street**," he said, "**that a clerk ruled in pencil in the spring, with a name in it that cannot come out and cannot be corrected and may not take a second, and I have been the reason that box is the only one in this city for about a term and a half, and I have not been able to fix it and I am not going to tonight.**"

"Asha. **What is the fifth thing in that binder.**"

"A person being wrong about what they said. And you have just written a fifth page. **And I have been in this room every day for a month and I have never once seen a page put in that was about something anybody in it got wrong.**"

"That is the price," said the man of twenty-two. "**It is four minutes. It is a five-mile walk each way and it is a sandwich and it is half a morning of somebody's Saturday, and it is the cheapest thing anybody in this case has ever bought and I do not think I am allowed to say that out loud as a happy thing and I have said it.**"

---

And then a woman of about thirty said the last true thing of that afternoon, in about nine words, to nobody, and got it exactly right.

"**Well. I said that out loud. And the wall is still there.**"

He said, "Then it is a line somebody wrote by hand and not a rule and not a charter and not a thing anybody can call anybody on, and I have never once said that word, and a wall is not a set of rules, a wall is nine things people wrote down because they needed them written down, and the second one happens to be the truest thing in the room."

"And I am not on it." She said it flatly, as a fact about a piece of hardboard, and looked at the binder. "**I have never been on it, and I have been on that wall in my head since I came in the first time and watched a woman write down a thing she could not spell.**"

Nobody moved towards her. Nobody said come here, and nobody said your turn. The binder shut.

Talia Venn said, "That is the answer to a question nobody asked you, and you have just given me the whole of the next four years of my working life in a corridor, and thank you for it." And then she went to find a bin and a broom and did not come back for an hour.

---

He got to the bench in Lattice Ward at about ten that night and the drawer under it still had five things in it, four of which are sentences other people wrote, and the tool roll was on the bench and the original was in the fourth pocket of it and nobody has ever asked him to fill in the fifth line and nothing was decided about it tonight, and the master is in the inside pocket of a coat on the back of a chair, and it did not move and it is not going to.

He wrote one thing.

*222. Monday of week 73, ten at night in a repair shop in Lattice Ward, and this entry is one pen stroke, about nine people, a box in pencil in another building, a line added to a wall, and the cost.*

*What was asked for, and by whom, and about what.* **At about eleven, at a sink, before the kettle, in a room with about nine people in it and the shutter up, a cook of twenty-seven asked for one item to be removed from a page. It took the room about four seconds to identify the item. The item is the eleventh of eleven on the first sheet of a ring binder: a person's own name, spelled as its owner spells it, entered in a second hand at the cook's request because she cannot spell it, and standing there for about a month. Its owner telephoned the cook at work on the Thursday of the week before, having been told her name is on a piece of paper in a building, and asked for it to be taken off, and the cook reported that request in the room in about nine words and declined to enlarge on it. The reason she gave is about the record and not about herself and she insisted on the distinction in the room: she is the person who entered the name, she entered it on a judgement that a name is a courtesy, she has been on the receiving end of a name on a page twice, the other ten items on that sheet are hers, and the eleventh was not hers to give.** Her own conclusion, given in the room, is that a name on a page in a binder on a shelf in a room with a door and a bell-push on it is a name that can be looked up, and that no form in this city takes a name off a page, and that the only route left is a pen.

*Three obstacles, each raised in the room and out loud.* **The name is not in the cook's hand, so the only person who could cross it is the woman it belongs to, who is four miles away at work, has not been asked to come, and would not come. Writing the question out first was proposed and refused, on the grounds that a written question is a page and this room has agreed not to have those. And the third obstacle was named by a first-year of nineteen who said plainly that she was not choosing him: there is exactly one person in that room whom anybody in this city can telephone, everybody present knows it, and he said it himself on that floor on the Thursday he signed the hardboard.** He asked for the question to be put aloud so that he would not be the one deciding it, said that he cannot answer it as a rule because a rule is not enforceable against the sister who is not in the room, and then said he is going to do it because there is nobody else and because his own argument about findability is not true until somebody applies it to him.

*What he did, and the four seconds around it.* **A biro from his own pocket, not the first one within reach. The first sheet. He did not read the sheet, did not touch the other ten items and did not go round the page. One stroke, top left to bottom right, through the middle of the name, in about a second and a half, not retraced and not tidied. A woman of about thirty was in the third chair from the door throughout, watched it, and was asked nothing then or afterwards. The room was silent for about eleven seconds and the cook of twenty-seven said one word. Nobody stopped it and nobody was asked to, and that is a fact about the room and not about the man.**

*Whether it can be undone, and the two answers to that in the room.* **No. A finger takes the ink off and leaves the paper, which is a different operation from taking a name off a page; and if the stroke is rubbed out at about four months, then at about four years somebody opens that binder and finds a name with a ghost through it and nothing to show that anyone ever asked, and the sheet becomes a document about a man striking a name out in the Lower Wards on a Monday, and the only trace anywhere of the request would be a smudge.** A first-year of nineteen said out loud that she is not going to argue with him about that and is going to argue instead with the fact that he is the only one who can be asked, and that this is a separate argument and she does not have it. A nurse of twenty-four said one word from the back of the room, which was the cook's surname, and did not come back for about an hour, and it is the first time in this volume she has said one word to a person instead of to him.

*The cost, in the two voices it was given in.* **A man of twenty-two named a box ruled in pencil in a building in Bower Street in the spring, containing a name that cannot be extracted, cannot be corrected, and may not take a second, said that he has been the reason it is the only box of its kind in this city for about a term and a half, and that he has not been able to fix it and is not going to tonight. A first-year of nineteen priced it at four minutes, a five-mile walk each way, a sandwich, and roughly half a morning of somebody's Saturday, called it the cheapest thing anybody in this case has ever bought, and said immediately that she does not think she is allowed to say that as a happy thing, and said it. A cook of twenty-seven said one word and went out to take a break she has not had for about eleven weeks.**

*What the wall gained, and who wrote it.* **A tenth line, in the cook's own hand, on the same unscrewed piece of hardboard that has carried nine lines in six hands for eighteen days: four words and a conditional, meaning that the room performs this removal on request. A first-year of nineteen said there is no procedure and gave the room's only procedure, which is that somebody is asked, and that the asking is the whole of it, and that a person who has been in a room a month can be asked by nine people. A man of twenty-two said the room cannot have a procedure, on the grounds that a procedure is a rule and a rule is unenforceable against somebody who is not in the room, and then said something he had said before in Chapter 206 in different words without knowing it: a wall is not a set of rules, it is a set of things people wrote down because they needed them written down, and the second of the nine happens to be the truest thing in the room. The first-year then said out loud that a person being wrong about what they said is a fifth thing in that binder, and that in a month of daily attendance she has never seen a page go in about something anybody in it got wrong.** It is still nine lines in six hands and seven names, and the tenth line is in a hand that is already on that piece of hardboard, and nobody added a name to it and nobody took one off.

*What the room did not do, in about four minutes and by design.* **The sheet was not reprinted, photographed, copied, entered anywhere or described to anyone who was not in the room. The stroke was not read aloud, not explained, not made an example, and not minuted. About nine people watched; about four had known it was going to happen before the pen came out and about four had not, and none of the nine said anything about it afterwards in that room. The binder was shut at about half past twelve, and the person whose name is in the middle of the stroke is not in this city, was not asked to come, and has not been told that it happened, because nobody in that room has a way of telling anybody anything and there is no form for it.**

*This entry carries no further record.* **No procedure, no count of the nine, no list of who knew and who did not, and no second copy of the sheet in any form. The strike is a pen stroke on a page and there is no system in this case to remove anything from, and the proposition that a rule is only as real as the copy people open still stands and has not been improved on. No share, no relay, no practice, and nobody taught, demonstrated, assessed or assisted anything, and the ninth line on that hardboard was enforced in this chapter by nobody at all, which is the tenth line's whole job. Nothing was settled by a form, a minute, a notice, a report, a committee, a leaflet, a registration or a constitution, and no name, title, office or post went on anybody. The condition is not quoted and is not paraphrased. The standing problem is not said and no rule about who may be asked has been invented. The binder, the hardboard, the sign-writer's board, the two keys, the folder, the Exchange book, the card in a rail thirty-nine days old, the leaflet, the laminated sheet and the master in a coat pocket are ten objects with ten owners and no two of them appear together above. Nobody was asked about the room. The licensor is unnamed and no message arrived from anybody. Entry 222.**
