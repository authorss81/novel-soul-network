# Chapter 217 — Forty-Five

The book in a back room with the shutters up went to forty-five lines on the Wednesday morning of week seventy-two, and it went there because a woman came through the door who had telephoned in the spring, and the only thing that has ever grown that book is a caller who came and signed in her own words.

There have been seven sittings. There have been six gaps of four weeks. Six of those seven sittings at which the book was not opened have become six and a half.

---

The room was hot by about eleven because the shutters had been up since about half past nine, and the woman of sixty-one put a hand on the shutter at eleven and that was the signal, and the tin went back into a drawer with no lock on it and the drawer has never had a lock on it.

There are eight chairs round the table and a ninth one standing against the wall with its back to the room, put there by hand by a young woman of nineteen who has been coming to that room since she was fifteen and who has never once been asked to move it and never once been named in it.

"**Thirty-three**," said Bea Nunn, at about half past ten.

Nobody had asked.

"It was twenty-nine on the Wednesday of week sixty-four and thirty-one on the Wednesday of week sixty-eight and it is thirty-three this morning, and I say that number out loud in this room every four weeks and I have said it twelve times and nobody has ever written it down. **Two of them rang me in the four weeks since and I told both of them to come on a Wednesday, which is what I say, and one of them is not here and I have not telephoned her and I am not going to.**"

"And the other one," said a man of about thirty.

"**I am not going to say which of the four is in this room and I gave the reason for that in the spring and I am not going to give it again, and the reason has not changed, which is that the answer to why anybody does not come is then *because a woman rang them twice*, and that answer would be true and it would be the whole of it.**"

---

The book has four columns and forty-four lines and it has been at forty-four since a Wednesday a long time ago.

The rule is given at every sitting of that room and it is given in one go and it is not shortened and it is not improved.

"**A book of what a caller said cannot be written by the people who did not call.**"

She put her hand on the tin.

"There were six sittings at which this book did not open. That is the Wednesdays of weeks forty-eight, fifty-two, fifty-six, sixty, sixty-four and sixty-eight, which is six sittings and five gaps of four weeks. This morning is the seventh sitting, which is six gaps, and the count that used to be six is six and a half, and there is nobody in this city who can tell you what a half is meant to mean, and neither can I, and I have not gone to find out."

---

There were two items and four minutes each, and neither of them is in this account in any detail, and neither of them was written down.

The first was a man of about thirty-eight who has been asked to sign something at work and wanted to know what happens if he does not. She told him what she tells about four hundred people in nineteen years and he wrote nothing down and neither did she.

The second was a woman of about twenty-six who wanted to know whether a person is allowed to say no to a thing that is good for them, and she got the same answer in the same shape, and it came out the same length, and the reason it comes out the same length is that the answer has not changed.

Nobody in that room has a notebook. There is a form in a drawer in another building that any of them could have asked for and nobody has ever asked.

---

She came in at about four minutes to eleven.

She is not anyone in that room's history and she was not referred and she is not on a rota, and she had a handbag and she kept both hands on it for about four minutes, which is a thing people do.

"**I telephoned you. In the spring.**"

"You did," said Bea Nunn.

"**And you said come on a Wednesday, and I said I would think about it, and I have thought about it for about four months and I want to say before I sit down that I nearly did not come, and I want that said while I am still standing.**"

"Sit down."

"I will sit down in a minute. **I telephoned you because somebody wrote down something I said. It was not a doctor. It was a room with a form in it and I was not asked anything. I am not going to say what I said, because if I say it in here then it is in here.**"

---

And then Bea Nunn did something she has not done at a sitting in nineteen years, which was to open the tin.

The tin is on the table and it has no lock on it and it is not a system and it is not a register.

"You have not said the words yet."

"**I know I have not.**"

"**Then that is the only order it can go in. I do not take a word off anybody, and I do not take a word anybody meant, and if you say it to me in a car I will write down *she said it in a car*, and that line has got in a book nineteen years ago and it is the worst line in there.**"

The tin was open for about eleven seconds.

---

And then she said it, and Bea Nunn wrote it down, and the words on line forty-five are the words of the woman who said them and nobody else's.

"They wrote down what I said and I never knew they were writing."

That is the line. It is in the first column and there is nothing in the second, and the fourth column has a name in it because the rule in that room is that the caller's own name goes at the bottom of her own words and not anybody's.

Bea Nunn wrote the words, and then she put the pen down and picked it up again, and then she wrote the name, and then she turned the book round and pushed it four inches across the table.

"Read that."

"That is what I said."

"**That is what you said. Read the name.**"

"That is my name."

"**Then sign it, and do not tell me anything else about yourself, and I will not ask you anything else about yourself, and that is not me being careful, that is me not being able to help you with it.**"

She signed it. She signed it herself, with a pen off that table, in the fourth column, under her own words, and she did not use a printed hand and she did not ask anybody how to spell it.

---

And then Bea Nunn named the price out loud, and nobody asked her to, and she did not improve the rule and did not shorten it and did not thank anybody.

"That is forty-five. **And the number is not the thing. The thing is what the number does to the next person who telephones me.**"

She put her hand flat on the book.

"**For nineteen years the answer to the question *why has this got forty-four lines in it and not forty-five* has been a rule. It is a rule and it is true and it is the best sentence I have ever had in this room, and it works, and it has never once cost anybody anything, because there is nothing in the book to take out.**"

"**And now there is a line in it with a woman's name under it. So from this morning the answer to that question is not a rule. The answer is that it grew once. And that answer is true, and it is true in a way that means a person is attached to it, and being attached to it means that anybody in this city can now telephone me and ask me when it is going to grow again.**"

Somebody said, "That is not much to ask."

"**It is the only question anybody has ever asked me that I have not got an answer to, and it has taken me forty-five lines and nineteen years, and I have never needed it before, and I have needed it since eleven o'clock this morning.**"

---

And then she said the other thing, and it is the thing that makes this chapter a cost rather than an event.

"**I am not going to thank the man in the corner.**"

He did not move.

"**He has been in nine of these. He has said nothing for about two hours today and I know what that costs him, because I have watched him do it in this room on five Wednesdays and he does it the same way every time, and there is not one of the nine of us who could stand up in a room like this and do what he has just done for nothing.**"

"You can stop," he said.

"**No, I cannot, and you cannot either, and that is the arrangement.**"

She put the tin back in the drawer and the drawer has no lock on it.

---

Nobody wrote anything down. There were about eleven people in that room and none of them has a notebook, and about nine of the eleven had not been in that room since the Wednesday of week sixty-four, and the twelfth thing about that morning is that a woman of nineteen who has been coming since she was fifteen heard the book open and did not say one word about it, and has not said one word about it since, and the woman of sixty-one has still never once used her name in that room.

The shutters came up an inch at eleven because the room was hot and the tin went into a drawer and the book stayed on the table with forty-five lines in it and nobody copied anything out of it.

---

He got to the bench in Lattice Ward at ten that night with a word that had been at nine all day and the door of the room four miles away was locked behind him and he had put that key in his pocket on a Monday nine days before and nobody can take it back off him.

He wrote one thing.

*220. Wednesday of week 72, ten at night in a repair shop in Lattice Ward, and this entry is a fourth-column book that opened after six sittings shut, and a price that was said out loud.*

*The run, recounted on the day and not rounded.* **Sittings on the Wednesdays of weeks forty-eight, fifty-two, fifty-six, sixty, sixty-four, sixty-eight and seventy-two: seven sittings, six gaps of four weeks between them. Six of those seven did not open the book, which makes the run of shut sittings six and a half, and the woman of sixty-one said out loud that there is nobody in this city who can say what a half is meant to mean and that she has not gone to find out. The last shut sitting was exactly four weeks before this one. The rule was given in one go and was not shortened and not improved: a book of what a caller said cannot be written by the people who did not call.**

*The telephone number, as a change and not as a figure.* **Twenty-nine on the Wednesday of week sixty-four, thirty-one on the Wednesday of week sixty-eight, thirty-three on the Wednesday of week seventy-two, two calls in each of the last two four-week periods, and it is now said out loud in that room twelve times and has never once been written down. The woman of sixty-one stated that two people telephoned in the four weeks since, that she told both of them to come on a Wednesday, that one of them is in that room and one of them is not, that she has not telephoned the one who is not coming, and that she is not going to say which of the four callers in eight weeks is the one on the morning, and gave the same reason she gave in the spring: the answer to why nobody comes would then be *because a woman rang them twice*, which would be true and would be the whole of it. She has said that reason twice and has not improved it and has refused to give it a third time.**

*The caller, as a person and not as a line.* **Unreferenced, not on a rota, a handbag, both hands on it for about four minutes. She said before sitting down that she had telephoned in the spring, that she had been told to come on a Wednesday, that she had said she would think about it, that she has thought about it for about four months, and that she nearly did not come, and that she wanted that said while she was still standing. She said the reason she telephoned is that somebody wrote down something she said, that it was not a doctor, that it was a room with a form in it, and that she was not asked anything, and that she was not going to say what she said, because if she said it in there then it was in there.** She is not described in any file in this case and has not been named anywhere else and is not described further in this entry.

*The mechanism, and the order of it.* **The tin was opened for about eleven seconds, and it has no lock on it and is not a system and is not a register. It was opened after the caller had declined to say her words standing up, and the reason given was the only order it can go in: a word is not taken off anybody and a word nobody meant is not taken either, and a word said in a car is written down as *she said it in a car*, and that line got into this book nineteen years ago and is the worst line in it. The words of line forty-five are the caller's and the four columns are the four columns: the first column has *they wrote down what I said and I never knew they were writing* in it, the second is empty, the third is empty, and the fourth has her name, and the book was turned round and pushed four inches across the table and read back by the woman who wrote it before she was allowed to sign her own name under her own words with a pen off that table.**

*The price, named out loud by the woman of sixty-one, and it is not a rule and not a mood.* **The number is forty-five and the number is not the thing; the thing is what the number does to the next person who telephones. For nineteen years the answer to the question why the book had forty-four lines and not forty-five has been a rule, and a rule is true, and it has never cost anybody anything, because there has been nothing in the book to take out. From this morning that answer is not a rule, it is that the book grew once, and an answer of that shape means a person is attached to it, and anybody in this city may now telephone and ask when it is going to grow again, which is the only question she has ever been asked that she has no answer to, and it has taken forty-five lines and nineteen years and she has never needed it before and has needed it since about eleven o'clock this morning.**

*What she did and did not do.* **She opened a tin she has not opened at a sitting in nineteen years. She named the price before anybody asked her to and she did not improve the rule. She did not thank the caller. **She did not thank a man of twenty-two who has been in nine of these sittings and said nothing for about two hours, and who was told she could not stop saying it and he could not either, and that this is the arrangement. Two items were taken at four minutes each, a man of about thirty-eight who has been asked to sign something at work and a woman of about twenty-six who wanted to know whether a person may say no to a thing that is good for them, and neither is recorded anywhere and neither is described here beyond the fact of the two of them.
