# Chapter 211 — Two Keys And A Reason

A key came into a room off a service road in the Lower Wards on the Monday morning of week seventy-one, and it came with a sheet of typing paper, and on the sheet there is one reason written in the hand of the woman who gave it away, and the reason is the only part of that key anybody in this case can check.

There are two keys in this case now. There was one on Friday, and there has never been a set.

---

The woman of about thirty was in the room before anybody else, and she was in it for the fifth time, and nobody asked her why, and nobody was going to.

She had been there on the Friday of week sixty-nine and three times in the six days after that, and on the Monday of week seventy she had been in for about two hours and had not been asked for anything, and on this Monday she had come in at about half past nine with a bag and had sat down in the third chair from the door and had not taken anything out of the bag.

Asha Reed said the reason out loud in the room, to nobody in particular, in about nine words, and it is the second time she has said it and she did not improve it.

"**We are not asking her twice, and we are not going to ask her at all.**"

"That is the right answer," said the woman of about thirty. "**And I would like to be left in the chair and not be the thing a person is careful about.**"

Nobody was careful about her.

---

Ianthe Roke came at about eleven and put both hands flat on the counter, which is what somebody does when they have brought a thing they have thought about for a while.

She is thirty-one. She has run the exchange room on a first floor in a building in Bower Street for six years, with a key and a latch and a diary in which she writes who came and what they wanted and whether they came back. She has been told twice this term that she has no standing. Nobody in this case has ever asked her for the diary and she has never once offered it, and she was not recruited, and she is not thanked.

The key is a cut key on a ring. There is a paper label on it and the writing on the label has gone to a ghost, so that it is legible if you know what it used to say and is not legible otherwise.

"Right," she said. "**Before anybody puts that in a drawer, I am going to say the reason, and the reason is the reason and not a courtesy, and I am not going to be asked twice for it.**"

Nobody had a drawer.

"**You can ring me.**"

She put her thumb on the sheet of typing paper.

"**There is a woman of thirty-one in Bower Street with a key and a diary and you can telephone her and say the room is shut, and she will come and take it out of your hand inside a day. That is the whole of it and that is all of it.**"

---

And then Asha Reed did the thing that nobody in that room had thought of doing, which was to treat the reason as a question and write the question down on a scrap of the back of a docket.

The scrap is not in the binder. It went into the same folder as the reason, and the folder is a folder.

She wrote, in her own hand, on the back of a docket: *who can be asked to take this key back.*

Then she put the scrap on the counter and four people looked at it, and four people answered it, and it took about nine seconds.

"Dessa."

"I ring him at work and he comes," said the man of twenty-nine, who works on lifts, who had come in on his own time and had not been asked to do anything and was carrying a bag of spanners for no reason he ever gave.

"Ianthe," said the cook of twenty-seven, without looking up, because she was already at the sink.

"I ring her and she comes and it is a day," said the woman of thirty-one.

And then everybody looked at Marek Senn, and he had a key on a ring in his own pocket that he had carried for twelve days, and everybody waited.

"Nobody," he said.

---

That is the test, and it is the first time it has ever been run, and it did not hold.

"A key is not a post because the people who gave it to you can take it back," he said. "**That is the whole of the sentence and it came out of a woman of thirty-one in a corridor in Crown Terrace and I wrote it down at the time and it is the sentence this room is built on. So I want to say what my key is, because nobody has asked me in twelve days and I have been carrying it and using it and never once asking the question.**"

He put it on the counter next to the other one. Two cut keys, one with a ghost on its label and one with nothing on it at all.

"**Mine came off a counter in this room from a woman of thirty-nine with a ring file, and she is not a person I can ring about a key. The people behind her are a company. A company is not a person, you cannot ask a company to take a key back, you can write to a company.**"

"That is a ring file," said Ianthe Roke.

"That is a ring file," he agreed, "**and a file is a place a letter goes and not a person, and if I write to them about this key then in four years the answer to the question on that scrap is *write to us*, and that is not an answer, and there will be a man somewhere who reads it and knows which room it is.**"

"So your key cannot be taken back."

"**No. And that is not the interesting part. The interesting part is what it makes me.**" He did not pick it up. "**A key nobody can take back is a key whose holder can never be relieved. Everybody else in this room could be relieved of a thing at a telephone. I cannot. So the first key is not the one that gives the room a door. The first key is the one that makes me a holder, and it made me a holder the day it came off that counter, and everything since has been a room with me in it.**"

---

Nobody fixed it.

That is what happened, and it is worth being exact about, because the alternative is a lie about who was in the room.

Nobody invented a person to hold the first key, because a person invented to hold a key is a post with a name on it, and every one of them in that room had spent the last three weeks refusing that. Nobody fitted a second lock. Nobody wrote a list. Nobody suggested that the first key go back in an envelope, and the man of twenty-two said out loud that it was not going back in an envelope, and gave the reason, which was that an envelope is a thing you can lose and a key in your pocket is a thing you know you have, and that the second key was a test and the test has been run.

Asha Reed said the hard version, because she is nineteen and it is her job in that room to say the hard version before somebody else does.

"**Then the best key in this room is the one nobody can take off you.**"

"Yes."

"**And the one anybody can take off you is a key that is only as safe as my telephone.**"

"Yes," said Ianthe Roke, "**and that is the best sentence anybody has said about a key in this city and it is nine words and it is about me.**"

---

And then she named her own cost, which nobody had asked her for, which is what everybody in that room does.

"**I have put my name on a piece of paper in a room with a door and a bell-push in it.**" She did not take the sheet of typing paper back off the counter. "**I have been careful for four years and I am the only person in this case who has been careful for four years, and being careful has meant that nobody can find me, and there is a form in a building in Crown Terrace with a job title at the top of it and my name in a box, and I have never been able to find out who reads it.**"

Nobody said anything.

"**So I am going to be findable about one thing, which is a key, and about nothing else, and if that is a small door then it is the door I can afford.**"

Nobody thanked her. She picked up her bag and went, and the sheet of typing paper stayed on the counter where she had put it, and Asha Reed put it in a folder and the folder went on the shelf beside the ring binder.

The scrap with the question on it went in the folder with it, and the four answers went nowhere, because nobody wrote them down, and there is no page in this case anywhere that says who can be asked about a key.

---

And it changed nothing, which is the fifth thing in three weeks in that room that has been done correctly and changed nothing.

The folder is on a shelf. It has been opened once, by the first-year, on the Thursday of week seventy-two, and the nine lines on the hardboard by the door were four days old at the time and had not been read by anybody before the wall went up either. The answer to the question on the scrap is still the four people who gave it and no written record of any of it.

And on the Sunday of that week, and the Sunday after, the unit was shut. The shutter was down and the door was locked and the sounder on the wall by the counter, which rings and is audible from the service road, was in a room with nobody in it, and there were two keys in this city that open that door and one of them opens a first floor in another building, and the person who can be asked about the first floor is a woman of thirty-one, and the person who can be asked about the shop is nobody at all.

---

At ten that night in the shop in Lattice Ward the docket had two job numbers on it and the word had been at nine all afternoon. The shutter was up, the lamp was on, and the tool roll was on the bench with the original in the fourth pocket, and nobody had asked him to fill in the fifth line and he had not decided anything about it, and there was a page and a half of a fortnight's work in a folder on a counter four miles away with a question on the back of a docket in it.

He wrote one thing.

*214. Monday of week 71, ten at night in a repair shop in Lattice Ward, and this entry is a second key, a sheet of typing paper, and a question four people could answer and one they could not.*

*Two keys, as a count and not as a system.* **One cut key on a ring with nothing on it, carried in a pocket for twelve days and handed over on a counter at about eleven in the morning on the Wednesday of week 69, by a woman of thirty-nine with a ring file, from a freehold that is an entry on a schedule of title, in a building whose plate outside carries a company's telephone number and no name. One cut key on a ring with a faded paper label on it whose writing has gone to a ghost, handed over on a counter at about eleven in the morning on the Monday of week 71, by a woman of thirty-one who runs an exchange room on a first floor in Bower Street with a key and a diary, and who has been told twice this term that she has no standing. There are two keys and there has never been a set, and the room's plan is nine, and nobody in this case has met the other seven and the whole of the defence against a holder becoming a post is that nobody knows all nine. No lock has been fitted, no list has been written, no second holder has been invented, and nobody in that room suggested the first key go back in an envelope and the man who holds it said out loud that it was not going back in an envelope.**

*The test, and what it returned.* **A scrap of the back of a docket, in a first-year's own hand, carrying one question: who can be asked to take this key back. Four answers were given to it in about nine seconds and two of the four were about a person and two were about a room. A man of twenty-nine who works on lifts: ring him at work and he comes. A cook of twenty-seven: ring him at work and he comes. A woman of thirty-one with a diary: ring her and she comes, and it is a day. And a man of twenty-two: nobody. The finding, in the order he gave it, and it is his own conclusion and not an improvement on anybody's: the sentence that a person holding a key is not a post until in about four years a person with a key is a post is the sentence the whole of this is built on, and the difference between the two is whether the people who handed it over can take it back, and the people who handed over the first one are a company with a ring file, and a company is not a person and cannot be asked to take anything back. A key nobody can take back is a key whose holder can never be relieved, and so the first key is not the one that gives the room a door — the first key is the one that makes him a holder, and it made him a holder the day it came off the counter.** The hard version was said by the first-year of nineteen before anybody else said it: the best key in that room is the one nobody can take off you, and the one anybody can take off you is a key that is only as safe as a telephone.

*The cost, in the giver's own words, unasked.* **A woman of thirty-one put her name on a sheet of typing paper in a room with a door and a bell-push in it, and gave the reason she gave: she has been careful for four years and being careful has meant nobody can find her, and there is a form in a building in Crown Terrace with a job title at the top of it and her name in a box that she has never been able to find out who reads. She is going to be findable about one thing, which is a key, and about nothing else, and she said that if that is a small door then it is the door she can afford. Nobody thanked her. She picked up her bag and went.**

*The woman of about thirty, on her fifth visit, and the reason nobody asked.* **In at about half past nine with a bag and nothing taken out of it, in the third chair from the door, for about an hour and a half, asked for nothing and asked of nothing. A first-year of nineteen said the reason out loud in the room, in about nine words, for the second time and not improved: they are not asking her twice and they are not going to ask her at all. The woman gave it back, in her own words, and the second half of what she said is that she would like to be left in the chair and not be the thing a person is careful about. Nobody was careful about her. She is not asked about the keys, she is not on the wall, she is not in the binder, she is not on the scrap, and she is not the person who says anything in this chapter.**

*What this entry does not carry.* **No drawer was named and no page was made, and there is no list in this case of anybody who may open a door or of who holds a key, and the four answers anybody gave to a question on the back of a docket were not written down by anybody. The sheet of typing paper and the scrap are in a folder on a shelf, and a folder is a folder. The Exchange book is at forty-four lines and has now been shut at six sittings. The card in a rail is twenty-five days old and is not referred to. No share, no relay, no practice, and nobody taught, demonstrated, assessed or assisted anything, and a room off a service road is not a practice in that department and the reason is not that he is the exception. Nothing was put over the door and no title, office or post went on anybody, and a woman of thirty-one with a key is not one and has said so twice this term and was not thanked. The condition is neither quoted nor paraphrased. The standing problem is not said and no rule about who may be asked was invented. The licensor is unnamed and the field is blank. The master did not move and nobody decided anything about it. No message arrived from anybody. Nobody asked him about the room. Entry 214.**
