# Chapter 218 — A Person In A Room And A Person In A Sentence

A man of thirty-one who fits hearing aids brought a thing he does four hundred times a week into a room off a service road in the Lower Wards on the Thursday evening of week seventy-two, and a first-year of nineteen wrote it down in his own words in about four minutes, and when he read it back it was not what he had said, and nobody in that room was at fault.

That is the whole of this chapter and it is the first time in this case that a page has been wrong.

---

He had come in on the Wednesday of the week before to fold a sheet, badly, on purpose, without knowing it, and he had come back on the Thursday of the week before to fold another one, badly, on purpose, and neither time had he said anything about why he was there.

He is thirty-one. He fits hearing aids and he has done it for nine years and he works from three rooms and out of a van, and about four hundred people a week sit down in front of him for about half an hour.

"**Right,**" he said, on the Thursday of week seventy-two, at about seven. "**I have got something and I have been thinking about it since Wednesday, and I have not written it down, and I am not going to pretend to you that I have.**"

Nobody had asked him to pretend to anything.

---

What he does is listen to a room for about three seconds without asking anybody anything.

A person who has stopped wearing their aid does not go quiet. That is the part everybody gets wrong. They keep answering, on time, and they turn further than they need to, and they look at your mouth about nine inches lower than the mouth is, and they do all three of those things and none of the three of them is a fault in the hearing aid.

He said it three times and he said it in about nine different sentences and he was good at it.

He has been in a room with Asha Reed in it and has talked for about two minutes, and then he said: "**You have got a minute and I will tell you what it is for, and then you can decide whether to write it down.**"

---

And Asha Reed asked him the right question and the right question had a use in it.

"What is it for."

He answered that. He answered it properly and he answered it for about a minute and a half, and about nine people in that room heard the answer, and the answer was a claim about a use.

It was this: it is for when somebody has stopped. Because a person who has stopped will go on answering you for months, and nobody finds out, and the moment you find out is the moment somebody tells them they have been talking to a wall for a year, and you cannot give that back.

And then he added the bit that turned out to matter and he added it as though it were a footnote and it is not a footnote.

"**And it is for the ones who cannot tell you, because they have been stopped for a long time and they have got very good at it, and the good ones are the ones you will not catch.**"

---

Asha Reed wrote it down, in his own words, in the order he said it, in about four minutes, and she is the best hand in that room and everybody knows it.

She put it on a clean sheet. She did not tidy it. She did not use a printed hand. She did not replace a word with a better one.

What she wrote was this, in a hand that got smaller towards the end of it: *when somebody has stopped, watch which way they turn their head and whether they look at your mouth, because the ones who have been stopped a long time are the good ones and you will not catch the good ones by the fault.*

---

He read it twice.

He read it the way a man reads a work sheet for a boiler he has to be right about, and it took him about eleven seconds each time, and he was not angry, and nobody in that room had any reason to think he was going to be.

"That is not what I said."

"**It is in your words.**"

"**It is in your words and it is in the right order and I could not have written it better in about eleven minutes,**" he said, "**and it is not what I said, and I want to explain why, and it is not about accuracy and nobody in here has done anything wrong.**"

---

He did explain it, and it took about four minutes, and it was the plainest thing said in that room in three weeks.

"**I have not told you what I do. I have told you what it is for. Those are two different things and you asked me the second one.**"

"I asked you what it was for."

"**You asked me what it was for and I answered it, and I answered it well, and I have answered it about four hundred times to about four hundred people and I always answer it, because the answer is the easy one. Nobody can do the easy one badly.**"

He put his finger on the sheet.

"**The thing I do is three seconds of a person not pretending to have heard. There is no instruction for that. You cannot put it in a book and you cannot put it on a wall and you cannot learn it off a person in four minutes, and the only way anybody gets it is by doing it about nine hundred times until their ear has it. That is the thing. What is on your page is a set of three things to look at and it is a set of three things to look at that I have been doing since I was twenty-two and it has never once been the thing.**"

---

And then the nurse of twenty-four, who had been at the back for the whole of it, said the thing that is the finding of this chapter and she said it once and nobody improved on it.

"**That is what I do all day**," said Talia Venn. "**I write down what a person says to me and it goes in a file, and about nine times in a year I read it back and it is not what they said, and it is never because they lied to me. It is because they told me the answer to my question and I wrote the answer down and then I used it as if it were them.**"

Nobody said anything.

"**A person in a room is doing a thing. A person in a sentence has got a reason attached, and you cannot get the reason off and keep the thing.**"

---

Asha Reed had gone a colour and she did not put that down either.

"I asked him what it was for."

"**You asked him what it was for,**" said the man of thirty-one, "**and he answered it, and he answered it well, and I would not change the question and I would not change him. You want to know what was wrong. Nothing was wrong. I said a thing and you wrote it down and I read it back and it was a different shape, and that is a Tuesday, that is what happens on a Tuesday.**"

"But I am careful," she said.

"**I know you are. I have watched you be careful for about nine minutes and it is the most careful anybody has been to me since I was twenty-four and I want to say that it is also the reason the page is wrong.**"

---

And then it stopped being about him, and it is the only part of this chapter that could be called the cost, and nobody had asked for it.

She said, to the room, not to him: "**I have a list of four things in that binder that this room will not call a technique. A cure. A promise. A result. An improvement.**"

She had the binder out of the shelf by then. She did not open it.

"**A cure and a promise are the two things people come into rooms for, and I have watched both of them take about nine months off somebody's life, and that list is the reason I am in this room. And what I have just done is write down a fifth one and not notice, and I only noticed because a man read it back, and I have been careful for two years and my carefulness made a false page.**"

"Then what is the fifth one," said the cook of twenty-seven, at the sink.

"**A use,**" said Asha Reed.

---

And the remedy she reached for next was a rule, and she caught it, and she named the catching.

"Right," she said. "**So we need a rule about what people are asked before they bring something here. We need to say the question has to be what you do and not what it is for. Somebody write that down.**"

"**No,**" said Talia Venn.

"I did not ask you."

"**You did not ask me and four other people in this room did not ask me and I am answering anyway, and you know why I am answering, because you are about to write the first rule anybody in this room has ever written that is about how to stop a person being findable. You are nineteen and you have been here three weeks and you already know that every rule anybody writes gets carried to somebody who was not in the room, and a rule about which question gets asked is a rule about which people get asked.**"

Asha Reed put the pen down on the counter and did not pick it up again for about a minute.

"**Then what do we do.**"

"**We do what he did,**" said Talia Venn, and nodded at the man of thirty-one, "**and what you did on Wednesday, and what the cook does about a kettle. You do not write a rule about it. You let the thing be wrong in front of the person who said it, and you do not put it in the book, and it costs about nine minutes, and the nine minutes are the price of the book being worth having.**"

---

He took the page off the counter. He did it without any of the ceremony of the man with the laminated sheet a day before and he said the reason in about nine words, and it is the same number as a lot of things in this room and this one was not improved either.

"**I have got about four hundred people to see this week**," he said, "**and about nine of them are the ones I am talking about, and I would rather know that I have not written the wrong thing down than have it written down well.**"

He put it in his inside pocket, which is the third time in a term that something has gone into an inside pocket in this case, and went.

---

The binder went back on the shelf with three things in it.

The room shut at ten and Marek Senn walked out with a docket and a word that had been at nine all day and at ten since about one in the afternoon, and he had said nothing at all in that room between about seven and about half past eight except one thing, which was that a question with a use in it is a question about a use, and which was said to nobody in particular and has not been improved on by anybody.

*221. Thursday of week 72, ten at night in a repair shop in Lattice Ward, and this entry is a page that was right and wrong in the same four minutes, and a rule that was not written.*

*The man, as a source.* **Thirty-one, hearing aids, nine years, three rooms and a van, about four hundred people a week for about half an hour each. He had been in the room twice before, on consecutive afternoons a week earlier, folded a sheet badly on purpose both times without knowing it, said nothing about it either time, and came back on the Thursday of week seventy-two having thought about it since the Wednesday. The thing he does has a duration of about three seconds, produces no words, and cannot be written down: he reads a person as not pretending to have heard. The three observable signs of a person who has stopped wearing an aid are that they go on answering on time, that they turn further than they need to, and that they look at a mouth about nine inches below the mouth, and none of the three is a fault in the aid. He gave the account three times in about nine sentences and was good at it.**

*The question, and the fact that it was the right one.* **A first-year of nineteen asked what the thing is for, and he answered, at her request and for about a minute and a half, in front of about nine people. The answer is a claim about a use and not a description of a practice, and it is this: it is for when somebody has stopped, because a person who has stopped will go on answering for months and nobody finds out, and the moment of finding out is the moment somebody tells them they have been talking to a wall for a year, and that cannot be given back. He added that it is for the ones who cannot tell you, because they have been stopped a long time and have got very good at it, and that the good ones are the ones you will not catch. Nobody in that room can say the question was wrong and everybody in that room can say it was the right one, and the chapter turns on the difference between the two and not on anybody being at fault.**

*The page, in a first-year's own hand, in about four minutes.* **Not tidied, not in a printed hand, and no word replaced with a better one. It is a set of three things to look at, and it is the three things he has been doing since he was twenty-two, and it has never once been the three seconds. He read it twice, about eleven seconds each time, the way a man reads a work sheet for a boiler he has to be right about, and told her it was not what he had said, and told her in the same breath that it was in his words, was in the right order, and could not have been written better in about eleven minutes by anybody, including him.**

*What he said about the difference, in about four minutes.* **He had not given a description of the practice; he had given the use of it, and the two are different things, and the second was the one she asked for. He gives the use about four hundred times a week and always gives it, because the use is the easy version and nobody can do the easy version badly; the practice cannot be printed, cannot be put on a wall, cannot be learned off a person in four minutes, and is acquired by repetition on the order of nine hundred times until the ear has it. Nobody in that room improved on this and nobody in that room improved on his account of his own work, which is the first time in three weeks that a person has been given the floor for four minutes and used it to disagree.**

*The finding, and who said it.* **A woman of thirty-one who fits hearing aids said that the page was a fifth thing in that binder and that she has never once seen a page put in that was about something anybody in it got wrong. A nurse of twenty-four, who had been at the back for the whole of it, said the finding in her own words and once: her own work is the same shape, she writes down what a person says to her and it goes in a file, and about nine times a year she reads it back and it is not what they said, and it has never once been because they lied to her; and a person in a room is doing a thing while a person in a sentence has got a reason attached, and the reason cannot be taken off and the thing kept. Nobody in that room wrote it down and nobody in that room improved on it, and the man of thirty-one agreed with it and added that the carefulness is the reason the page is wrong, and that it is the most careful anybody has been to him since he was twenty-four.**

*The rule that was not written.* **The first-year of nineteen asked, out loud, for a rule fixing which question gets asked before anybody brings something to that room. She was told no before she had finished the sentence, by a person who was not asked, and the reason given was that every rule written in that room gets carried to somebody who was not in it, and that a rule about which question gets asked is a rule about which people get asked. She put the pen down on the counter and did not pick it up again for about a minute, and then asked what they should do instead, and was told to let the thing be wrong in front of the person who said it, not to put it in the book, and that it costs about nine minutes, and that the nine minutes are the price of the book being worth having. **No rule exists, none was written, and the only four things this volume has on a page are a cure, a promise, a result and an improvement, and the fifth one is now named aloud and is not written down anywhere.

*What went back into a pocket.* **The page went into the offerer's own inside pocket, with a reason of about nine words: he has about four hundred people to see that week and about nine of them are the ones the account was about, and he would rather know he has not written the wrong thing down than have it written down well. **The ring binder went back on the shelf with three things in it. The page is not in this case anywhere, is not a copy of anything, and no second version of it exists, because this is the second thing in a term to have been put in an inside pocket and the two men who did it are not the same man and neither of them knows the other did it.

*What this entry is not.* **Not a technique, not a page, not a draft, not a copy, and not a list of anything offered and not recorded. The practice, the reason for it and the finding are in this entry as a record of what three people said, and no sentence in it can be lifted off a page and used by anybody, which is the last of the chapter's arguments and the reason it is here. No share, no relay, no practice, and nobody taught, demonstrated, assessed or assisted anything by anybody, and the man of thirty-one taught nobody anything in that room, which is what three seconds are. No form, minute, notice, report, committee, leaflet, registration or constitution, and no name, title, office or post on anybody. The condition is neither quoted nor paraphrased anywhere in this movement. The standing problem is not said, no rule about who may be asked was invented, and no number is attached to one. The hardboard with nine lines in six hands, the binder, the folder, the two keys, the card in a rail thirty-five days old, the leaflet, the laminated sheet, the Exchange book, the docket and a man's inside pocket are ten objects with ten owners and are not together in one sentence here. Nobody was asked about the room. The master did not move and nothing was decided about it, and no message arrived from anybody. Entry 221.**
