# Chapter 227 — Eleven Times In A Corridor, Badly

On the Monday morning of week seventy-nine a first-year of nineteen stood against a wall in a corridor in Lattice Ward with a piece of hardboard about the size of a tea tray that she had gone and bought herself, and she said the first line of a wall in a room off a service road eleven times to strangers, and it went wrong twice, once because she said it too fast and once because a man of about sixty said it back to her with the words in the wrong order, and she corrected neither, and by that afternoon one of her four employers had found out.

Nobody learned anything that day. That is the finding and it is the whole of the resolution of this movement and it is not a resolution of anything else.

---

She got the hardboard from a builder's merchant's yard at about seven in the morning and it cost her about two pounds and it is not the piece of hardboard on that wall and it is not screwed to anything and it has one line on it in her own hand.

She wrote the first line and not the other nine, and when somebody asked her why she said it in a corridor, standing up, to a woman with a bag, and got it out on the first go.

"**Because the other nine are about what that room will not do and this one is about what it will not keep, and if somebody hands me a thing in a corridor and takes it off me then the other nine are a list and this one is the reason.**"

---

The corridor is on the ground floor of a public clinic in Lattice Ward and it is about four feet wide and about ninety feet long and there is a desk at the end of it with a clipboard on it and a person behind the desk, and about nine people an hour go past that desk and hand something over.

A thing is handed over at that desk eleven times a day, in a corridor, to a stranger, with a name on it, and the person who hands it over is not asked whether the room they are handing it to can be found.

Nobody had told her that. She worked it out in a corridor, at about ten past eight in the morning, by watching a man of about forty take about four minutes over a piece of paper.

She stood against the wall eleven feet from the desk so that the people going past had to turn their heads to read it.

---

Eleven people in about an hour and a half. She said it eleven times and it went wrong twice, once in her mouth and once in his.

The first time was at about ten past eight and it was too fast and she knew it was too fast and went straight on to the next person.

The second time was at about half past eight and a man of about sixty with a plastic bag said it back to her, and he said it with the words in the wrong order, so that what he said was that the room will not keep a thing unless it can take it back, which is the opposite, and it is the version that is easier to remember.

"**No**," she said.

He waited.

"**No, I am not going to correct you.**"

"**I said it wrong.**"

"**You did. I have said it twice this morning and you have said it once and you have said it wrong, and if I correct you then in about four years there is a person in this city who has the wrong version and no way of finding out, and there is no form anywhere that takes a sentence off a person.**"

"**That is about a sheet of paper, not about a thing people are saying.**"

"**It is the same shape and I have not got time.**"

---

The man of twenty-two was not in that corridor and was not in the building.

He was on grade two until about four in the afternoon, and at about twenty past four he was eating alone at a table in a canteen off a back road with a spike of tickets by the hatch and tea out of a jug, and there were nine people in it and not one of them knew another.

She came in with her coat over her arm and the hardboard was not with her, and she had been crying, which she said about four seconds after sitting down and then did not mention again.

"**I did it eleven times and it went wrong twice, once mine and once his, and I did not fix either.**"

"You bought a board."

"**I bought a board off a man in a builder's merchant's yard for two pounds and I wrote one line on it and I stood in a corridor in a clinic and said it eleven times to people who were going to a desk to hand something over, and I did it in my own time on a Monday morning, and I did not ask anybody.**"

"How many people stopped."

"**Nine stopped and about eleven went past. And one woman of about sixty-eight asked me whether I was all right and I said yes and she said I did not look it, which is the second time anybody has said that to me.**"

He looked at her for a while.

"That is going to be on a wall in about a month, and somebody is going to photograph it."

"**It is not going to be on a wall in a month, and you have not been in that corridor, and you are about eleven miles away on a docket, and I have been telling you what a photograph is since last Friday.**"

"I am only saying—"

"**You are only saying the thing you have said since week sixty-eight, which is that a room anybody can walk into needs a description, and I have been the person who wrote the sentence that says a description is a description of the people in it, and I know what you are going to say and you are about four seconds late.**"

---

And then she said the part that had happened at about eleven o'clock and that she had not wanted to say in a canteen and was saying anyway.

"**A woman wrote a letter to the clinic about me. I work Thursdays at that clinic — I do the stores, I have done it for a year, it is one of my four — and the letter went on a board by the desk, which is where that clinic puts everything anybody has ever written about anything, and the woman at the desk read it out to about four people in a queue.**"

"What was in it."

"**That a girl was standing in the corridor with a board and shouting at people about a room, and that it was frightening, and that somebody ought to look into where she works.**" She turned the cup round on the table. "**And somebody has. My Friday is gone. Nine Fridays, across four employers, and one of the four is a Friday, and it went to somebody else at about eleven o'clock this morning, and nobody rang me to ask me about it, and a woman I have never spoken to has put a piece of paper on a board in a public corridor and my name is not on it and it does not need to be.**"

"How much is a Friday worth."

"**About eleven pounds and it is nine of them, so about ninety, and I have got about two hundred in that account, and I am not telling you that for a reason, and you are not going to ask me the reason, and I have said the number because a canteen is not a room and about nine people in here do not know my name.**"

He said: "You should have asked somebody first."

"**There is nobody to ask. That is the entire content of the wall I bought the board for.**"

---

And then she said the other thing, and it is the last thing she said in that canteen and it is the one that made it a week rather than a day.

"**I did not make it up. That is what I cannot get out of my head and it has been about six hours. There is a nurse who works four miles from here and I have never spoken to her, and in a corridor outside a clinic a few months back she said to a queue of about nine people that a clinic asks a person for nine things at a desk before it asks them one question about any of it.**"

"And you have that instead of mine."

"**I have got both. I have got hers, which is a true sentence, and I have got the first line of a wall, which is a true sentence, and hers is the one that would get a person arrested and mine is the one that a person can act on at a desk, and I have been standing in a corridor for about ninety minutes saying the one that can be acted on, and that is not nothing, and it is also not very much.**"

"Somebody wrote a letter about you."

"**Yes. And the letter is about a girl shouting in a corridor and it is not wrong, and I put her there.**"

---

At about half past seven she was in that room. Nine chairs in a curve, four people standing, the kettle on at half past eight at the point in the day it goes, and the wall with ten lines in six hands and seven names on it, and nobody read any of them out.

The hardboard was in her bag and it came out and went back in three times.

At about nine she said one thing to the room, and it was not reported to anybody in this file and it was about ninety words and it was not improved on by anybody who was standing in it.

And about eleven people a week in this city are still going to be taught that a release waits for a man in a lanyard, and the number is on the back of a woman's own card and is on no form.

---

He got in at about ten. The lamp was on, the shutter was up, the docket had two job numbers on it, and there is a second piece of hardboard in a bag in a corridor in Lattice Ward with one line on it in a first-year's hand, and it is not the piece of hardboard on that wall, and neither of them has a name over it and neither of them can be rubbed off with a thumb, and there is no name over the door.

He wrote one thing.

*230. Monday of week 79, at a bench in Lattice Ward after eleven with the lamp on and the shutter half down, and this entry is a piece of hardboard, a corridor, eleven repetitions, two errors that were not corrected, and ninety pounds.*

*The board, as an object.* **One piece of hardboard about the size of a tea tray, bought at about seven in the morning from a builder's merchant's yard for about two pounds, carried in on foot, not attached to anything, and bearing a single line in a first-year's own hand. It is a second object and not the wall: the wall carries ten lines in six hands and seven names, is unscrewed, and was written in a fortnight by people who were in the room. This board carries one line, was bought by one person, and exists because she chose to carry it to a building she does not work in. The line is the first line of that wall, being the cook's, and the reason it is that line and not another was given standing up in a corridor: the other nine describe what a room will not do, while the first describes what it will not keep, and a person who is about to hand something over at a desk needs the second kind.**

*The corridor, and what happens in it.* **Ground floor of a public clinic in Lattice Ward; about four feet wide, about ninety feet long; a desk at one end with a clipboard on it and a person behind it; about nine handovers an hour. She stood eleven feet from the desk with the board against the wall, so that anyone going past had to turn their head. In about an hour and a half about eleven people went past and nine stopped. The line was spoken eleven times. The line was said too fast the first time, and she knew it and went on to the next person. A man of about sixty returned it with the sense inverted, so that what he said was that a room will not keep a thing unless it can be able to take it back, and she refused to correct him, and gave the reason: correcting a stranger creates a person in this city holding a sentence in the wrong order with no means of finding out that they hold it, and no form in this city takes a sentence off a person. He said the failure was about paper and not about speech; she said the shape is the same and she had not got time.**

*The sentence she did not invent.* **A nurse of twenty-four, working four miles away, whom she has never spoken to, said in a corridor outside a clinic a few months back, to a queue of about nine people, that a clinic asks a person for nine things at a desk before it asks one question about any of it. Both sentences are true. The comparison she made in a canteen at about half past five is that the nurse's is the sentence that would get a person arrested and the wall's is the one a person can act on at a desk, and that ninety minutes of saying the actionable one is not nothing and is not very much. The nurse does not know that anything was said on her account, and this chapter does not put the two of them in a room.**

*The price, counted by the person who paid it.* **A woman wrote to that clinic about a girl standing in a corridor with a board. The letter went on a board by the desk, which is where that clinic displays anything anybody has written, and a woman behind the desk read it out to about four people waiting in a queue. Its contents: that a girl was shouting in a corridor about a room, that it was frightening, and that somebody ought to find out where she works. Nobody rang her about it. One of her four employers is a Friday at that clinic, where she has done the stores for about a year, and that Friday was covered by somebody else at about eleven o'clock the same morning. Her own figure, given in a canteen with about nine people in it who do not know her: about eleven pounds a Friday, nine of them, about ninety in total, and about two hundred in the account. She said the account figure for a stated reason and it was not asked for. The letter does not carry her name and does not need to.**

*The canteen, and the two sentences spoken into it.* **About twenty past four; a canteen off a back road; a spike of tickets by the hatch; tea out of a jug; nine people in the room, none of whom knows another. A man of twenty-two said that the board would be on a wall within a month and photographed, and was told that he was about eleven miles away on a docket and that she has been telling him what a photograph is since the previous Friday. He then said that she should have asked somebody first, and was told that there is nobody to ask and that this is the entire content of the wall the board was bought for. No scene, and nothing said by either of them that concerned the two of them; the words we, our and us do not occur in this chapter in a sentence about the two of them, and the word our does not occur in it at all, and the only place in this movement where it occurs in a printed thing is a company's caption standing above four lines that are not the company's.**

*The evening.* **Back in the room at about half past seven: nine chairs in a curve, four people standing, the kettle on at its fixed point, the wall unread. The board came out of the bag three times. One statement of about ninety words was made to the room and is not reproduced in this file, and it was not improved on by anybody standing in the room.**

*Excluded on purpose.* **The content of the ninety words; the two passers-by who did not stop; the name of the clinic's landlord; the employer who lost the Friday; the woman who wrote the letter, who is not identified here and is not a party to anything. No page, sheet, letter, form or notice arising from this day is held by anybody in this case, the letter is the clinic's and not the room's, and the board is not on the wall and is not in the binder. No share, no relay and no practice is on these pages, and nothing was taught, demonstrated, assessed or assisted by anybody in this chapter, in a room or in a corridor. The condition appears neither as a quotation nor as a paraphrase, in prose or in this entry. Nobody spoke the standing question, and this entry invents no rule about who may be put to a question, and no figure is set against one. The licensor is unnamed, the field is blank, the master did not leave its pocket, and no message came from anybody. Entry 230.**
