# Chapter 228 — A Pair Of Shoes

A man of fifty-eight with a bad hip came back into a room off a service road in the Lower Wards on the Tuesday afternoon of week seventy-nine with a pair of shoes in a plastic bag, and the shoes were not a practice, and they were not his sister's words, and they were not his, and the room could hand them straight back inside about a minute, and nothing else anybody has ever carried into that room has ever been able to do that.

It is the first thing in eleven weeks that the room has ever been able to do.

---

He came in at about half past two, nineteen days after the Thursday, and he was slower than he had been the first time and he put his hand on the frame longer.

"**I have not brought the envelope**," he said. "**I have brought something else and you are going to say no to it, and I want to say before you do that I know that, and I want to say that I did not know that until about eleven o'clock this morning.**"

The cook of twenty-seven had her hands in a sink.

"Go on," said Asha Reed.

He put a plastic bag on the counter and took a pair of shoes out of it.

---

One of them has been resoled eleven times and the other one has not, because he paid for eleven and not for twelve, and the sole on the left is a shape that a man in a shop off a service road cut by hand because the machine does not do it, and he can walk in them for about an hour and a half and after that he is a man with a stick in a corridor.

"**That is not a practice**," he said. "**That is not a method. That is not hers and it is not mine and I have been trying to work out for a fortnight what it is and it is a pair of shoes.**"

---

Asha Reed asked him what it was for, because she asks everybody that, and he said the thing that turned the afternoon round.

"**I went to the shop that cut the last one and the man there asked me a question about the heel and I gave him an answer and he wrote it down on the back of a receipt.**"

"And the answer."

"**It was mine. It came out of me and it had not been in my sister and it had not been in an envelope and it was about my own feet.**"

He put them back on the counter.

"**And I would like to leave them here for a while. Not to be recorded. I would like to leave them here where somebody can see them.**"

---

Nobody had said anything for about nine seconds.

Then the cook of twenty-four — no, the cook of twenty-seven, from a sink — asked him the question, and it was the fourth time in three weeks that somebody in that room had asked a man a question instead of telling him something.

"**What do you want them to do there.**"

"**I want to be able to pick them up and put them down again without asking anybody.**"

"**That is a pair of shoes**," said the cook of twenty-seven. "**You are going to be able to do that. Nobody is going to ask you a single question about them and there is nothing to refuse.**"

"So they can stay."

"**They can stay until half past seven, and then you are taking them home with you, and that is not a favour and it is not a rule and it is the only thing in this building that has ever worked.**"

---

And that is the whole of it, and it is not small, and everybody in the room knew it was not small except the man with the shoes.

The first line on that wall says the room will not keep anything it cannot take back. A pair of shoes can be picked up and put down in about nine seconds by a person with a bad hip and nobody else's permission. A ring binder cannot. A page cannot, and one of them has been struck through once in a single stroke and cannot be un-struck.

The ninth line says the room will not keep a copy of a thing nobody asked to keep. There is no copy of a pair of shoes and there never will be one, because there is nothing to copy.

The line in the printer's hand says the room will not be printed. A pair of shoes has never been printed.

The tenth, in a cook's hand, four words and a conditional: it will take it back if you ask. He did not have to ask.

Every other thing anybody has carried into that room in eleven weeks has been a person, or a person's account of a thing they do, and every single difficulty that room has had has been about whose words those are. A pair of shoes has no words on it.

---

And then the cook of twenty-seven said the other half, and she said it at the sink, and nobody argued with it.

"**Now listen to me, because I am going to say the thing I said about the kettle and the fold and you are all going to think it is the same sentence and it is not.**"

The room stopped.

"**A kettle is not a technique and a sheet of cloth is not a technique and a pair of shoes is not a technique, and if that goes in that book then in about four years there is a man somewhere in this city who is doing it wrong and somebody will be able to put a card in front of him saying he failed, and the card will be about his feet.**"

"That is not going in the book," said Asha Reed.

"**No. But it can stay on the counter, and that is a different thing and it is the first different thing anybody has found in this room, and I have been in here eleven weeks and I have not seen one before and I am not going to be the person who spoils it by saying it out loud twice.**"

She went back to the sink.

---

The man looked at his sister in a binder for about four minutes.

Nobody stopped him and nobody read anything out loud. He read the seventh thing on a clean sheet in a hand that got smaller towards the end of it, and he put his thumb on the middle of it twice, and he did not take it out of the binder and nobody told him he could not, because everybody in the room already knew it.

"**She put a thing in that I did not say.**"

"She did."

"**She said the chair is not optional.**"

"**She said the chair is not optional, and she said that where another person is helping, the count is not taken by the person being helped, and she said the second one about four seconds after she had finished, and she did not explain either of them.**"

He read it again.

"**Nine years**," he said. "**Nine years and I have asked her for a number about two hundred times and I have never once heard that.**"

---

And then he said the thing that is the reason the afternoon is in this file.

"**You wrote that down from a telephone call.**"

"Yes."

"**From a telephone call, in a room, with about nine people in it, from a woman who was in another district and did not agree to it.**"

"I did."

"**And nobody here has ever met her, and nobody here can be asked whether she meant it, and if I had been able to walk in on the Thursday instead of telephoning you on the Friday then this would have been the fourth page anybody in this room has been allowed to take back.**"

"Yes," said Asha Reed, "**and it is the first page in that book that nobody can take back and there is nothing anybody can do about that, and I told the room when I did it and I would do it again and about four people in that room thought I was wrong.**"

"**You were wrong**," he said.

"**Yes**," she said. "**That is also correct and it is also the second time I have heard it and the first time it was from a woman of sixty-one in a room with the shutters up, and I am not going to argue with it and I am not going to stop.**"

---

A woman of thirty-four with a district jacket and a clipboard came in at about four and drank a cup of tea and asked for nothing and left at about half past four, and it is the third time and nobody has thanked her for any of the three.

The cook of twenty-seven put the kettle on at about half past four at the point in the day it goes, and about four people in this city are somebody doing that alone on a Tuesday with no hot water.

The man of fifty-eight left at about half past six with the shoes in the bag and he walked to a bus stop that does not come to a service road and got off one stop too far again and walked the rest of it.

He was the only person in that room in eleven weeks who has ever handed that room something and had it handed straight back, and the whole of what that means is that the room could do it in nine seconds.

At the door he said four words to a man of twenty-two who was standing at the back with a docket and had said nothing all afternoon.

"**That is the first time**," he said. "**In eleven weeks. Somebody has brought me something I could hand back in a minute.**"

"**It is**," said the man of twenty-two, "**and it was you that made it handable and not us, and I am not going to be the one who improves that into something about the room.**"

He got in at about ten and the lamp was still on and the shutter was up and the docket had four job numbers on it, and there is a ring binder four miles away with seven things in it of which six are pages and one is a page written from a telephone call by somebody who has never been in the room, and the wall has ten lines in six hands and seven names on it, and the card in a rail in this building is about twelve weeks old and has a name on the front of it and nothing on the back.

He wrote one thing.

*231. Tuesday of week 79, at a bench in Lattice Ward at about ten with the lamp on and the door open and the roll out, and this entry is a pair of shoes and eleven weeks.*

*The object, described as an object.* **One plastic bag; one pair of shoes, men's, resoled eleven times on the left and once on neither, the left sole cut by hand because a machine does not make that shape; wearable for about an hour and a half, after which the wearer is a person with a stick in a corridor. The offerer is the man of fifty-eight with the hip that did not go right, who presented it as being neither a practice, a method, his sister's words, nor his own.He had been in the room once, nineteen days earlier, with an envelope containing a sequence written by a woman of sixty-four in another district, and had been refused; he came back without the envelope and said he had known the refusal was coming and had not known that until the morning of the day he was asking.**

*Why it was offered, and in what words.* **A man who cuts soles by hand asked him a question about the heel, and the answer was his own, and the man wrote it on the back of a receipt.The stated request was not that anything be recorded: it was that the shoes be left where somebody could see them, and the reason he gave, asked, was that he wanted to be able to pick them up and put them down again without asking anybody.** He was told, in four words and a conditional already on that wall, that they could stay until the shutter came down and that he would then take them home, and that this is neither a favour nor a rule.

*Why it is the first thing of its kind, set against the wall line by line.* **The first line states that the room keeps nothing it cannot take back, and a pair of shoes can be lifted and returned in about nine seconds by a person with a bad hip and no other permission, whereas a binder cannot and a page cannot, and one page in that binder has been struck through once and cannot be restored. The ninth line states that no copy of a thing nobody asked to keep is held, and a shoe admits of no copy. The line in a printer's hand states that the room will not be printed, and a shoe has never been printed. The tenth line, four words and a conditional, written by that same cook on the Monday of week seventy-three and now about six weeks old, was not invoked, because no request under it was necessary.Every earlier offering in eleven weeks has been a person or a person's account of a thing they do, and every difficulty that room has had has concerned whose words those are. The finding of this chapter is that the object carries no words and is the first thing that room has been able to hold.**

*What the cook of twenty-seven said, twice, and the difference.* **That a kettle is not a technique, that a sheet of cloth is not a technique, and that a pair of shoes is not a technique; that a record of any of the three would in about four years produce a person somewhere doing it wrongly with a card in front of them saying so, and that in the case of the shoes the card would be about a man's feet. The page was not written and the binder was not opened for it.She then named the difference in terms: an object may stay on a counter where it is visible and be taken away at the end of a day, and that is a different class of event from a page, and she said it once and refused to repeat it.**

*The seventh thing, and what the man made of it.* **He read the sheet in the binder for about four minutes, unassisted, and nobody read any of it aloud and nobody stopped him. He put a thumb on the middle of it twice. It contains two sentences he did not say: a chair is not optional, and where a second person is helping, the count is not taken by the one being helped.He stated that he has asked his sister for a number about two hundred times in nine years and has never heard that second sentence. He then established, in the room, that the page exists because a woman telephoned a company's number on a plate, spoke to a first-year of nineteen, and was written down from a telephone call by somebody who has never met her and cannot be asked whether she meant it; and he said that had he been able to walk in on the Thursday instead, that page would have been the fourth thing in that binder anybody there could take back. The first-year of nineteen answered yes, stated that it is the first page in the binder that nobody can take back, and said she told the room when she wrote it and would do it again. He said she was wrong. She agreed, said that is also correct, said that the first person to tell her so was a woman of sixty-one in a room with the shutters up, and said she would not argue it and would not stop.**

*The rest of the afternoon, as a count.* **A woman of thirty-four with a district jacket and a clipboard came in at about four, drank one cup of tea, asked for nothing, and left at about half past four; this is the third such visit and she has not been thanked for any of them. The kettle went on at about half past four at its fixed point. A woman of about thirty sat in the third chair from the door for the whole of the afternoon with her bag on her lap and was asked nothing by anybody, and is not on the wall and is not in the binder. The man of fifty-eight left at about half past six with the shoes in the bag, walked to a stop that does not serve a service road, alighted one stop too far, and walked the rest of the distance.At the door he said, to a man of twenty-two who had said nothing all afternoon, that this was the first time in eleven weeks that somebody had brought him something which could be handed back inside a minute. He was told that the shoes were handable because of what he had done and not because of the room, and that this was not going to be improved on into something about the room.**
