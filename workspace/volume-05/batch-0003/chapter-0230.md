# Chapter 230 — Twelve Lines, Seven Things, And A Corridor

On the Thursday evening of week seventy-nine the wall in a room off a service road in the Lower Wards had two more lines on it than it had a fortnight ago and the ring binder had one more thing in it than it had, and the room had begun, four days earlier, to tell people what it would not hold before they handed it over, badly, in a corridor, standing up, on a piece of hardboard a woman of nineteen had gone and bought herself.

It is eleven weeks and three days old and it is the smallest room anybody in this case has ever built and it is still the only new thing in the city.

---

The room got busy at about half past seven and got smaller at the same time, and a first-year of nineteen said the arithmetic out loud to a man of twenty-two with a docket and got it right for the fourth time in eleven weeks.

"**About eleven people. Four of them standing. The same as the Thursday three weeks ago.**"

"**It is busier and smaller**," said the man of twenty-two.

"**It has been busier and smaller since the middle of the month and you have not been here for one of the two**," she said, and about four people laughed, and he did not, and he was not at the back, he was standing in the two feet of floor with everybody else for the first time in eleven weeks and nobody made a thing of it and about nine people noticed.

---

And then the wall.

The hardboard by the door is about the size of a tea tray and it is not screwed to anything, and on Thursday of week seventy it had nine lines in six hands and seven names on it, and on the Monday of week seventy-three a cook of twenty-seven had added a tenth, in a hand that was already on it, four words and a conditional.

On Thursday of week seventy-nine it went to twelve.

The eleventh line was written first and it was the man of fifty-eight with the bad hip, and he wrote it standing up, and he read it back to the room before he let Asha Reed know that he wanted it on the wall at all.

"**It will not keep a thing whose words are somebody else's.**"

"**That is eleven words**," said the cook of twenty-seven, from the sink.

"**It is eleven words and it is the only thing I have learned in a fortnight and I have had it since the Monday, and I did not have it on the Thursday you said no to me, and that is the whole of what your room did.**"

And Asha Reed said the thing about the two men of fifty-eight, out loud, in the room, because there are two of them and it had to be said.

"**For the record, and I am saying it now so that nobody has to ask in four years: that is the second man of fifty-eight to write in this room. The first one was the Monday of week seventy and he refused to write anything and I think about him about twice a week. They are not the same man. The other one does not inspect buses.**"

---

And the twelfth line was hers, and it took her a while, and she did not improve it.

"**It will tell you what it will not hold before you hand it over.**"

Nobody said anything for about four seconds.

Then the man of fifty-eight said: "**I have been coming to this room for a fortnight and nobody told me that in four words and I have been doing the other thing, which is finding out afterwards.**"

"**You have not been doing the other thing**," said Asha Reed. "**You have been doing the first line and it has taken you fourteen days and about ninety pounds, and I bought a board off a man in a yard for two pounds and did it in a corridor in about ninety minutes and a woman wrote a letter about me, and both of those are worse than being told and I would like that said in this room and I would like it said now.**"

---

Which brings the movement to the place it was always going to arrive at, and it arrived at about a quarter to nine on a Thursday, in a room with about eleven people in it, and it did it in about four minutes and nothing was written down and nothing was minuted and it is not a rule.

"There is a piece of hardboard in a corridor in Lattice Ward," she said, "**with one line on it that I paid two pounds for, and I have said it eleven times and I got it wrong twice and I did not fix either, and a woman wrote a letter about me and I have lost nine Fridays and about ninety pounds, and I would do it again, and that is what a room looks like when it starts telling people what it will not hold.**"

"You cannot do that every day," said a man of twenty-nine who works on lifts, out of the fourth chair he has been sitting in since seven with a bag of spanners he has never once opened in that building.

"**No. I cannot do that every day and I cannot do it on a Friday because I am on four of them and one of the four is at a clinic, and I cannot do it in here because nine people in here already know the line and telling a person a thing they already know is not telling them anything.**"

"Then what is it," said the man of twenty-nine. "**That is the best hour and a half I have had in four years**" he had said once, at a door, in week seventy-one, and had not explained it and had not been asked. "**What is the corridor for.**"

"**It is for the people who are not here.**"

---

A woman of twenty-four said the last true thing about it and said it once and it was about method and not about anybody in the room.

"**Four days ago a girl stood in a corridor in a clinic and said a sentence eleven times to people going past, and it was the first time in four years that anything has been said about that room which was not made by one of the people standing in it being photographed, and it was said badly, and I want to say one thing about badly.**"

"Go on."

"**Badly is the only shape available. If it is done well then somebody will come and ask us how, and there is no how, and the how is a person, and the person is findable.**" She had her hand on the counter and not on the binder. "**I am not going to be in the picture. I have never been in the picture. That is not modesty and it is not a rule and it is the only arrangement in this building I have ever been able to rely on.**"

Nobody asked her about that either.

---

And the ring binder came off the shelf and was opened on the counter and not on a mat on the floor, because there was no room on the floor.

Seven things.

Eleven items in a cook's hand, with the eleventh of them being a person's own name struck through in a single stroke and not erased, and about four feet of the sheet after the strike is clean paper. Four words in a first-year's hand, being the four things that room will not call a technique. One sheet with four hands on it, being the first thing four other people did in that room while a fifth stood at the back with a docket. One page each from a printer of fifty-eight, a man of twenty-nine who works on lifts, and a woman of thirty-one with a key and a diary. And a clean sheet in a first-year's hand with a hand that got smaller towards the end of it, written from a telephone call, by a woman in another district, about a man who was standing in the room, and which is the only page in that binder nobody in that room can take back.

Asha Reed read the seven out loud and read the contents of none of them, and had done that before, in week seventy-three, and about four people noticed that she had done it twice.

"**Seven**," she said. "**Six of them in six hands and one of them in a hand that has never stood in this room, and six of the seven have his name on them, and he is the only one of the seven that anybody in this city can go and find.**"

"I know," said the man of twenty-two.

"**Then we are going to go on with seven**," she said, "**and you are not going to do anything about it, and neither am I, and I have thought about it for a week and there is nothing else to do with it, and about four people in this room have said so to me privately and one of them is a man of fifty-one with a tool bag.**"

---

The cook of twenty-seven put the kettle on at half past eight at the point in the day it has gone on since the first week, and about four people a week in this city are somebody doing that alone on a Tuesday with no hot water, and a woman of thirty-one with a key and a diary did the door and the outside of the shutter at about half past nine and found that the sounder had been knocked by a trolley and had come loose at the top, and put two screws into it in about four minutes, and tested it from the service road, and it rang, and nothing happened, because a sound at a man on a step does not make anybody come.

And a woman of about thirty was in the third chair from the door for the whole of the evening with her bag on her lap. She is not in the photograph that about nine hundred people have, and she is the only one of the seven this room is made out of whose face is not in it, and she is not on the wall, is not in the binder, is on no document in this case, and has never been asked why she comes, and about four people in that room wanted to ask her and did not, and it is the last thing in this volume that is not written down.

About eleven people a week in this city are still going to be taught to wait for a man in a lanyard; four of the eleven are on no list at all; the count is on the back of a woman's own card and nowhere else; and a name on a wall carrying twelve lines is not a rota, and nobody in that room said otherwise.

---

He shut the shutter at about ten with the lamp still burning and four job numbers on the docket, and the tool roll was out on the bench, and in the fourth pocket of it there is a corner of card the size of a playing card, a photocopy of a photocopy, with a fifth line on it that has been blank for a term and a half, that nobody has ever asked him to fill in, and about which nobody decided anything tonight.

The card in a rail in Lattice Ward is about twelve weeks old. It has his name on it in his own hand and a week at the bottom left and nothing on the back of it, and it was the last thing he had, and it is still the only thing anybody in this city has learned how to find.

He wrote one thing.
