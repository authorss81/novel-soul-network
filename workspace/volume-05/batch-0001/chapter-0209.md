# Chapter 209 — Four Feet Away

There is a trestle table in a market in Saltmarket on a Wednesday afternoon, and it has been put there by a company that does not know what is on it, and there is a leaflet on it with a name at the bottom of it, and there is an amplifier and a microphone on a stand with a lead in it going down through the trestle and out across the floor to a socket on a pillar about nine feet away, and about forty people go past that table in an hour.

Nobody is paid to be there. That is not modesty. It is the entire reason a woman of thirty-four with a clipboard was able to put a leaflet on it in the first place.

---

The company has a stall-holder's licence for two pitches in that market and uses one of them about nine times a year, and the licence comes with a trestle and a power supply, and the company is not the letting agent and has no connection to the room off the service road except that both numbers belong to a group that has an office about eleven miles away and a person in it who signs things.

A first-year of nineteen wrote to that company in the second week of week sixty-eight. She wrote a letter of one paragraph and she did not sign it with a room or a name, and the company said yes in about eleven days, and the answer came back on a piece of paper with a job title on it and no name, and the piece of paper is in a folder in a room off a service road and it is a different object from a lease and from a card in a rail and this chapter does not put any two of them in a sentence.

**The name at the bottom of the leaflet is Asha Reed's, in her own hand, because she put it there on purpose, on the Tuesday, having been told twice by two other people that a description of a room is a description of the people in it.**

She did not consult anybody. She wrote to the woman of thirty-four with the clipboard on the Monday morning and said one sentence — *you are right about the sentence and I am putting my name under it anyway, and if that is stupid then it is on me and not on the room* — and the woman of thirty-four read it and did not reply and has not mentioned it since.

Nobody can take it off. There is no form on which to take a name off a leaflet and there is no mechanism in this city that can do it, which is the same sentence that has been said about a card in a rail and about about four hundred and thirty-two other objects, and the man who said it most recently is a woman of twenty-four and she is nine feet away.

---

**He was not a speaker and nobody introduced him as one.**

He was at the depot until about half past two on grade two, and he came to the market at about ten to three with a bag of cable ties in it because the bag was in the van and the van was parked in a market car park, and Asha Reed was on the end of the trestle with a flask and a first-year of nineteen's way of standing, which is with both feet, and Dessa Kwan was behind the trestle with a plate of something cut into nine pieces because there is a health rule about a plate and she has never broken one in her life.

**A woman of thirty-four with a district jacket and a clipboard had put the trestle there.** She is a district nurse and her name is Sinead Corrigan and she is not a post and she is not on any list of anybody and she told two people at the end of that table that she had put it there and had not told anybody why, and the reason she gave was one sentence: *the people who need a room like that are the people who are already standing in a market with a bag, and none of them is going to be told about a room off a service road.*

She has a card in a plastic wallet with eleven names on it, and she has never once given that card to anybody, and she was on the end of a table for about two hours and turned about four people away from a leaflet by saying one thing to each of them, which was that she was busy until four.

---

A woman of about fifty-two came at about half past three with a carrier bag of fish and no intention of stopping, and she stopped because there was a plate of something cut into nine pieces on a trestle and a young woman saying you can have one if you want one, and about half the people in this market take the free thing and do not stop.

"You've got a room," she said, to Asha Reed, about nine minutes later, with a piece of something in a paper napkin.

"We have."

"My sister was in a room," said the woman of about fifty-two. "**Not that one. A different one. And she was told to be quiet in it and she was quiet in it, and about a year after that I found out that what she had been quiet about had been written down, and the writing down was done by a woman who was being kind to her, and the woman who was being kind to her has still got the file.**"

Asha Reed did not say anything to that and did not look at anybody, and the microphone on its stand was four feet away with a lead in it, and the amplifier was on, and the lead was in.

**What happened next took about eleven seconds and the woman of thirty-four with the clipboard was standing at the end of the table and heard every word of it and has never written any of it down.**

"Who is that on the bottom of the paper."

"That is me."

"Then can I ask him."

"You can ask anybody you like," said Asha Reed, "**and he is the one holding the bag and he is not on this table and he has not got anything to do with this table, and that is on purpose.**"

---

He was nine feet away with a bag of cable ties in it and he came over because somebody said a thing and he has never in his life not come over.

"I am not — " he started.

"**Is it true that what people say in that room stays in that room.**"

---

He could have said a great deal. He had a term and a half of it in his head and a card in a rail with his name on it and about nine hundred doors behind it, and there are three lines in a specification that eleven thousand premises pay for, and a rule in a public room three shelves down from a folder on a high shelf that nobody has ever withdrawn, and a woman's name in a box in pencil four miles away that cannot come out.

**And what he said was nine words, and the nine words were true, and the nine words did not answer her, and he has never found a way to make them do both.**

"Nobody in that room can promise you that today."

---

She waited. She is a woman of about fifty-two who has been in a market for about four seconds longer than she meant to be, and she waited about nine seconds, and she is not a fool and she had heard the shape of the sentence.

"That is not a no."

"No," he said. "**It is a woman of twenty-four in a corridor saying that a page nobody may read is a page that dies with the person, and about nine days of a room that has four chairs in it and a ring binder with two things in it.**"

"And who takes it out."

"**Nobody. That is the answer to the second half and I have said it in about nine rooms in a term and a half and nobody has ever asked me the first half, which is that a room with four chairs in it is the smallest room anybody has ever built for this and it is not going to be the one that fixes it.**"

---

And then the thing came, four feet away, in the space where the microphone stand was, in the two seconds between one sentence and the next one, and it was not a voice and it was not anybody.

* * *

> The condition stops you doing the thing.
> It does not stop anybody asking you about it.

---

Nothing followed it. He had heard it five times in two hundred chapters and it had never once told him anything, and this was the sixth and it was the only one that was in a market.

It was true. It was exactly true and it was the whole of the standing problem in two lines, and it was the worst thing that had happened to him in two hundred chapters, and the reason is that it was a rule and a rule is the one thing in this case that nobody in this city has ever been able to ask a question about.

**And he looked at it. He looked at the rule instead of at her face, and the reason he looked at it is that the rule was true and it was the most useful sentence anybody had put in front of him in about eleven days, and the woman of fifty-two was four feet away and had stopped talking and had her hands on a paper napkin.**

---

**That is the thing that cost and it is the whole of what the movement's private price is, and it is not a new thing about him and it is the first time it has happened to a person who was not on a floor at ten past four in the morning.**

In his first year at the institute, in the autumn, he did not hear a person in a corridor and it cost the case a fact, and about three terms ago he talked over three people in three rooms in a fortnight and failed to hear one, and the one he failed to hear is the reason a thing in this city happens, and he wrote down in his own book that he is not a reliable witness to his own ears, and nobody has argued with him about it, and on the Monday of week seventy in a canteen off a service road he had been thinking about it for a term and a half.

The woman of fifty-two said one more sentence and he did not hear it.

"Sorry," he said.

"It does not matter," she said, and then she said it again, once, and slower, which is a thing about her and not about him, and this is what she said:

"**There is a woman in this city who has been waiting since the spring because nobody told her that a man could refuse to come, and I am not going to say her name to you in a market and I have not said it to the girl on your table either.**"

---

Talia Venn was nine feet away and had been for about eleven minutes and she did not say anything, and the reason she did not is not forgiveness and it is not coldness, and she gave it to him afterwards on a stair, in one sentence, and she gave it once.

"I could have stepped in," she said. "**Somebody in front of forty people would have stepped in. And then the thing that went out of that table would have been mine, and it would have been a nurse saying that a room is careful, and a nurse saying that a room is careful is worth about half of a cook saying it, and I am not spending mine on about half.**"

"You could have said something smaller."

"**I could have said something smaller and you would have looked at me instead of at the rule, and then in about four years somebody would be able to say that the woman who made a room careful was a woman who is in a corridor with him, and that is the one sentence I have spent four years not saying in a room.**"

---

Nobody took anything off the trestle and the amplifier went off at about four because the socket is on a pillar and somebody else's kettle went on, and about forty people went past that table in the hour and about eleven of them stopped and four of the eleven were spoken to by the woman of thirty-four and seven of the eleven ate a piece of something and went on their way without saying anything.

**A man of fifty-eight with a flat cap stopped at about ten to four, having gone past that table about nine times in four years and never stopped, and he looked at the leaflet and at the name at the bottom of it and at the man of twenty-two standing nine feet away with a bag of cable ties.**

"**Right," he said, out loud, to nobody. "**That is a sentence somebody has already printed."

He did not come back to the room that week and he comes back in about nine weeks with something different, and the timetable went into his inside pocket in the market and not on the step of a room off a service road.
