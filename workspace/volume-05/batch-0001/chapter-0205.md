# Chapter 205 — Four Names On A Sheet

On the Monday morning of week sixty-nine a woman of thirty-nine with a ring file put a lease on the counter of a converted shop unit off a service road in the Lower Wards and read nine clauses out of it in about nine minutes, and then she put her finger on the fourth one and asked whether they were going to sign it, and that was the whole of the reason she had come.

---

"Seven of these are about what happens if you use it for something the freeholder does not like, and I am going to read you the sentences and not the reasons."

"You said that on Monday."

"**I did, and I have thought about it for four days and I am going to do it anyway, because four of you have now been in that room twice and one of you has a ring binder with about nine sheets in it, and I am not going to be the person who finds out in a year what is going on in a building I opened.**"

She read the seven sentences. They were ordinary and every one of them was true: no commercial cooking without a ventilation certificate, no alteration of the frontage, no subletting, no signage above the transom, no storage of anything heavier than a person on the second floor, no opening before seven or after ten, and the last of the seven, which was that the person letting has no obligation to any of them for anything that happened to any of them in the room.

"Numbers eight and nine are the ones I have not read to anybody."

"Read them."

"Eight says the term is a year and the rent is nil for the first year and there is no second-year figure on this page. **That is not an omission. I have looked for the second-year figure on four copies of this and it is not on any of them, and it is not on the schedule and it is not in the file, and I asked a manager about it in the spring and he said he would find out and I have not heard.**"

"And nine?"

"Nine says that if four people sign this, the four people are the four people, and it does not say what happens if one of the four of you stops." She put the page down. "There is no clause nine(b). I looked. **I have read that clause to four sets of people and every single one of them has asked me about clause eight and not one of them has asked me about clause nine, and the one who is going to ask me about clause nine is the one who has been in the room twice and has not written anything down.**"

---

**They asked her about the rent four times in about nine minutes and she gave the same true sentence four times, and the fifth time she gave a different one.**

"The first year is at no rent."

"Because the frontage has been empty."

"It has been empty four years, not eleven. The eleven years is this side of the row."

"**The frontage has been empty.**"

"You've said that four times."

"**I have, and I am going to say it a fifth time because you have now asked me four times and there is a version of this conversation where I stop being exact, and I am not going to be that person on a Monday morning in the fourth month of a tenancy.**"

She put her hand flat on the lease.

"**Here is the fifth version, and it is the one I have, and it is not the whole of it. I have a ring file and about four years of units in it and this one is a void and the void has been on a list every week since the spring before last, and a void on a list every week is a thing that a person above me can see, and I have never been told to do anything about it and I have never asked, because the last time I asked a question about a void a manager explained to me for about nine minutes why a question about a void is a problem for the person who wrote it and not for the person who asked it.**"

"Then you do not know why it is free."

"**I know why it is free and it is not a reason. It is free because it has been empty and because a woman in an office I have never been in looks at a column on a Friday.**"

---

Then they signed it, and it took about four minutes, and it is the first document in this case with four names on it.

**Dessa Kwan signed first and gave the reason before she signed, unprompted, and the reason is about a business.**

"My work is registered to a kitchen in Saltmarket and if I put this address on a lease then in about four years the two are the same address to a licensing man with a clipboard, and I will not be able to cook somewhere without the room being the place I cook. I have had that before. I am signing anyway."

"**I am signing a lease with a home address on it for a certificate that takes me two evenings a week, and the certificate is about how to be difficult on behalf of somebody, and I have spent four years making sure that nothing anybody could look up has my name at the top of it, and I am putting my name on a document about a building. I am signing it because the alternative is that the four of us are two people and a woman of nineteen and a cook, and a room with two names on it is a man's room.**"

"Asha."

"**I am nineteen and it is the first thing I have ever signed that has an address on it, and my college does not know I am in it, and I have been careful for two years and I am going to be careful for about a term and a half and then I am going to have to stop being careful, and I would rather stop in a room with a bell-push in it than in a common room with a kettle in it.**"

That left him, and he did not do it quickly, and the woman of thirty-nine with the ring file watched him do it.

"Go on," she said. "You have got a face on you and I have read four leases this month."

"**A card went into a rail in a room off a street in Lattice Ward on the Thursday of the week before last and it has my name on it and a week at the bottom left and nothing on the back of it. That was eight days ago. This is an address and a landlord's name and four dates in a row, and a lease is a document anybody can hold, and I put the card in myself and I said the reason out loud in a room with five people in it and an officer of a fund of forty-four told me the reason does not help.**"

"Does it help?"

"**No. I am signing it.**"

Then everybody signed and the woman of thirty-nine dated it and initialled the corner and said that a year from that day somebody would have to look at it again and that she would not necessarily be the one, and that if she was not the one it would be a woman with a ring file and a coat with a company's name off it and that this was a good thing and not a comfort, and she left before anybody could thank her.

---

**That is the whole of what happened on the Monday afternoon of week sixty-nine and he and she left at about half past three and walked to the service road and he said, out loud, on a pavement, four words.**

"That is a mistake."

"That is a signature," said Talia Venn. "**You have signed a document with an address on it and you are correct that it is a mistake, and I have signed one too, and the reason we both signed it is that a room with two names on it is not a room. So the arithmetic is not four names, it is one name, and you have been doing that arithmetic since the spring before last and it is the only arithmetic you have ever been sure of.**"

"Then somebody else should have to be findable as well."

"**Yes. And they are. I have just signed. You have just watched me.**"

He did not have an answer to that and he said so, which is a thing he has done four times in five volumes and about nine times in this one.

---

At ten at night in a repair shop in Lattice Ward the docket had three job numbers on it and the word was at nine all afternoon and at ten by the time he sat down, and the lease was in the folder on the counter, signed four times, and he took it out and put it back twice and did not write anything about it on the page.

*208. Monday of week 69, ten at night in a repair shop in Lattice Ward, and this entry is a lease, nine clauses, a void on a weekly list, and four names on one sheet.*

*The lease, as an object and not as an event.* **A year. Nil rent for the first year. Seven ordinary clauses about use, all of them true: no commercial cooking without a ventilation certificate, no alteration to the frontage, no subletting, no signage above the transom, nothing heavier than a person stored on the second floor, no opening before seven or after ten, and one that the letting clerk read out in the flat voice she uses for everything, which is that the person letting has no obligation to any of them for anything that happened to any of them in the room. Two clauses she had not read to anybody: the absence of a second-year figure from the page, the schedule and the file, and a ninth that says that if four people sign then the four people are the four people and does not say what happens if one of them stops. **There is no clause nine(b) and she looked.** The freehold is an entry on a schedule of title. Nobody in this entry can describe the person on the other side of the entry on the schedule, she has never met them, and the volume does not go looking.**

*The reason for the nil rent, in the only form it was given in.* **A sentence about a vacant frontage, given four times and not improved. On the fifth asking, a different sentence, which is the whole of what she has: the unit has been a void for four years, a void has been on a weekly list since the spring before last, a void on a list is visible to somebody above her, she has never been told to do anything about it and has never asked, and the reason she has never asked is that the last time she asked, a manager explained to her for about nine minutes that a question about a void is a problem for whoever wrote it and not for whoever asked it. The rent is free because the unit is empty, and the unit is empty, and the person who sets that in motion is a woman in an office she has never been in, looking at a column on a Friday.** **The four years of this unit and the eleven years of the frontage on one side of the row are two different numbers and this entry does not merge them.**

*Four names, and four costs, each in the signatory's own words before signing.* **A cook of twenty-seven, whose registered work address and this address becoming one address to a licensing man with a clipboard is the cost, and who named the risk and signed. A nurse of twenty-four, for whom a home address on a lease attached to a part-time certificate is four years of making sure nothing anybody can look up has her name at the top of it, and who gave as her own reason that a room with two names on it is a man's room. A first-year of nineteen, for whom it is the first thing she has signed with an address on it, out of two years of being careful, and who said she would rather stop being careful in a room with a bell-push in it than in a common room with a kettle in it. And a man of twenty-two, who named the card in the rail, eight days old, and the second document in four days with his name and an address on it, and who said the reason the card's reason does not help, and who said the word *mistake* about four times on a pavement afterwards to a woman who told him the arithmetic was one name and not four and then told him it was her signature that had proved it.** Nobody thanked the woman with the ring file and she left before anybody could.

*What the entry deliberately does not contain.* **No register, no minute, no notice, no form, no committee, no lease register and no entry anywhere of who holds this tenancy — the document exists twice, in the letting agent's file and in a folder on a counter in the Lower Wards, and the clerk said in four years somebody would have to look at it again and that she might not be the one. No key and no keyholder: that is Chapter 206. No board and no sign-writer. No name over the door, which is the thing four people refused to do and did not know they were refusing. No share, no relay, no practice, and nobody taught, demonstrated, assessed or assisted anything by anybody. No public argument and no leaflet, and the leaflet from the Thursday is still in the folder face up where he left it. The box in pencil in Bower Street is not referred to and was not touched and still has one name in it that cannot come out. Entry 208.**
