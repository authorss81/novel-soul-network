# Chapter 202 — Sixth Wednesday

There is a back room with the shutters up and a table in it and a tin on the table with no lock on it, and a book with four columns and forty-four lines in it, and eight chairs round the table and a ninth one standing against the wall with its back to the room.

On the Wednesday morning of week sixty-eight, ten people were in that room and the tin was not opened and the book was not opened and neither of those was news.

---

Bea Nunn is sixty-one. She has run that room for nineteen years, longer than the shutters have been painted, and she puts a hand on the tin when a person is talking and takes it off when they stop, and she has never once been asked to do that and she has never once said that she does it.

"Thirty-one," she said, at about half past ten.

Nobody had asked, and that was the arrangement, and the arrangement was nineteen years old.

"It was twenty-nine four weeks ago and I have said that number out loud in this room every four weeks for a term and a half and nobody has written it down anywhere, and two of them rang me in between. **Both of them did not come. And I have not telephoned anybody, and I am not going to, and the reason has not changed in four years, which is that the day I start ringing people to come to a room in here, this room is a woman on a telephone instead of a room, and a woman on a telephone can be asked where a room is.**"

"Who rang you?" said a man of about thirty.

"That is the first question and it is the wrong one and I am going to answer it anyway, because the other answer is that it is nobody's business, and it is not, and I have found that out the hard way." She put her hand flat on the tin. "**They rang and I said come on a Wednesday, and they said they would think about it, and that is what people say, and I have not rung either of them, and if I ring them then in about four years the answer to *why did nobody come* is *because a woman rang them twice*, and that answer would be true and it would be the whole of it.**"

---

There are two items, and there are four minutes an item, and a man gets a wall clock and not a stopwatch, and the clock is a clock because the person running it does not have to be able to see the back of the room to know when four minutes have gone.

The first item was a man of forty-four who wanted to know whether a thing he had been told about in a corridor existed, and Bea Nunn read a card out of the catalogue rather than the book, and the card had a year on it and nothing else on it, and the year was not the year he wanted.

"That is a card for a box. The box is not here and I did not say it was."

"Right."

"You said you had been told about it in a corridor."

"I said I had been told about it."

"**Then I have told you the same thing you were told and no more, and I have done that to about four hundred people in nineteen years, and in about four years somebody is going to want to know which of the four hundred of us was the first to say a thing in a corridor, and I am not going to be able to help with that, and neither will you.**"

---

The second item was a woman of about thirty who wanted to know whether a person who telephoned her in the spring could be found, and Bea Nunn said no, and gave the reason in one sentence, and then gave the other half of the reason because a clerk of fifty-three in a different building had taught her in the spring that a person will forgive a no and will not forgive a thing that is merely true.

"**A page that never went into a system has nothing in it that could answer. It is in a stack of about two hundred on a counter in another building and there is no number on it and no copy and no copy of the copy, and the person whose name is in it is the only person who could put it right, and she cannot, and neither can I, and neither can the man who wrote it.**"

She put her hand on the tin and took it off.

"That is the shape I say it in and I have said it in that shape for a term and a half, and every time it has come out the same length, and the reason it comes out the same length is that the answer has not changed. **What I have not said, because I have not been asked it, is that the reason I keep saying it in exactly the same shape is so that nobody in this room can build a better answer out of the two of us having a conversation about it.**"

---

And then the young woman on the floor said it.

She had been sitting on the floor since about ten because all eight chairs were taken and the ninth one is against the wall with its back to the room and has been since she put it there herself, and nobody has moved it and there is no rule about it.

She is nineteen. She is in the first year of a maintenance-track course and she has organised a low-cost practice study group for two years in a common room with a kettle, and she has a folder under her arm that she did not open. Her name is Asha Reed. She has been coming to that room since she was fifteen and the woman of sixty-one, who has run it for nineteen years, has never once used her name in it and has never once been asked to.

"**You have a room, and you speak for us.**"

Bea Nunn looked at her for about four seconds and did not defend herself, and Marek Senn, who was in that room and had been in nine of them, understood inside about a second that he was not going to be asked anything and that the sentence was not aimed at the woman of sixty-one.

---

It was aimed at him.

"Go on," she said.

"I do not mean you are doing it. I mean you have a room, and it is yours, and in about a month somebody is going to ask you what is in it, and you are going to say it, and you will say it well." She shifted on the floor. "**And then it is a room with you in front of it, and everybody in it is a person you said it for, and nobody in it chose that, and in about four years somebody is going to write down that the first person who spoke for the Practice Commons was a man of twenty-two who never had a Solo Seal.**"

"He has not got one," said a man of about thirty.

"He has not got one," she agreed. "**That is not why I said it.**"

---

Somebody asked her which part was the important bit and she gave an answer that Bea Nunn said out loud afterwards was the best sentence said in that room in a term and a half, and the answer had four parts and none of them was clever.

"The bit that matters is the order. **Nobody in a room has to say anything to be described by the person who has the room. That is the whole of it. You do not have to do anything to be in it. You turn up, and somebody else does the describing, and in about four years the describing is the only part anybody can remember, and about nine of the people it describes will not have been in the room on the day.**"

"**Nine,**" said Bea Nunn, "**is not a number I would use in this room and I am going to use it anyway, because that girl has just said nine out loud in front of eleven people and I am sixty-one and I have been counting the calls on a piece of paper in a drawer since the spring.**"

Then she said the thing she says in this room, in one go, and she did not shorten it and she did not explain it, and it has been said at every one of these sittings for four years.

"**A book of what a caller said cannot be written by the people who did not call.**"

---

Nobody wrote it down. There are ten people in that room and none of them has a notebook, and there is a form in a drawer in another building that any of them could have asked for, and nobody has ever asked.

At about eleven the shutters came up an inch because the room was hot, and Bea Nunn said that was the signal and the signal was a hand on the shutter, and the tin went back in the drawer and the drawer has no lock on it and never has.

---

At ten at night in a repair shop in Lattice Ward the docket had two job numbers on it and the word had been at ten since about two.

He wrote two lines and they were the only two lines in that book that week and both of them are about other people.

*205. Wednesday of week 68, ten at night in a repair shop in Lattice Ward, and this entry is a sixth Wednesday, a number that has gone up by two, and nine words said by a person of nineteen sitting on a floor.*

*The run of sittings, counted the way it has been counted since week 52.* **Six sittings at which the book has not been opened, on the Wednesdays of weeks forty-eight, fifty-two, fifty-six, sixty, sixty-four and sixty-eight. That is six sittings and five Wednesdays in between them and five gaps of four weeks. The fortieth line is three years old and was put there by a man who rang, said nine words and signed his own name under them, and there has been no line since. Line forty-four is a gap in the shape of a woman of sixty-eight out of bay four, and she is never going to be written down. The tin is not locked. There are eight chairs round the table and a ninth against the wall with its back to the room, put there by hand.**

*The telephone number, as a change and not as a number.* **Twenty-nine on the Wednesday of week sixty-four, thirty-one on this Wednesday, two calls in four weeks, and neither call came to the room. She has not telephoned anybody. Her reason, given in her own construction: the day she starts ringing people to ask them to come to a room in there, the room is a woman on a telephone, and a woman on a telephone can be asked where a room is. She offered the name of neither caller and gave the reason for that offer as well, and the reason was that the answer to *why did nobody come* would then be *because a woman rang them twice*, which would be true and would be the whole of it. Nine is the only other figure that was said in the room, it was said by the young woman and not by the woman of sixty-one, and it is not counted here and it is counted there.**

*The floor, as an object.* **A first-year of nineteen, first year of a maintenance-track course, a folder not opened, a study group of two years in a common room with a kettle, sitting on a floor because the eight chairs were taken. Nine words, counted on the page: *You have a room, and you speak for us.* Her own account of why the nine words were the right nine, in four parts and in order: nobody in a room has to say anything to be described by the person who has the room; turning up is enough; in about four years the describing is the only part anybody can remember; and about nine of the people it describes were not in the room on the day. The woman of sixty-one said that the number was the best sentence in that room in a term and a half and then said that nine was not a number she would use in that room and used it anyway. Both of those are hers and neither of them is a rule.**

*What the entry deliberately does not contain.* **No caller, no name of a caller, no new line, and the book does not go to forty-five — it goes to forty-five in a later movement of this volume and the only thing that has ever grown it is a caller who came. No answer to the ninth question, which is a yes that is a sentence: nobody in a room has to say anything to be described by the person who has the room. No resolution of it and no rule proposed by anybody in that room. No share, no relay, no practice, and nobody taught, demonstrated, assessed or assisted anything. The card in a rail went in on the Thursday of week 67, six days ago, and is not mentioned. The fourth condition is not quoted and is not paraphrased. Asha Reed's own sentence is retired and was not said and does not appear in this entry, and the nine words above are not it and are not in the same shape. Entry 205.**
