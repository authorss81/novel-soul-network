# Chapter 203 — Four Lists

The second room of a shop unit off a service road in the Lower Wards has one window and the window faces a wall about four feet away, and the light in it comes on at about half past four in the afternoon and goes off at about half past seven, and on the Thursday morning of week sixty-eight four people sat in it with a ring binder and about nine sheets of paper and argued for two hours and forty minutes.

Two of the four had not been in that room at the same time before and one of those two was the reason the other three were there.

---

Talia Venn came at about eleven with her bag and a folder of her own and a consent clipboard that no institution has ever asked her for.

She is twenty-four. She works out of a mobile clinic and is part way through a certificate in this city that is about how to be difficult on somebody's behalf, and she is the only one of the four who has ever ruled a box on a form for another person and then been told, twice, that she has no standing.

"Right," she said. "**If we are doing this, I want it written down that I am the fourth person and not the first one, because if three people arrive in a room and a fourth turns up, the fourth one is the one everybody listens to, and that is not a thing about me, it is a thing about rooms.**"

"Noted," said Marek Senn, who had no intention of noting it and who reached for the pen.

"**Do not note it. Note it on Thursday.**"

---

The four lists are these and they are given here in the order they were made and not in the order anybody would have chosen.

**Dessa Kwan's list has eleven things on it and they are the eleven things she cooks.**

A pot of water that comes to the boil and does not go past it. A hot plate with a mark on it that means off. Bread. A light left on. A chair somebody can sit down in. A knife that is sharp and a board that is not. Salt, and knowing which kind. A window open an inch. A towel that is dry. Somebody standing in a doorway who is not asking you anything. And, last, in a different hand because she asked somebody else to write it, a person's own name, spelled the way they spell it.

"That is the work," she said. "**That is the work I have been doing for nine years and nobody has ever put it in a room with anybody else's work, and every one of those eleven is a thing you can be taught badly and got wrong, and about four of them will hurt somebody if you get them wrong.**"

"Somebody wrote the last one."

"**Somebody wrote the last one because I cannot spell it and it is my sister's and it goes on the wall and not in my mouth.**"

---

**Asha Reed's list has four things on it and they are the four things that will not be called a technique in that room.**

A cure. A promise. A result. An improvement.

"**If somebody walks in there and says I have got better at this, I want somebody to be able to say no, in the room, out loud, and I do not care who says it.**"

"Somebody will be able to say no."

"Somebody will say no and then in about four years somebody will write that the Practice Commons does not permit a claim of improvement, and it will be on a prospectus, and it will be the only true sentence on it." She put the pen down. "And the first two are worse than the last two. **A cure and a promise are the two things that people come into rooms for and I have watched both of them take about nine months off somebody's life.**"

---

**Talia Venn's list is about what the room has to refuse to do and she wrote it last and in a hand that went smaller towards the end of it, and it has five things on it.**

Hold anything it cannot take back. Put a person's name on the front of anything. Answer a question about a person who is in it. Be the only place a person has to come. Keep anything overnight.

"Keep anything overnight," said Asha Reed.

"**A thing left in a room overnight has a morning in it, and a morning is when somebody decides what it was for.**"

---

**Marek Senn's list has one line on it and it is the shortest of the four and it is the one everybody in that room argued about for the longest.**

*A person runs a practice, with a boundary, and a fourth step, and somebody watches the fourth step happen.*

He read it out and then said the part he had been carrying up the service road in his head all week, which is that this was a practice and therefore the fourth condition was in force in it, and he said the condition out loud, whole, in the order it has to be said in, because a man of twenty-four had corrected him about paraphrasing it in a corridor a term ago.

"There is a condition on my share. I may not demonstrate, teach, assess or assist at any practice in this department, on its premises, under its insurance, at any time, unlimited by hours, and the department may put four names in front of any of them."

Nobody said anything.

"**So I cannot do that line. Not in that room, not on a Saturday, not with the door shut. I cannot teach it and I cannot watch somebody do it and say whether it was right, and if I stand at the front of that room and show four people how to put a boundary in, I am doing the thing the condition exists to stop, and the reason the condition exists is a boy called Tam Arrick with two tendons cut in a forearm who is still not able to use a middle finger, and he is not a story I use, he is a person who is alive and has a kitchen.**"

"Then who runs it," said Asha Reed.

"**Somebody else. Somebody who can be asked whether they are competent and who can lose something when the answer is no. And there is nobody in this room like that, and there are about four people in this city, and not one of them is going to walk into a room off a service road for nothing.**"

---

That was the middle of the two hours and forty minutes. The rest of it was worse and quieter and it was about a leaflet.

He had a page of it in his own handwriting, in the folder he had brought and not offered, and he had had it since the Sunday, and he put it on the table face up and said that the room could not be a secret, because a secret room in this city is a room that only works for people who already know about it, and about eleven people a week in this city are being taught to wait for a man in a lanyard and a room that nobody hears about is one more of those eleven.

He wanted a morning, a trestle, a leaflet, and about forty people past it in an hour. He wanted it in a market, because the market is where a man of fifty-one had been paid six times to say a true sentence into a microphone and it is the only place in this case where a room has ever been told something by a stranger.

"Read it out," said Talia Venn.

He read it out.

"**A room off a service road in the Lower Wards where people can be shown how to do a thing properly, by people who do it, and who are paid nothing for showing you.**"

"That is fine," she said. "**That is a true sentence and every word of it is in order, and you have just described a room and four people and the fact that they are not paid, and you have not described one single person who would walk into it.**"

"It is a room anybody can walk into."

"**It is a room any person can walk into, and a room is described in those words, and the description is a description of the people in it, and you have not got round it by not putting a name on the front of the leaflet. You have got round it by putting a sentence on the front of the leaflet that says anybody can walk into, and in about four years that sentence is the only thing anybody has got, and about nine of the people it describes will not have been in the room on the day.**"

"That is a first-year's argument," he said, "and it is a good one, and I heard it first on a Wednesday on a floor, and you are both saying the same thing and neither of you is wrong."

"I am not saying the same thing as a first-year of nineteen," said Talia Venn. "**I am saying the same thing as a woman of thirty-one with a diary who put a box in pencil on a form in a counter in Bower Street, and who was told twice that she had no standing, and who did it anyway, and the whole of what she has for it is a pencil and about two hundred pages in four stacks and a person who cannot be ill.**"

---

They did not settle it. That is what happened in the two hours and forty minutes and it is worth being exact about, because the alternative is a lie about who was in the room.

**He did not drop the leaflet. He said she was right about the sentence and wrong about the conclusion, and he said that a room nobody can describe is a room that only works for people who already know about it, and she said that was true and that it was the reason for doing it carefully and not the reason for doing it badly, and he said there was no version of careful that also got out of the door, and she said there was, and that it was slower, and that he had not tried it, and that she had watched four years of people not try it.**

Nobody took a vote. Nobody wrote down a decision. Asha Reed wrote down four lists and that was all she wrote, and when he asked her at about half past one whether the lists were the room's or hers, she said they were hers and she would let anybody take them off the wall, and that was the end of that and it was the correct answer.

---

There were four job numbers on the docket when he got to the bench in Lattice Ward at ten that night, and the count that comes with them had not moved all afternoon and was a higher number by the time he sat down.

The master was in the inside pocket of a jacket on the back of a chair and it was in the inside pocket of it on Friday and on Saturday and on Sunday, and the original was in the fourth pocket of the tool roll with a fifth line on it that nobody has ever asked him to fill in, and the decision about that is unmade after a term and a half and was not made tonight, and the leaflet was in the folder on the counter, face up, where he had left it, and he did not put it away and he did not put it out again.

*206. Thursday of week 68, ten at night in a repair shop in Lattice Ward, and this entry is four lists on nine sheets of paper, and a leaflet with one sentence on it that has not gone in a market.*

*The four lists, as objects and not as positions.* **One is eleven items in a cook's hand with the eleventh in a second hand, and the eleven are a pot of water brought to the boil and not past it, a hot plate with a mark that means off, bread, a light left on, a chair somebody can sit down in, a sharp knife and an unsharp board, salt and which kind, a window open an inch, a dry towel, a person in a doorway not asking you anything, and a name spelled as its owner spells it. One is four items: a cure, a promise, a result, an improvement. One is five items: it does not hold anything it cannot take back; it does not put a name on the front of anything; it does not answer a question about a person in it; it is not the only place a person has to come; and it does not keep anything overnight. The fourth is one line, and it is about a person running a practice with a boundary and a fourth step and somebody watching the fourth step happen, and it cannot be done by the man who wrote it and he said so out loud in about nine seconds and gave the reason.**

*On the writing.* **Four lists, about nine sheets, and one ring binder. The first writing in that room since it was a room, and it was done four days after a letting clerk of thirty-nine stopped a pen with a sentence about a page being able to be carried, and the argument for it was made by a woman of twenty-seven and against it by nobody, and the person who put the pen in her hand was a first-year of nineteen and she wrote her own four and the cook's eleven and the nurse's five and she did not write the fourth, because the fourth was one line and the man who wrote it had read it out loud and could remember it.** No decision was written down. No vote was taken and nobody asked for one.

*On the condition.* **The whole of it, in the order it has to be said in, said out loud once in this movement by the man it is on, in about nine seconds, and it was not paraphrased anywhere and it is not paraphrased here. It is the only time in this case that the whole of it has been said out loud by the man it is on, and the first time it has been said in a room with a door and a bell-push in it. The sentence that followed it, in his words: he cannot teach it and he cannot watch somebody do it and say whether it was right, and if he stands at the front of that room and shows four people how to put a boundary in, he is doing the thing the condition exists to stop, and the reason the condition exists is a man of twenty whose forearm has two cut tendons in it and who is not a story and is a person who is alive and has a kitchen. No names beyond that one and no consent is recorded in this entry and none was asked for, and the man is not in this room.**

*The leaflet, as a single sentence, and what was said about it.* **One line, in his own hand, in a folder he brought on the Thursday and did not offer until half past one. The sentence is a description of a room and of four people and of the fact that they are not paid, and it contains no name and no technique. The woman of twenty-four's answer, in about nine words of the argument and not the answer: a description in public is a description of the people in it, and the sentence is a description of the people in it, and he has not got round that by not putting a name on the front. The first-year's version of the same objection was made the evening before on a floor in a room with the shutters up; it is on entry 205 and is not restated here. The exchange between the two of them is the first private friction of this volume, is about public testimony, and is not resolved: he did not drop the leaflet, she did not agree to it, and neither of them said one word about anything that happened under a building in Crown Terrace two weeks ago.**

*What the entry deliberately does not contain.* **No share, no relay, no practice, and nobody demonstrated, taught, assessed or assisted anything — the one line on the fourth list is a thing that cannot be done in that room by the man who wrote it and that is the chapter's finding and not a failure. No standing problem and no number attached to one. No name over the door and no board and no sign-writer: those are Chapter 206 and 210. No lease and no clause: that is Chapter 205. No key and no keyholder. No decision, no vote, no form, no minute, and no agreement of any kind between four people other than that four people were in a room. The words *we*, *our* and *us* are in this entry inside a sentence about a public leaflet and not inside a sentence about two people, which is the line Volume 05 walks. Entry 206.**
