# Chapter 806 — A Monday, A Bench Until Two And A Counter Until About Six, And A Second Date At The Foot Of That District's Own Notice Which Arrived And Was The Day The District Went, And A Room Off A Road Where A Man Of About Thirty-Four Said That The Date At The Top Had Come And Gone In The Spring And That A Date Which Has Already Proved It Means Nothing Is Not Going To Mean Anything This Time Either

**A Monday began at a bench, went through a counter, and finished in a room off a road in a second district, on a day when the second date at the foot of that district's own notice arrived and the district went. Four hundred people went with it. Nobody has said so in a sentence and nobody has said it in a speech.**

---

The board for that Monday carried fourteen names and fourteen dates, and the fourteenth name was entered at ten to five. The shutter came down at ten on that Monday, at the hour it comes down at on every day of this stretch of days except one.

**Those four rooms and one behind the other three stand at one thousand four hundred and seventy days, two hundred and ten weeks to the day. Line sixteen is one thousand two hundred and sixty days, a hundred and eighty weeks to the day, and the nine hand copies of the front of one page are one thousand and thirty-six days, a hundred and forty-eight weeks to the day.**

---

**The date at the top of that notice came and went on a Tuesday in the spring and the district did not go and nobody wrote down what that meant. The date at the foot of it was added afterwards in the district's own hand. On that Monday it arrived.**

Nobody in that district had been asked about it and four hundred people in that district had read it. Nobody has asked anybody what they thought would happen and about nine of them have been asked since and have given nine different answers and two of those nine answers agree with each other.

**The gate of the yard that opens at a weekend opened at five that morning instead of at six, and the yard was swept at six instead of at seven, and the bus was let out at its own hour and not four minutes late.**

---

**And the room off that road was open on that Monday from about five, and nobody put a figure on anything, and about nine people came and about four of them stayed, and the first thing said in that room was not about the district.**

A man of about thirty-four said in nine seconds that the date at the top of that notice came and went in the spring and nothing happened. He said that a date which has already proved it means nothing is not going to mean anything this time either.

He said that he was not saying it to score a point. He said that he was saying it because he had been in that room for a fortnight waiting for somebody to say it, and that four people in it had been waiting for it, and that four had been hoping somebody else would.

He said that if nobody says the true thing about a date that has already failed, then every date after it is treated as a formality, and that a formality is what a person is allowed to ignore.

---

**And a woman of about thirty-four said in nine seconds that the difference was that somebody had been told in advance, and that being told in advance is the only thing that has changed, and that it changed on the Friday of that week at about ten past six in a morning, by a person walking past.**

She said that no such thing had happened before the spring date. She said that on the spring date four hundred people in that district woke up on a Tuesday and found out, and that the whole of what they did about it was nothing, and that this is not a criticism of them.

She said that this time one man was told three days before, by a woman who keeps a haulier's books and who was not asked and who was not thanked, and that that man repeated the date correctly once and has not been asked since.

A man of about thirty-eight said that one man is not four hundred people, and that she had said that herself at some point in a fortnight, and that she had said it in a room and not in a gateway.

**She said that a man who has been told a date he cannot hold will repeat it, and that a man who repeats a date is a man who has told somebody, and that nobody in this city has asked him who and that is the correct arrangement and not a gap in it.**

---

**And the district went on that Monday, and the four hundred people went with it, and about nine people who had been part of a network were not in this city and had not been asked about anything, and nothing has been asked of them about this either.**

Nobody in that room said the word went out loud until about half past five. Two people have said since that this was a thing about the word and not about the district, and that four people in it had been able to say it all day and had not.

A woman of about forty-eight said in nine seconds that the district had gone in the ordinary way a district goes, which is that the work stops arriving, and that nobody in that district had stood up and said anything and that this had been available to them since the spring.

**Nobody in that room said that anybody in that district had done anything wrong, and nobody in that room said that anybody had done anything well either.**

---

**And the line that was being held is not being held by anybody now, and nobody has said that, and four people in that room know what the line was and about nine know that there was one.**

A man of about fifty-two said in nine seconds that there was no line. He said that a line is something held at one end, and that the other end went on Monday, and that what is left is a practice in about four people's hands and not a line.

A man of about thirty-four said that he had been saying since Thursday that there was no line, and that he had been saying it to be told he was wrong, and that he had not been told he was wrong.

**Nobody asked him why he wanted to be told he was wrong, and two people in it have said since that they know why, and that neither of them is going to write it down.**

---

**A man of about twenty-two was in that room for about an hour and said nothing and was asked nothing, and he has said since that he was four feet from a table for an hour on the day four hundred people left a district.**

He said that he was asked on the Friday whether he was going to stand at a gate and say a date, and that he was not asked again, and that the person who said it was a woman who walks past that gate every morning, and that he had not known she had done it until the Monday.

He was told a thing and did not repeat it.

---

**The rest of that Monday was a counter until about six and about half an hour on a bus stop flag bracket in a second district, where the bracket had been set in a cold joint and the joint was open.**

A flag bracket is a plate with a slot in it that a flag slides into, and the plate is bolted to a post, and if it is bolted into a joint in a concrete post that joint is not continuous with the post and the plate works loose in about four years.

The bracket on that post had worked loose at two of its four bolts and was holding on about a quarter of an inch. He grouted the open joint with a cementitious grout, drilled the four holes through into sound concrete, and refitted with expanding bolts.

Twenty-six pounds. He told the man that a flag bracket in an open joint is the commonest fault in this city on anything bolted to concrete, and that four stops on that road have one, and that grouting the joint costs less than the paint.

---

**And the second fault on that Monday was a set of steps at a yard in the same district, where the fourth step had sunk a quarter of an inch in eleven years.**

A step that sinks a quarter of an inch over eleven years is a step with a soft bearing under one corner, and it is the corner that goes first, and about nine people a day put a heel on it without noticing and about four notice.

He lifted the four steps, dug out about four inches of the material under the sinking corner, put a solid block and a length of angle under it, and relaid the steps on a bed of mortar with the fall set to the gully.

Forty-four pounds. He told the man that a step is not a paving slab and that a step laid on sand is a step that will be a trip in about eleven years, and that four sets of steps in that district are laid on sand.

---

**And a rainwater pipe on the front of that workshop had been running down the brickwork for about four years and had been called a downpipe, and it was not one, and it is put here because nobody had ever looked at it.**

A pipe that runs down a face instead of into a gutter is a length of waste pipe laid against a wall to make a hole go away, and four years of that is four years of water going into the wall. He took it off, cut the head off the hopper at the top, fitted a downpipe of the size the hole wanted and a hopper and a shoe, and ran a bracket line up the wall to a gutter that was six feet higher than it had been drawn.

Nineteen pounds. He told the man that a pipe that runs down a wall is a hole that has been painted, and that four frontages on that street have one, and that the wall behind one of them has been wet for so long that the plaster has come off in sheets.

---

**And a roller shutter on a workshop in that same district came down crooked on the Friday and had been left that way over the weekend, which is the ordinary reason a shutter is crooked and not the only reason.**

He came back on the Monday because the man had said it on the Friday and had not been told anything after that, and the curtain was sitting a quarter of an inch out of true in the guides at the bottom.

He took the bottom rail out, shimmed the guide on the closing side a sixteenth of an inch, and refitted it, and wound the curtain down by hand and let it go to see whether it ran. Thirty-one pounds.

He told the man that a shutter out of true is a guide and not a curtain, and that the guide on that shutter had been packed with a length of card at the bottom in the winter of before last, and that four shutters in that district have a packed guide and about none of them have been looked at.

---

**And a man of about fifty-two who keeps a kettle where he keeps a kettle came to that counter on that Monday at about four for a length of chain, and bought it, and said nothing about a district, and has not said anything about a district since.**

He has said since that he was not asked whether he was leaving and that he was not going to be, and that a man who has a district is a man who has to have an opinion about it by the end of a week and that he has never had one in his life.

He bought four feet of chain and a shackle and paid for both and went out, and that is the whole of what he did on the day four hundred people left a district.

---

**And the fourth of those four rooms has a woman of about thirty at her chair from half past six, her door shut, and on the shelf behind her the eighth of eight has been sitting for two hundred and fifty-four days and has not been read.**
