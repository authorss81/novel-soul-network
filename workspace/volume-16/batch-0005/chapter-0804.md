# Chapter 804 — A Friday, A Bench Until Two And A Counter Until About Six, And A Gate In A Second District At About A Quarter To Seven In The Morning Where A Woman Of About Thirty-Eight Who Runs A Counter At A Yard Walked Past On Her Way To Work And Said Four Words To A Man Who Locks It Without Stopping Walking, And Nobody Asked Her To Stop

**A Friday began at a bench, went through a counter, and went out early through a gate in a second district, where four words were said to one man in about four seconds by a person who was walking past and did not stop. Nobody has said anything about it since. The shutter came down at ten.**

---

The board for that Friday carried thirteen names and thirteen dates, and the last name was entered at twenty to five. The shutter came down at ten on that Friday, at the hour it comes down at on every day of this stretch of days except one.

**Line seventeen is one thousand two hundred and thirty-nine days, one hundred and seventy-seven weeks to the day. The post at that corridor end is one thousand and fifteen days, one hundred and forty-five weeks to the day. The separation is eight hundred and forty-seven days, one hundred and twenty-one weeks to the day.**

**Those four rooms and one behind the other three are at one thousand four hundred and sixty-seven days, two hundred and nine weeks and four days, and the card on end in the rail by that door is one thousand four hundred and seventy-one days, two hundred and ten weeks and one day.**

---

**The thing that happened on that Friday took about four seconds and it happened at about a quarter to seven in the morning in a gateway in a second district, and it was arranged by nobody, and there were two people in that gateway and only one of them was listening.**

The yard opens at a weekend and shuts at about two on the second day, and the man who locks it on the closing day has done that for about nine years. He stands in that gateway for about eleven minutes at two and then he goes home.

The woman of about thirty-eight who takes the money over the scale at the gate walks past that gateway at about a quarter to seven every morning and has done for four years. She had said on the Wednesday before that she would say four words to him and nobody had arranged it and nobody thanked her for saying so.

**She said them in about four seconds and she did not stop walking and she did not turn round.**

---

**And a man of about thirty-four said on the Friday morning, in nine seconds, at the bench, that this was the wrong person and that the argument for it had been sound and the timing had been the difficulty.**

He said that a person who locks a gate at two on a weekend is not a person who can be asked to hold a date. He said that a man standing in a gateway for eleven minutes with nothing to do thinks about nothing at all, and that if four words go into that eleven minutes they go in beside whatever he was thinking about.

He said that the four words would be in that man's head by the time the yard opened and out of it by the middle of the following week, and that on the following week the yard would be shut and the man would be doing something else and the four words would be a thing he had heard.

**He said that a date told to one person is not held by one person, it is repeated by one person, and that a person repeats a date badly.**

Nobody at that bench said that he was wrong.

---

**And she said it anyway, on the Friday morning, at about a quarter to seven, in about four seconds, and she has said since that she had not thought about the argument because the argument was about a different Friday.**

She said the argument was sound about a man in a gateway with eleven minutes and that this man was not in a gateway, and that he was on the step with his coat half on and his hand on the latch, and that a person says a thing differently at a quarter to seven than at two in the afternoon.

She said she did not stop because stopping turns it into an event, and that two people at a gate at a quarter to seven on a weekday are two people passing each other, and that an event at a gate at a quarter to seven on a weekday is a thing people notice and a thing people notice is a thing people repeat.

**A man of about thirty-four who was told about it afterwards did not ask her to stop walking, and has said that he wanted to and did not, and that the reason is that she had not stopped walking when he was in that gateway.**

---

**And the man who locks that gate said nothing to anybody about it, and did not say it had happened, and did not deny it, and has been asked by nobody because nobody knows.**

Two people have said since that he repeated the date correctly to a man who works Fridays, on the Friday, and that the second of them has said that a man who repeats a date correctly once is a man who has been thinking about it for a week.

A man of about fifty-two said that there is now one person in that district who has been told, and that there was none on Thursday, and that one is not a number anybody can do anything with, and that this is the correct state of it.

**Nobody thanked anybody and nobody said anything about it afterwards, and the register of correct acts that made no consequence stood at four on that Friday and stands at four.**

---

**And on that Friday four people in a room off a road were asked whether the four words had got out of the corridor, and four people gave four different answers, and none of them was that somebody had said them to a man at a gate.**

A man of about thirty-eight said that nine people in that district could say them and had been able to since the Thursday before last, and that the room was still asking the wrong question, and that the question was not whether it had got out.

A woman of about thirty-four said that nobody had been told who the nine were and that nobody had been asked, and that this was correct, and that a list of nine people in a district who can say four words is a list of nine people who can be found.

**Nobody in that room asked for the list and two of them have said that asking for it would be the first sensible thing anybody does and two have said it would be the end of it.**

---

**A man of about twenty-two was at a counter in a first district from two until about six on that Friday and four feet from a door closer and said nothing and was asked nothing.**

He has said since that a sentence that goes from a wall into nine mouths in a fortnight and then into one man's head at a gate is doing something no institution in this city has a name for, and that he has no idea whether it is working.

He was told a thing and did not repeat it. He has said that he wrote nothing down on that Friday and that there was nothing to write down.

---

**The rest of that Friday was a counter until about six and about an hour on a gate latch in a fourth district, where the latch and the staple had been fitted to a gate that opens outwards on a wall.**

That gate opens outwards and there is a wall two feet from it on the open side, so a man standing at that gate opens it into his own chest unless he stands to one side, and every man who has worked that gate has learned to stand to one side, and a man who has not learned stands into it.

The staple had been set on the closing side and the latch was on the other, so the bolt came down and the gate had to be pushed to close it, and a gate that has to be pushed to close gets pushed.

He took the staple out, re-set it a quarter of an inch higher, and fitted a longer bolt with a flat on the end so a man can catch it and draw it without putting a shoulder into it. Twenty-two pounds.

The man said the previous man had done the gate four years ago and had put it in exactly this way, and that he had told him at the time that a gate which opens outwards needs the staple on the opening side, and that this had not been done.

He said that four gates in that yard are the same and that about nine gates in that district open outwards into a wall and that two people in this city have measured the gap on all nine.

---

**And the second fault on that gate was not the latch, which is the ordinary shape of a morning's work, and it was a drop bolt that had been fitted at the wrong end and had been lying on the ground for four years.**

He picked it up, put it in the socket at the bottom of the gate, and found that the socket was set in line with the hinge rather than in line with the bolt, so the bolt came down about half an inch to one side of the hole and had to be persuaded.

He re-set the socket in line with the bolt and put the bolt in it, and it went down straight with a hand on it. Twenty-two pounds was for the latch and this was eleven, and he told him the order, because a man who has paid for a latch will tell the next man the gate was sound.

He said that a drop bolt lying on the ground is not a broken gate and is a gate with one fastening out of two, and that four gates in that yard had been like that for years and that nobody had counted them.

---

**And the third fault on that Friday was a bucket and a rope in the same yard, where the rope had been cut and spliced by somebody who used it for four years and got the splice sound by accident.**

A splice in rope is a cut, an unpicked lay, an overlap and a seizing, and a splice that has been done properly holds better than the rope around it, and a splice that has been done quickly holds until it is wet.

He cut the old splice out, laid the rope open for about two feet, overlapped it by eight times the rope's diameter, took four turns of small stuff and two of marline, and squeezed the whole thing flat with a Vice and left it under about half an hour's load to set.

Eight pounds and fifty minutes. He told the man that a spliced rope that has been squeezed and left under load will not slip, and that the same splice squeezed and left loose will slip in about a month, and that this is the whole of it and takes a hundredth of the time.

He said that four ropes in that yard had been spliced and that one of them was in a lifting eye and that nobody had ever taken the weight off it to let it set.

---

**And the fourth fault was a pulley that had been turned over on its axle, which turns over for one reason only, and the reason was still lying on the ground beside it.**

A pulley turns over when it is side-loaded, and a pulley is side-loaded when a rope is run off it at an angle instead of straight, and the ordinary cause of that angle is somebody shortening the throw by eye.

The block had been reeved over once and left, and the becket was lying in the dirt about a foot to one side of where it had been. He reeved it back over the sheave, took the turns off the becket to put them back on, and greased the axle.

Eleven pounds. He told the man that a pulley which has turned over has been told something about how the rope is being pulled and that the block is the last thing in the chain to fail.

---

**And the fourth of those four rooms has a woman of about thirty at her chair from half past six, her door shut. On the back shelf behind her the eighth of eight has been sitting for two hundred and fifty-one days and nobody has taken it off.**

---

**And tomorrow carries no chapter at all, and the day after that is the only one of its kind in this stretch of days, and on it everything in this city shuts at about two.**
