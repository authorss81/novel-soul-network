# Chapter 802 — A Wednesday, A Bench Until Two And A Counter Until About Six, And A Room Above A Line In Saltmarket Where A Yard In That District That Only Opens At A Weekend Was Named And Somebody Offered To Tell It On A Wednesday And Was Told Why Not, And A Woman Of About Thirty-Eight Who Said She Would Walk Past It On A Friday Instead

**A Wednesday began at a bench, went through a counter, and finished on a first floor above a line in Saltmarket, where a yard in that district was named and an offer to tell it something on a Wednesday was refused with a reason that was about a weekend. No number was said in that room on that day and nothing on any sheet in it moved.**

---

The board for that Wednesday carried thirteen names and thirteen dates, and the thirteenth name was entered at twenty to five. The shutter came down at ten on that Wednesday, at the hour it comes down at on every day of this stretch of days except one.

**Line eighteen is one thousand one hundred and eighty-three days, a hundred and sixty-nine weeks to the day. The man of about fifty-one against that north wall is one thousand and seventy-one days, one hundred and fifty-three weeks to the day.**

**What is still owed is one thousand one hundred and fifty-five days, one hundred and sixty-five weeks to the day. Behind the chair of the woman of about sixty a place has stood empty for three hundred and forty-three days, which is forty-nine weeks to the day, and it is printed here and on no other page of this stretch of days.**

---

**The thing that got decided on that Wednesday was that one business in that district was going to be told a date before anybody asked it about anything, and the yard is one that opens at a weekend and on no weekday of any week.**

It is a yard for metal. It opens at six on the first day of the weekend and it shuts at about two on the second, and the only person who is in it on both of those days is a woman of about thirty-eight who takes the money over a scale at a counter that is a plank on two blocks at the gate.

Nobody has told about four hundred people in that district that the four words exist, except by accident and except in about nine mouths, and one of the nine is a man who works the last weekday at the yard and said them to a man who locks the gate at the end of the weekend.

**A man of about thirty-four said in nine seconds that a yard which only opens at a weekend cannot be told anything on a Wednesday.**

His reason was that the whole point of telling somebody first is that nobody is standing behind them when they are told. He said that a Wednesday is a day with no one in that yard at all, and that anything said on a Wednesday about a yard becomes by the weekend a thing that was said on a Wednesday, and that the Wednesday is the part people repeat.

He said that about four hundred people who work in that district are not waiting to be told anything and that a thing said in an empty yard on a Wednesday is a thing four hundred people will hear at a weekend as a rumour with a day on it.

---

**And a man of about fifty-two said that was true, and said the other half of it, and neither half of it was new.**

He said that the reason nobody had said anything to that yard in eleven days was not that it had been overlooked and that it had not been overlooked, and that a yard which opens on a weekend is the easiest thing in a district to say anything to and the hardest thing to say anything to quietly.

He said that four businesses in that district do not open on a weekday, and that two people in that room had been trying since the Friday to work out how anybody says a thing to a business that is not there, and that a fortnight of trying had produced a Wednesday and nothing else.

He said the difficulty is not that the yard is shut. He said the difficulty is that the yard is shut the way a thing is shut when everybody already knows what it is, and that four hundred people in that district know precisely what that yard is and what it weighs and what it pays, and that there is no version of four hundred people being told a date by a person who knows all of that which does not become the yard being shut for a reason.

**A woman of about thirty-eight said in nine seconds that she would walk past it on the Friday and say four words to the person who locks it, and that four words to one person is not telling anybody anything.**

She said she locked that gate herself at the end of every weekend for four years before she took the counter, and that she was still the person who put the chain on at two, and that the person who locked it on a Friday was a man who has done Fridays for about nine years and who stands in that gateway for eleven minutes before he goes.

She said she walks past that gate at about a quarter to seven every morning and has done for four years, and that a person who says a thing on a bus says it wrong, and that a person who says it standing in a gateway at six in the morning does not.

Nobody thanked her.

---

**And a man of about thirty-four said in nine seconds that what she had just described was a thing that happened to be arranged, and that the arrangement was the whole of the difficulty.**

She said that nothing had been arranged, and that she had been going past that gate at a quarter to seven for four years before anybody in this city had four words, and that if it is arranged now it can be unarranged now, and that a person does not have to be asked to stop doing a thing she has done for four years.

A man of about thirty-eight said that was the best argument anybody had made in a fortnight and then said that he had been the man who had been going to stand at that gate on the Monday, and that nobody had asked him and that he was not going to say anything about it either.

**Nobody thanked anybody and nobody said the argument was settled.**

---

**And about nine people went up that stair on that Wednesday between half past six and seven and four of them stayed, and one of the four asked whether the yard is open four times in a month.**

The woman of about sixty said that it opens twice and that it shuts at two on the second day and opens at six on the first, and that a person wanting something from that yard four times in a month is a person who has not been in it twice.

She said it in the form she has used for two months and she said it to the door rather than to the person, and a man of about fifty-two who was in the room said afterwards that she had answered a question about the yard with a fact about the hours and that this was the only thing she had done all evening that she had not done before.

---

**And nothing was written down on that Wednesday about the yard, and the name of the yard is not on the drawing of nine streets, and the drawing has no names on it at all.**

A woman of about forty-eight said in nine seconds that this was correct and that a drawing with a name on it becomes a list, and that a list of places in that district is the first half of a list of people, and that nobody in this city has asked for the second half and is not going to get it.

A man of about thirty-four said that the drawing had been made on the back of a docket sheet and had been given to nobody, and that this was now the third Friday on which something had been decided in a room and had been written on nothing.

Nobody in that room corrected him about which Friday it was.

---

**A man of about twenty-two was in that room for about fifty minutes, said nothing, was asked nothing, and was told one thing and did not repeat it.**

He has said that a person who locks a gate on a Friday and a person who walks past a gate at a quarter to seven are two different arrangements and that only one of them has to be given permission and that the other does not.

He has also said that he wrote nothing down and that nothing was put in his hand, and that he noticed the drawing of the streets was in the docket book and not on a shelf and that he did not ask why.

---

**The rest of that Wednesday was a counter until about six and about half an hour on a bench in a second district, where four seat slats had been replaced with four slats of a different width and had been screwed down through the middle instead of through the bars.**

A slat on a bench sits on cast bars, and the screw goes down through the slat and into the bar and not through the top of the slat into the frame. Somebody had screwed through the middle because the bars under those four slats had moved, and the middle of a slat is where the sitter's weight is worst and the screw head is exactly where a coat goes.

He took the four screws out, moved the bars back under the bars' own fixings where they still lined up, cut four new slats to the width of the two sound ones, and screwed all eight through their bars. Forty-four pounds.

The man said the previous man had done four slats in about twenty minutes and that the slats had looked fine. He said that four slats of a different width on a bench is not a repair anybody notices and that it is a bench that will be thrown out in four years instead of eleven.

---

**And the second fault on that bench was not the slats, which is the ordinary shape of a morning's work, and it was a back leg standing about half an inch forward of the other three.**

A bench that has been carried once has a leg out, and a bench with a leg out puts the whole frame into a twist over about two years and then the bench starts to rock at the front. He packed the back of that leg to a shim of two thicknesses, planed the high leg by a sixteenth of an inch, and re-bedded the foot.

Forty-four pounds was for the slats and this was nineteen, and he told him the order, because a bench with new slats looks like a bench somebody has been at.

He said that four benches on that street had a leg out and that two of them had been carried since he was last on that street and that carrying a bench is the only way to break one that is otherwise sound.

---

**And the third fault on that Wednesday was a catch on a window in the same building, which had been a wedge and had been packed with a piece of newspaper.**

A window catch is a small lever and a cam that draws the sash against the frame, and the cam tightens as the wood moves and the wood moves as the building moves, which is why a catch on a building four years old has stopped pulling properly and needs packing behind the sash.

Packing a newspaper behind a sash stops the draught for a season and puts grit into the rebate, and the grit is what wears the cam out in about two years. He took the wedge out, took the newspaper out, cleaned out the rebate and the cam groove, and packed the sash with a length of tapered wood shim instead.

Nineteen pounds was for the bench and this was eleven, and he told the man the order, because a man who has paid for a bench and not for a window will say the window was fine.

He said that four windows in that building had newspaper in them and that in about four years the catches on those four will need replacing rather than packing, and that nobody in that building has a list of which four.

---

**And the fourth fault was a bell housing on a stair door in a third district, where the bell had gone silent and the housing was still working.**

A bell housing is a small drum with a hammer in it and a striker on the door, and a bell goes silent for three ordinary reasons: the hammer has lifted off its pivot, the pivot is dry, or the dome has come off and cracked so the sound is going out of the gap instead of into the stair.

It was the third of those. The dome had a crack across the seam that had opened by a quarter of an inch and had been let go for a winter, and he could see daylight through it from the landing.

He took the dome off, put a new length of the same steel over the seam, refitted it, put a drop of oil on the pivot, and tapped the hammer three times to see it lift and fall. Eight pounds and twenty minutes.

The man said he had reported it in the autumn and that somebody had come and had said it was the pivot and had oiled it and that it had gone quiet again in about a month. He said the man who came had charged him eleven pounds for the visit.

He said that oil on a pivot under a cracked dome is a repair that works for a month and that a crack in a dome is the whole of the fault and that four bell housings in that district have open seams on the winter side.

---

**And the fifth fault was a hinge on a gate in a yard in that same district, which was not the yard that opens at a weekend and which he did the last half hour of that Wednesday.**

That yard opens on a weekday. The gate is nine feet wide and it is hung on two hinges in a frame of soft oak, and the lower hinge had drawn about a quarter of an inch out of the oak and the gate now hangs about a quarter of an inch low at the free end.

He took the gate off, took out the top pin, levered the lower leaf up, put a length of galvanised plate over the screw holes and refitted with longer screws into sound oak further in, and eased the gate by an eighth of an inch at the free end so that it swings without a catch.

Thirty-one pounds. He told the man that a gate that is nine feet wide on two hinges of this kind is four years from the bottom hinge being out, and that the plate will not stop that and will only make the next repair easier.

---

**And the fourth of those four rooms has a woman of about thirty at her chair from half past six with her door shut, and the eighth of eight has been on the shelf behind her for two hundred and forty-nine days.**
