# Chapter 794 — A Friday, A Bench Until Two And A Counter Until About Six, And A Room Off A Road In A Second District Where Nine Streets Were Drawn On The Back Of A Docket Sheet In Four Minutes By A Man Of About Thirty-Four And Then Not Corrected, And Where Nobody Could Put A Name On Any Of Them

**A Friday started at a bench, went through a counter, and ended in a room off a road in a second district, where a man drew nine streets on the back of a piece of paper in four minutes and did not correct them. Nothing was uncovered. The streets have been there since before anybody in that room was born.**

---

The sheet for that Friday carried twelve names and twelve dates, and the last name was taken at twenty to five. The shop was shut by ten, as it is shut by ten on every other day of this stretch of days.

**The rooms off that service road and one behind the other three stand at one thousand four hundred and fifty-three days, two hundred and seven weeks and four days, and the card on end in the rail by the door is one thousand four hundred and fifty-seven days, two hundred and eight weeks and one day.**

**The nineteen, last line in use, is one thousand one hundred and forty-nine days, a hundred and sixty-four weeks and one day. What is still owed is one thousand one hundred and forty-three days, a hundred and sixty-three weeks and two days.**

---

**The streets had always been there. Nobody in that room found anything out on that Friday, and about four people in this city have said since that this is the only reason the work went as fast as it did.**

Nine streets. About four hundred people live on them and about nine hundred more do. A half bus route goes past all nine and does not go into any of them, and the stop nearest the far end of that route is a nine-minute walk from one end of the ninth street and nobody has measured it in a way that anybody kept.

A man of about thirty-eight said that this is the least surprising sentence anybody has said in eleven years and that about four people in this city have been walking about four hundred yards of it every day for eleven years.

Nobody in that room disagreed. Nobody in that room claimed to have found anything.

---

**And then the difficulty came out, and it came out in nine seconds, and it was a street with two names on it and not a hole in the ground.**

The ninth street is called two things. One name is on the wall at the gate end and on the bus stop and in about nine hundred mouths. The other is on the post at the corner at the other end and in about four hundred mouths, and the two have been both of those things since before the spring.

A woman of about forty-eight said the whole of it in nine seconds, and she had said a version of it on the Thursday and nobody had known she would say it again.

"**That is why nobody has drawn it,**" she said. "**Not because it is hard. Because the moment you put it on paper you have to write one of the two names down, and about nine hundred people will come and tell you the other one is the correct one, and you will spend a fortnight in a room instead of at a gate.**"

A man of about thirty-eight said that was an argument for drawing both. She said that two names on one line is a thing four hundred people can read as an error, and that an error about a street is the fastest way to lose a street.

---

**And then a man of about thirty-four drew it, and it took him four minutes, and he did it on the back of a docket sheet because there was nothing else on the table.**

He drew nine lines. He drew the half bus route along one side of them as a single line with about nine marks on it and no stops marked, because the stops are not on the streets.

He did it from memory and eight of the nine came out as they are. He wrote the ninth street twice, once at the top of the sheet with one name and once at the bottom with the other, and left three inches of blank between them and did not join them up and did not explain the gap.

**Nobody corrected him. A woman of about thirty-eight asked whether the two were meant to be the same and he said in four seconds that they were and that he had not known which one to write, and that a person who draws a thing he does not know is not doing the same job as a person who draws a thing wrongly.**

That is the first time anybody in this city has said that, and about four people have said since that it is either very good sense or a way of not doing the work, and about four have said the other.

---

**And then the sheet was finished, and it is on the back of a docket sheet, and it is the first drawing anybody in this city has made of where about four hundred people live.**

There was no drawing before that Friday. Nobody in this city has a map of that district, of any district, and about four people have said that this is the most surprising absence in eleven years of looking, and about four have said that a district is not a thing anybody ever had to draw.

Nobody in that room gave it to anybody. It went into the docket book on the open shelf behind that counter, between two books about hinges, at half past six, and it is the third figure-page in that book and none of the three is his.

A man of about fifty-two said that a drawing on the back of a docket sheet is a drawing that will be thrown out by whoever clears that shelf, and that this is a fact about the shelf and not about the drawing, and that he would not put it anywhere else.

**Nobody thanked him. He has said since that he did not want it thanked, and that two others at that table would have drawn a worse one and been thanked for it.**

---

**And the thing that everybody in that room noticed about the drawing after nine minutes of looking at it, is that there are no names on it, and there are no names on it because there is no list.**

Nine streets and a half bus route and about nine marks on the route and not one name anywhere on it. Not a street name, not a house, not a person, not a yard.

A man of about thirty-four said it himself, in nine seconds, standing up, and he said it to the table.

"**This is a thing a person can be shown,**" he said. "**It is not a thing a person can be given. You can hand a person a map and they will ask you who lives on the ninth street and I have to say I do not know, and about four hundred people will then find out on a Tuesday that the man who drew it did not know.**"

Nobody at that table said he had it wrong. One of them has said since that a map with no names on it is a wall with no door on it, and another has said it is better than a wall with a door on it that goes nowhere.

---

**A man of about twenty-two stood four feet away from that table for about an hour and a quarter, said nothing, was asked nothing, and has said since that he could have named eleven people on that sheet in four minutes.**

He said that every one of the eleven would have been somebody's brother or somebody's mother, and that he does not know eleven people on nine streets who are not, and that this is not a reason to put eleven names and it is a reason to be careful.

He also said that he wanted the drawing to be put on a wall and did not say so, and that on a wall it would be a thing about a district and in a docket book it is a thing nobody has to answer to.

---

**And a woman who has lived on the ninth street for nine years came up that stair on that Friday evening and looked at the drawing for four minutes and said that the bend in the middle of it has not been there since before the spring.**

Nobody asked her how she knew the street's whole shape from one piece of paper. She said that she does not, and that she looked at the part she knows, and that the part she knows has one bend in it and the drawing has two.

**The man of about thirty-four did not go back to the sheet. He said in four seconds that a person who has been on a street for nine years knows it and that he drew it from memory, and that the second bend is probably the one that goes past the allotments, and that he could not say.**

The woman said that the allotments end at a gate about a hundred yards before that bend. He said thank you in nine words and did not change anything and she has said since that he did not need to and that the bend is where it belongs and he is not.

**And four of the people who came up that evening can now say what is on the sheet, and nine cannot, and nobody has made a copy and nobody has taken the sheet out of the docket book to show anybody.**

The three inches of blank between the ninth street written at the top of that sheet and the ninth street written at the bottom is still there. About two people in this city have said that the blank is the most honest part of it, and about two have said that a drawing with a hole in it is a drawing of somewhere and not of a thing.

---

**The rest of that Friday was a counter until about six and about half an hour on a cold room in a third district, where the handle had been carried on the outside and the door had been riding on its seal since before the spring.**

The door had a lever handle on the outside and nothing on the inside, and outside handles on cold room doors are put there by people who have been locked in, and there were four people in that district who could say they had been.

He took the handle off, packed the spindle behind the escutcheon and refitted it, and then found the real fault, which was that the door had been closing on the top corner and had worn a quarter of an inch of rubber off the seal in four years.

He charged twenty-two pounds for the handle and nothing for the seal, and told the man that the seal will want doing in two years and that if he does it now it will be done again in eleven weeks.

The man asked him whether the handle was worth doing at all and he said that it was worth about four hundred pounds to about nine people and eleven pounds to the building, and that those two numbers are not the same number and that he could only charge the second one.

---

**And the cold room had a second fault, which was on the outer door and which nobody had ever mentioned, because the outer door is only used by deliveries and a delivery comes in when it comes in.**

The outer door had a closer on it that had been adjusted over four years until it was set to hold the door barely shut at all, because a door that slams in a cold room puts more heat into the room than the room loses through its roof.

He put the closer back to where it was when it was new, marked the position with a pencil cross on the arm, and told the man that the mark is on the arm where a man in two years can find it and not on the frame where it would be painted over.

Thirty-one pounds and half an hour for the handle, the seal advice and the closer, and he charged nothing for the closer because it took eleven minutes and he had already taken the money.

**He said that about four things in that building had been fought with for four years by somebody who had been correct about the heat and mistaken about the cause, and that a building is a bad place to be certain in, and that this is the second time this month he has said that sentence.**

And the second job that Friday was a freezer in a fourth district whose thermostat had been set by a man who wanted ice, and had been left at that setting by four men who wanted ice, and had been running at about minus fourteen for two years instead of about minus four.

He read the dial, said what it was, and reset it to a mark he made on the case with a pencil, and told the man that two years of running ten degrees colder is the whole of his electricity bill and the whole of his reason for having stopped buying ice.

The man said he had bought ice on Tuesdays for two years. He said that on a Tuesday you can get the ice anywhere and on any other day you cannot, and that a man who buys ice on a Tuesday should leave the thermostat where it is, and that about four shops on that road have a Tuesday for it.

---

**And the fourth of those four rooms has a woman of about thirty at her chair from half past six with her door shut, and the eighth of eight has been on that back shelf for two hundred and forty-two days without being opened.**
