# Chapter 796 — A Tuesday, A Bench Until Two And A Counter Until About Six, And A Building In A First District Where An Office Was Asked For A Record Of What A District Has Actually Been Doing And Produced A Book Of Returns About Hinges, In Which About Two Hundred Lines Carry One Man's Name

**A Tuesday opened at a bench, went through a counter, and then went across this city on foot to a building in a first district, where a man asked an office for the thing it had and the thing he asked for was not the thing the office expected to be asked for. It was handed over. Nobody said thank you.**

---

The board for that Tuesday carried ten names and ten dates, and the tenth name was taken at twenty to five. By ten the shutter was down and stayed down, as it does on every day of this stretch of days.

**The four rooms off that service road and the one behind the other three stand at one thousand four hundred and fifty-seven days, two hundred and eight weeks and one day.**

**Line sixteen is one thousand two hundred and forty-seven days, one hundred and seventy-eight weeks and one day. The post at that corridor end is one thousand and five days, one hundred and forty-three weeks and four days.**

**Eight hundred and thirty-seven days is the separation, one hundred and nineteen weeks and four days, and it is in a one-line box sixteen months old, and nothing in that paragraph has anything to do with any of this.**

---

**The walk across that city took eleven minutes each way and the man of about thirty-four made it twice in one afternoon, and the reason he went in person is that the request could not be made any other way.**

There is no form for asking a district's office what a district has actually been doing. There is a form for asking what a district has decided, and it has three boxes on it and one of them is for a date.

A man of about twenty-two said, on the Tuesday morning, that the request could go on the form with three boxes, and a man of about thirty-four said no in four seconds, and gave a reason, and the reason was that a box is for an answer and what he wanted was a record of what had happened.

**He had said that the reason was not politeness. He said that a district which is about to stop holding something is going to be asked what it held, and that nobody in that district had ever asked the office, and that the office had never been asked, and that two sets of about nine people are about to find out the same thing on the same Tuesday.**

---

**And the office produced what it had, and what it had was a book of returns, and the book is about hinges.**

It is a hard-backed book about four hundred pages. Every page has a date at the top and nine lines under it and every line has a name in the middle of it and a word at the end of it, and the word at the end of most of the lines is hinges.

A woman of about thirty-eight, who works at that counter and has worked there for four years, said in nine seconds that what anybody wanted was not in it and never had been.

"**This is what came back,**" she said. "**If you want to know what that district decided then there is a box and the box has a date in it and it is probably full. If you want to know what it did, then it did nothing except receive loads for eleven years, and that is in here, four hundred pages of it, and nobody has ever asked anybody to read it.**"

---

**And then the man of about thirty-four asked the second question, and it took him four seconds to ask and about nine to have answered, and the answer went through the whole room.**

"**Who took them,**" he said.

There is no column for that on the return. The name in the middle of a line is the name of whoever signed the return, and nine people in that office have signed returns for eleven years between them.

A woman of about thirty-four found it herself, in nine minutes, by going backwards through about forty pages, and then she went forwards, and about four of the people at that counter stopped what they were doing.

**About two hundred of those returns carry the same name in the middle of the line as the person who took a load, and it is one man, and he has been at that yard for eleven years.**

He has never been told. Nothing on the book says who he is, and there is no page in that building that says what the name means, and about two hundred lines in that book are about four hundred loads that went through a gate at five in the morning.

---

**And a man of about fifty-two said the thing that made the afternoon, and he said it in nine seconds, and he said it to the counter and not to the room.**

"**You have asked for a thing nobody has ever asked you for,**" he said. "**Not the name. The name is on the page. You have asked what a name on that page was, and about two hundred loads went through a gate with a man standing at it and nobody in this building ever wrote down what he was standing at.**"

The woman of about thirty-eight said that the office thought those returns were about hinges. He said yes, and that the office has thought that for eleven years, and that a building which holds a record of about two hundred signatures by one man and has never known what they were is not a building with a bad record, it is a building with no record.

**Nobody thanked anybody. A woman of about thirty-four has said since that this was the first time anybody had walked in there and asked what was on a page rather than for a page.**

---

**And Iven Sore came out of the office at half past four and asked what had been asked for, and a man of about thirty-four told him, and he said nothing for nine seconds and then said one thing.**

He is under a review he agreed to without reading it. He has not been told what the review says and has not asked. He has been in post four years and has not enlarged anything and has not apologised for anything.

"**Take the book,**" he said. "**Not a copy. I have wanted that book asked about since the year it started and I have not asked you to ask me and I am not going to now.**"

The man of about thirty-four said he would take a copy and Iven said no in four seconds, and said that a copy is a thing a building can lose and a book is a thing a building can be asked for.

**He said it to end with, eleven seconds later, and nobody in that room has repeated it: *Do not thank me. I have four years of that in this building and I did not ask for it.* Nobody in that room thanked him and about two of them have said since that it would have been the wrong thing to say and about two have said that it was not the true thing to say either.**

---

**Nobody in that building has been told what a release line is, and this is the finding of that Tuesday, and about two people in this city have already found it out by accident.**

A release line is a thing nobody in that office had a term for. The word was not used. The book has about two hundred lines in it and not one of them says what the man was doing at the gate.

A woman of about thirty-four said, in nine seconds, that the building had just handed over the only written record anybody in this city has of about four hundred people using something, and that it had done it by accident, and that nobody in that building knew what it had handed over.

---

**A man of about twenty-two stood in that building's corridor for fifty minutes and did not come into the room, and has said since that he wanted to be the one who asked the second question and did not ask it.**

He said that he had worked out on the Friday that a return sheet would have a name in it, and that he had worked out that a name in the middle of a line is the name of whoever was standing there, and that he had four days to say so and did not.

He also said that a man who works out the question four days early and says nothing for four days is a man who is training himself, and that he does not know yet whether that is a good thing to be training.

---

**And the book went back into that building's own shelving at five o'clock, and two people in that corridor asked whether they could look at it again on another day, and were told that they could not.**

A woman of about thirty-four said that a book that has been out of a building once will be asked for again by anybody who knows it has been out, and that four people in that office have found out in eleven minutes that it can be asked for.

A man of about fifty-two said that this is the correct answer and that four people in this city would have said the other thing, and that the other thing is that a record which has been asked for once belongs to whoever asked, and that this is how a building stops being able to say no to anything at all.

**Nobody in that corridor said what the book should be for, and about four people in this city have noticed that this is the second consecutive week in which the most consequential document in a room has had no stated purpose attached to it.**

A man of about thirty-four did say one thing before he left, and he said it to the counter and not to the building. He said that the man whose name is in that book has eleven years of it in a drawer in a second district, and that the book has just added about two hundred more of him to a building he has never been inside, and that neither of those two facts was going to be sent to him.

Nobody in that building agreed to send it and nobody in that building said they would not. A woman of about thirty-eight said that a man does not get told that a building has his handwriting, and that he will be told something, and that nobody in that room knows what.

---

**The rest of that Tuesday was a counter until about six and about an hour on a loading bay in a third district, where a leveller's lip seal had been smeared with grease nine times over two winters.**

The leveller sits in a pit and a lip comes up out of it and the lip has a seal round it, and the seal is meant to close on the lip when the leveller is down and is the only thing between about four hundred degrees and a man standing on it.

He went down into the pit, cleaned about nine thicknesses of grease off the seal with a scraper and a cloth, and found the seal itself sound and the lip about a sixteenth of an inch out of true on one side, which is what had let the grease in from above.

Thirty-eight pounds and about an hour, and he told the man that the seal will go again in about two winters and that the way to stop it is not grease and is a lip with a new bearing under the low corner.

The man said two other men had done this and had both said the seal was perished. He said that a perished seal is what a greasy seal looks like from above, and that a man who has never been under the lip cannot tell the two apart, and that about four people in this city are in the same position about a lot of things.

---

**And the leveller had a second fault behind the one at the lip, and it was in the hose, and nobody had seen it because the hose is under the machine.**

The power unit is under the leveller and the hose from the pump to the cylinder had a crack on the underside nine inches along, and a crack on the underside of a hose is filled rather than shown, so it had been filled with a piece of inner tube and a hose clip.

He took the inner tube off and found the crack had gone through the cover and a third of the way into the hose. He cut the hose back four inches, fitted a new length and two clamps, and refilled it, and he told the man that a filled hose is a hose that has been failing for two winters.

Thirty-eight pounds was for the seal and the lip, and he charged a further eleven for the hose, and he told the man the order, because a man who has paid for the seal and not the hose will not believe him later when the machine will not lift.

**He said that about four faults in this city are found in a different order from the one the customer paid for, and that a man who is told what he has got rather than what he needed will go to the same man twice, and that about four people in this city have learned that this year.**

And the second job that Tuesday was the ramp edge outside the same bay, where the kerb had been chipped by four years of a tailgate going down in the same place every time.

He dressed the chipped concrete back with a cold repair compound, ground it flat, and marked the back of the bay with a line of road paint at the point where a tailgate lands. Twenty-four pounds and half an hour.

The man said marking the line was not a repair. He said that a repaired kerb gets chipped again in two winters by the same tailgate in the same place, and that a line costs eleven pounds and lasts until somebody repaints it, and that about four men in that yard have been lowering the same tailgate in the same place for two winters because nothing was ever put down to say where.

---

**And the fourth of those four rooms has a woman of about thirty at her chair from half past six with her door shut, and the eighth of eight has been on the back shelf behind her for two hundred and forty-one days.**
