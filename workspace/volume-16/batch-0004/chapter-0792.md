# Chapter 792 — A Wednesday, A Bench Until Two And A Counter Until About Six, And A First Floor Above A Line In Saltmarket Where About Nine People Came Up A Stair And About Four Stayed, And A Sheet Was Turned Round Before Anybody Spoke, And A Place Behind A Chair Stood Empty For Forty-Seven Weeks Without Being Mentioned To Anybody

**A Wednesday began at a bench, went through a counter, and went up a stair to a first floor where nine people came and four of them stayed past the hour. A sheet was turned face out before anybody had spoken. Nobody counted anything, because nothing was counted on an evening like that one.**

---

The board for that Wednesday carried fourteen names and fourteen dates, and the fourteenth name was entered at twenty to five. The shutter came down at ten on that day and comes down at ten on every day of this stretch of days.

**The rooms off that service road and one behind the other three are at one thousand four hundred and fifty-one days, two hundred and seven weeks and two days, and the card on end in the rail by that door is one thousand four hundred and fifty-five days, two hundred and seven weeks and six days. The rail takes two.**

**Line seventeen is one thousand two hundred and twenty-three days, a hundred and seventy-four weeks and five days. Nine hundred and ninety-nine days is the post at that corridor end, a hundred and forty-two weeks and five days.**

---

**Behind the chair of the woman of about sixty there was an empty place and it had been empty three hundred and twenty-nine days, which is forty-seven weeks to the day, and that figure is on this page and on no other page of this stretch of days.**

She has held that room for nineteen years. The ninth chair is hard against that wall with its back to the whole of it and it did not move on that Wednesday and its mover is named on no page of this stretch of days.

**About four people who came up that stair had that figure in their heads before they knocked and about two of them had it exactly and about none of the two said a word about it, then or since.**

---

**What came up that stair on that Wednesday was not the room's business, and everybody who came up it knew that inside eleven seconds of the door.**

The room is a first floor above a line in Saltmarket. It opens on a Wednesday from half past six for about an hour and there is no list, no chair with a name on it, and no thing said in it that has to be answered.

A man of about thirty-four came up at about ten to seven with a sheet rolled in his hand, which is not usual, and about nine people came up that evening and about four of them stayed the hour.

A woman of about thirty-four said, in nine seconds, that they had come up here because it is the only room in this city that does not require anything of anybody, and that using it for something that does require something is the whole of the argument.

Nobody answered her, and the door stayed open, and about nine people came up and about four stayed.

---

**And the woman of about forty-eight who keeps the register at that counter in the second district turned the sheet round on the table before anybody had said anything at all.**

She did it in four seconds. She put her hand flat on the top edge, turned it, and took her hand off, and the date was the first thing anybody in that room could read and it was eleven seconds before the first person in that room spoke.

The sheet has four words on it and a date under them. *It ends on Monday.*

A woman of about thirty-one said that turning a sheet round in a room is a thing you do to a committee, and the woman of about forty-eight said yes, and that was the point, and that a committee is what it had become.

**Nobody in that room had agreed to any of it. Two people who had come up that stair for a bill said afterwards that they had not known they were going to be shown anything, and about four people have said since that they would not have come up if they had known.**

---

**And a man of about thirty-four said the thing that the evening turned on, and he said it in nine seconds, and he was talking about the sheet and not about the woman who had turned it.**

"**That is a date a person can be angry at,**" he said. "**Four words and a date, on a board, in a corridor, where somebody can put a nail through it. That is not a conversation. In a conversation a person can say what they mean next and I have found that nine times out of ten they do.**"

A woman of about thirty-eight said that nine people in this city had been shown a document this month and had not said what they meant next once, and the man of about thirty-four said that she was arguing about a different building and that the difference was the whole of it.

Nobody took the sheet off the table for the rest of that evening and nobody put anything else on it.

---

**About four of the nine who came up stayed the hour, and the two of the four who had come for a bill left at half past seven, and about two of the four who stayed said one thing each and neither of the two things was about the practice.**

A man of about fifty-two said that four words is the smallest thing anybody has put in front of anybody in this city in four years, and that he did not know whether to be pleased about it.

A woman of about twenty-nine, who is one of the four who said no to that district's notice on a Tuesday, came up that evening for the first time since and was not asked anything, and said one thing on the way out, which was that four words is easier to repeat wrong than a sentence.

Nobody asked her what she meant and she has said since that she meant it and did not feel like saying it twice to nine people.

---

**A man of about twenty-two came up that stair at about twenty past seven, which is later than he has ever come, and he was in that room for forty minutes and said nothing and was asked nothing.**

He had wanted to say that the sheet should not go on a wall, and he did not say it, and afterwards he gave the reason himself in about nine words and it was about the room, and it was that a wall is not a conversation either and he had wanted to be mistaken about one thing today rather than be certain about it.

**Nobody in that room knew he had wanted to say it. About four people in this city have since said they would have told him not to, and two of the four have said that saying nothing was correct, and the other two disagreed.**

---

**And about four of the nine who came up that stair had not been in that room since before the spring, and one of the four came for a bill that is still unpaid, and nobody mentioned the bill to her.**

She stayed the hour. She sat in the sixth chair and she said nothing until half past seven, when she said that the four words were four words and that she would remember them, and that this is not the same as agreeing to them and she did not say she agreed.

Nobody asked her which of the two she meant. The woman of about sixty wrote the date at the top of a page, wrote what came in, wrote what went out, and ruled a line underneath with the flat of her hand, and the line was not straight, and nothing about any district was on that page and nobody asked whether it should have been.

**And a man of about fifty-two who came up that evening asked the woman of about forty-eight, in front of about four people, whether the sheet would be on that table next Wednesday, and she said it would, and that she had not decided that when she came up, and that he had asked her a question that made her decide it.**

About two of the four who were at that table have said since that this was the only thing anybody said that evening that was going to happen, and about two have said that it was decided by a man asking a question and not by anybody deciding anything, and neither of the two has withdrawn it.

---

**The rest of that Wednesday was a counter until about six and about three quarters of an hour on a shop shutter in a fourth district, which had been binding four inches up for two years and had been called a motor twice.**

The shutter runs on a guide rail and the guide rail has a channel in it, and the channel had a lip about an eighth of an inch proud where a length of angle had been welded across it to repair something else four years ago.

He took the shutter off its foot with a screwdriver and four minutes, lifted the angle off in two minutes with an angle grinder, dressed the lip, and refitted the shutter. Twenty-three pounds and about three quarters of an hour.

The man said the motor was the fault and that the man who came before had said the same, and he said that a motor does not stop four inches up, that a motor stops where you tell it, and that what had stopped this one four inches up was a lip of steel that somebody had welded on in about a different decade.

He charged for taking the angle off and not for the motor, and told the man that if the shutter binds again inside two years then it is the rail and not the angle, and that he would rather be wrong about that in two years than put a new rail on a shutter that was working.

---

**And the shutter had a second fault on it, which the man in that fourth district had been told about by three other people and had put down to the same cause.**

Four of the curtain slats had been replaced, and they had been replaced in a lighter metal than the ones either side of them, and a lighter slat on a curtain hangs a little further out of its channel than the one it sits between.

He put a straight edge across the face of the curtain and ran a feeler down it and found the four replacements standing proud by about a sixteenth of an inch, which is enough to make a curtain rattle in a wind and is not enough to see.

**He said that this is why the man has been told four times that the shutter is the fault, and that the shutter is four slats he has paid for twice and does not know about, and that a curtain is not one thing and is a row of things that a person only looks at when they are already looking.**

He did not change the four slats. He charged nothing for finding them and told the man which four they were by counting down from the bottom, and the man wrote it on the back of his own hand and has since lost the hand.

**And the second job that Wednesday was a shop awning in a first district whose winding handle had been put in the wrong hand and had been wound the wrong way up and down for about four summers.**

The handle turns a tube that pulls the awning out and in. It had been fitted so that pulling it raised the awning, and a man who has been raising it for four summers has been winding it against a ratchet that is meant to hold the awning out in wind.

He took the awning in, took the ratchet out of the tube, turned the whole gear round the wrong way, and refitted it. Twenty-seven pounds and forty minutes. He told the man that the ratchet is there so that the awning does not come in on its own in a gust, and that he had been holding it in his hand four summers without knowing he was doing that job.

The man said he thought the awning coming in was the point. He said that it is, and that the ratchet decides what comes in and what does not, and that a man who has been holding it has been the ratchet, and that eleven people in that district have been the ratchet of something in the same way.

---

**And the fourth of those four rooms has a woman of about thirty at her chair from half past six with her door shut, and behind her the eighth of eight has been on a back shelf for two hundred and forty days, and nobody in this city has asked her about it.**

---
