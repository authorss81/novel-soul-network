# Chapter 795 — A Monday, A Bench Until Two And A Counter Until About Six, And A Room Off A Road In A Second District Where A Building Was Looked For And Was Not Found, And Where A Room Above A Line In Saltmarket Was Offered By A Man And Taken By A Woman With A Condition Attached

**A Monday ran from a bench to a counter and then into a room off a road in a second district, where about nine people spent an evening failing to find a building and then found one room in nine seconds. The room belongs to somebody who was not asked. Nothing was sent and nothing was said.**

---

The board for that Monday carried thirteen names and thirteen dates, and the thirteenth name was written down at twenty to five. The shutter came down at ten. It comes down at ten on every day of this stretch of days.

**That card in a rail that takes two is one thousand four hundred and sixty days, two hundred and eight weeks and four days. Line thirteen is one thousand three hundred and twenty-seven days, a hundred and eighty-nine weeks and four days.**

**Line seventeen is one thousand two hundred and twenty-eight days, a hundred and seventy-five weeks and three days. The hold on nine crates and the floor they stand on is one thousand and eighty-nine days, a hundred and fifty-five weeks and four days, in the second of the four towns.**

---

**What that Monday was for was a building where about four hundred people can come and look at what is being said about them, and every part of finding one went the way those things go.**

The reason it was needed is the one from the Tuesday. There is a printed sheet in a corridor in that district held on by two drawing pins, about nine people walk past it between eleven and two, and about four of them read it. Four hundred people will not walk past a corridor.

A woman of about thirty-eight said in nine seconds that what is wanted is not a notice, it is a place, and that a place has to be a place a person can come to without having a reason to be in that street.

Nobody in that room disagreed and nobody in that room knew of one.

---

**And the answers came back all evening, and every one of them was a room above something, and every one of them held about nine people.**

There is a hall over a shop that holds about nine and belongs to a man who lets it out on the evenings. There is a room over a garage that holds about nine and smells of a release. There is a room over a butcher's that holds about nine and shuts at eight because of the cold.

**And a man of about thirty-four put the arithmetic of it on the table in nine seconds and nobody in that room had done it before.**

"**Four hundred people at nine at a time is forty-five visits,**" he said. "**Not forty-five days. Forty-five visits, on top of everything else anybody in that district is already doing on a Monday. And every room anybody in this city can put a name to is shut at about ten.**"

He said that the arithmetic was not the reason and that the reason was that about four hundred people would not come at night, because about four hundred people who use a release line at a yard are at that yard at seven in the morning and are not finished by six in the evening.

**Nobody at that table had thought about the hours. Two of them had, in four years, and neither has said it out loud in a room.**

---

**And then the man of about thirty-four said he had a room, and the room he had was the one they were standing in, and the reason he had it is not his reason to give.**

The building off that road has a first floor above a line in Saltmarket, and it opens on a Wednesday, and about nine people go up it. He did not say he had arranged it. He said that a room in a building he has keys to is not the same as a room he can give away, and that he would ask.

He asked her on the Thursday, at about ten past seven, in nine seconds, standing up, in front of about four people.

She said yes in nine seconds and said the condition, and the condition is the whole of what happened that evening.

"**Yes,**" she said. "**It is my room and it is not yours. I hold it. You can have an hour of it on a Wednesday and you can put what you like on that table while you have it, and when you have not got it, it is mine, and if anybody in this city asks me what is written on that table I will say what I like.**"

Nobody argued with the condition. One of them has said since that the condition was the arrangement and not a restriction on it. Another has said that the arrangement and the restriction are the same thing. Two more said nothing at all.

---

**And then it was a room, and nobody had been asked to give it, and nine people in that district found out over the following fortnight in four different ways.**

There is no form. There is no letter. There is a Wednesday from half past six to half past seven, and there is a table, and there is a woman who will be in the room.

A woman of about thirty-four said in nine seconds that about four hundred people will not find out because nobody has told them and nobody can, and that this is now the same problem the sheet had and nine days earlier.

A man of about fifty-two said that a room nobody knows about is a room for about nine people who already know about it, and that this was true of the last two things anybody tried in this district, and that he was not saying it to stop it.

**Nobody in that room gave the room a name and about four people have said since that a name would have been the first step towards owning it, and about four have said that a room without a name is a room that will be shut by whoever holds the keys in a bad week.**

---

**And a man of about twenty-two was in that room for two hours on that Monday and said nothing at the table and was asked nothing at the table.**

He has said since that he worked out that about four hundred people would come in about eleven mornings and not on a Wednesday evening at all, and that he had worked it out in four minutes and had not said it.

He said afterwards, to a man of about thirty-four, that if he had said it he would have known what he wanted and that a man who knows what he wanted at about seven in the evening is a man who has stopped listening, and that he has not been sure which of the two he would have been.

Nobody in that room knows that this was said and it was not said to anybody in the room.

---

**And on that Monday evening somebody did the arithmetic again with real days in it, and got a worse number than the forty-five, and the worse number was not argued with.**

A man of about thirty-one had been counting how many of the four hundred people pass a particular point on a route, and he did it on a Tuesday morning and a Friday morning and got two numbers, and the two numbers are not the same.

He said that on a Friday the count was higher by a third, and that this is because the release line is used more at the end of a week than at the start of one, and that nobody in that room had thought that a Tuesday is the quietest day of the week and had built the whole plan round a Tuesday.

**A woman of about thirty-four said in nine seconds that the plan was not built round a Tuesday, and that a Tuesday is the only day anybody had, and that this is now known and will be written on the sheet on Thursday.**

Nobody argued with her. Two of them said that nothing changes, because four hundred people who are not there on a Tuesday are not anywhere else either. Two others said that a plan which works on a Tuesday and not on a Friday has not been tested and has been declared.

**And the question nobody had asked, which was what happens if the woman of about sixty is ill, was asked at half past six by a man of about thirty-one and was not answered before the shutter came down.**

She said that she has not been ill in four years, and that if she were, then it is her room and it would be shut, and that this is the correct answer and that anybody who wants a second key to that room is asking for a thing they have not thought about.

Two of them have said since that she gave the correct answer to the wrong question, and that the question was not about her, and that nobody has worked out what the question was.

---

**The rest of that Monday was a counter until about six and about three quarters of an hour on a stair handrail in a second district, where nine steps had been polished to nothing by four years of a working man's hand.**

The rail was oak and had been varnished four times over nine years and the varnish had gone hard and slippery on the top of the rail and nowhere else, because a hand goes in one place.

He took the rail off its brackets in eleven minutes, stripped the varnish off the top two feet with a scraper and sandpaper, and oiled it, and told the man that a handrail wants oiling about once a year and a varnish wants nothing for nine years.

Twenty-six pounds and about three quarters of an hour. The man said that the man before him had varnished it and that it had been fine for two years, and he said that two years is about how long it took for the fourth coat to go hard enough to be a hazard, and that the man before him had done exactly what everybody does.

He wrote the date on the back of the man's own invoice and told him to keep it, and the man said he would, and two years from now that date will be the only thing anybody knows about this rail.

---

**And the balustrade at the bottom of that stair had a newel on it that had been loose for two years, and the loose newel was the same fault as the rail and had been treated by a different man entirely.**

A newel takes the whole handrail load at the turn, and the one at the bottom had two screws holding it and two years ago somebody had added a third screw higher up the post rather than replace the two, so the head of the newel had begun to lean out of square.

He took the three screws out, filled the two old holes with two lengths of dowel, drilled one new hole properly into solid wood, and refitted the newel with one screw and a washer. Nineteen pounds and about half an hour.

The man said he had a man in who comes on Fridays and had put the third screw on, and he said that a third screw higher up is not a repair and is a way of making a thing feel better from the bottom of a stair, which is the only place anybody looks at it from.

He wrote both the old hole positions and the new one on the back of the man's own invoice with the dates, and said that nine people in that building have put a third screw in something, and that on four of them it worked, and the four were not the ones people remember.

**And the second job that Monday was a threshold at the foot of that same stair, which had been built up with three coats of paint over four years and which was stopping the door a quarter of an inch above the floor.**

He took the door up off its hinges with two wedges and a screwdriver, and laid three thicknesses of paper on the floor and closed the door onto them, and the paper showed exactly how much of the frame had been painted.

He scraped the paint back off the frame with a chisel and filled it, and refitted the door, and told the man that a painted frame is a frame that has been made shorter by whoever painted it, and that nobody has ever told him that, and that this is the fifth door this month where it was true.

---

**And the fourth of those four rooms has a woman of about thirty at her chair from half past six with her door shut, and on the shelf behind her the eighth of eight has been sitting for two hundred and forty-five days and has not been opened.**
