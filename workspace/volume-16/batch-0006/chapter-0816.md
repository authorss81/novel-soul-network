# Chapter 816 — A Monday, A Bench Until Two And A Counter Until About Six, And A Printed Sheet With A Date On The Top Of It Carried On Foot To Four Doors In One Street By A Person Who Was Not Sent Anywhere And Who Was Given The Sheet By A Man Who Was Not Allowed To Carry It

**A Monday began at a bench, went through a counter, and finished on a street in a second district, where a printed sheet was put into four hands and not into a post box and not into a doorway. Nobody arranged it and nobody counted the people who saw it and the shutter came down at ten.**

---

That Monday's board took thirteen names and thirteen dates, the thirteenth at twenty to five. The shutter came down at ten on that Monday, at the hour it comes down at on all ten of these days.

**Those four rooms and one behind the other three stand at one thousand four hundred and ninety-eight days, two hundred and fourteen weeks to the day. Line sixteen is one thousand two hundred and eighty-eight days, a hundred and eighty-four weeks to the day, and the nine hand copies of the front of one page are one thousand and sixty-four days, a hundred and fifty-two weeks to the day.**

---

**Four doors on one street got the sheet in about an hour and a half, and the man who carried it did not put it through any of the four letter boxes and did not leave it with any of the four shops.**

He gave it to a person at a door. He said four seconds about what it was and did not say what was in it and did not ask whether the person had read the one before this one.

A woman of about thirty-four said in nine seconds that this is the correct arrangement and that it is not the one anybody would have chosen, and that a sheet pushed through a letter box on a Monday morning is a sheet nobody opens until about the fourth day, and that four days after the date at the top of it is a different day.

**She said that four doors is not four hundred doors and that about four people on that street have since asked where the other three hundred and ninety-six are, and that nobody has said.**

---

**And a man of about thirty-eight said the thing that the man carrying the sheet had not thought about, and he said it about nine minutes after the sheet was handed over.**

He said that a sheet given to a person at a door is read by that person and by nobody else, and that a sheet left in a hall or on a counter is read by whoever comes past, and that the second of those reaches about nine times as many people for the same four seconds of work.

The man carrying it said in about four seconds that he had given it to four people because four people had asked him for it, and that he had not thought about the four hundred.

A man of about fifty-two said in nine seconds that this is the same sentence as the one said about a gate a fortnight ago and that this city has now had that sentence twice from two different men and has not once had a way of settling it.

**Nobody on that street settled it and about two of the four people who were given the sheet said afterwards that they would have left it on the counter and about two said they would not.**

---

**And the drawing of nine streets was not on the sheet and was not mentioned by anybody on that street, and a woman of about forty-eight said that this was correct and did not say why.**

She said that the moment a name goes onto that drawing it stops being a drawing and turns into something else, and that places named in a row are half a list of people, and that nobody in this city has asked for the other half and is not going to get it.

A man of about thirty-four said that the drawing is on the back of a docket sheet in the inside of a docket book in a first district, and that it has been there since before the spring, and that nobody has asked what happens to a sheet that has been out of a building once.

**She said that four people in this city have asked that question since the spring and that it has been answered twice in two different ways, and that both of the answers were true and that neither of them covers a drawing.**

Nobody on that street asked her what the fifth answer would be and nobody on that street has asked her since.

---

**And a man of about thirty-four said the thing about the date on that sheet that nobody else on that street had noticed, and he noticed it because he had been the one to agree the sentence and not the date.**

He said that the date at the top of that sheet is nine days after the night the sheet is being handed to people at doors, and that this is not a mistake and that it is the first thing anybody has done in this city since the spring that puts a person's week in front of them before they are told what is happening in it.

He said that the four seconds is enough if the person hearing it has nine days, and that it is not enough if they have none, and that nobody has worked out which of those the four hundred people in that district are in.

A woman of about thirty-four said in four seconds that nobody is going to work it out, and that the four doors will read it on their own time and the rest of the street will hear about it from them.

**Nobody on that street agreed with her and nobody on that street said she was wrong, and about four people in that street have said since that they were both describing the same thing.**

---

**And the four doors did four different things with it, and the man carrying it watched all four and has said since that he had not expected any of them.**

The first went in a bin. The second put it on a table under a kettle and did not read it and has still not read it. The third read it standing up and gave it back. The fourth read it and asked him whether he knew what was in it.

He said to the fourth that he did not know and had been told not to say, and the fourth said that this was the correct arrangement and that the person who gave it to him had said the same thing four weeks ago about a different sheet.

**He said that four hundred people in that district have now been told four times and that two of them have been told twice by different people and that neither of the two knows about the other.**

Nobody on that street asked the fourth what he had done with it after that and nobody on that street has asked him.

---

**And a woman of about thirty-one said the half nobody had said, which is that four doors in about an hour and a half is about nine doors a day and that nine doors a day is a street a week.**

She said that nine doors a day is about forty-five doors in a week, and that four hundred doors is about nine streets, and that this city has never once had anybody sent to nine streets.

She said that this is not an argument for sending somebody and that it is the reason nobody has been sent, and that nine people in this city have made that connection and that nobody has acted on it.

A man of about fifty-two said in nine seconds that the correct answer is that four people go out on a Friday with a stack and reach nine doors each, and that this has been available since the spring and that nobody has done it because nobody has been given the time to do it.

**He said that the body has no staff and that the word staff is the whole of the difficulty, and that four people in this city have asked what happens to a floor where five people carry paper between tables.**

---

**A man of about twenty-two was in that street for about fifty minutes and said nothing and was asked nothing, and he has said since that he watched a sheet go into four hands and that he could not tell afterwards which of the four had read it.**

He has also said that he was told one thing and did not repeat it, and that the thing he was told was that a sheet carried on foot is a thing that cannot be produced in a room, and that this is the opposite of what he was told on the last Monday.

He said that both of those people were correct and that he had not been in a room when either of them said it.

---

**And about half an hour of that Monday afternoon was a first floor above a shop, where four people went through a list of what was in that district and found that nobody could say what was in it.**

A woman of about twenty-four said that the list had about ninety items on it and that thirty-one of them were doors and that the other fifty-nine were rooms, and that every room on the list had somebody's name on it and that no room had ever been asked whether it was still a room.

A man of about thirty-eight said in nine seconds that this is the same list that has been in a drawer since before the spring, and that a second list has been made out of it, and that nobody has compared the two and that nobody is going to.

She said that a second list made out of the first list is not a check, and that a check needs somebody to go and stand in thirty-one doors, and that four people in that room have never stood in one of those doors.

**Nobody in that room went and stood in one of those doors on that Monday and nobody has said they would.**

---

**The rest of that Monday was a counter until about six and about two hours of work in a second district, where a row of about eleven lockers on a wall had been built off the wall on spacers without any backing behind them.**

A locker bank on spacers takes the knocks of people and the weight of doors, and a locker with no backing behind it is a locker that moves in its own frame every time the door is shut hard.

He took the bank off the wall, packed the four fixing points at the ends and the middle with tapered shims until the whole bank came true against a straight edge across its whole width, and refitted it.

Twenty-four pounds. He told the man that a locker bank on spacers with nothing behind it takes about four hundred slams a week against a frame that was never braced, and that four banks in that building are on spacers and that one of them has a door that will not shut.

---

**And the second fault on that wall was a locker that had been repaired with a hasp and a staple, which is what happens to about four lockers in this city every year.**

He took the hasp and staple out, cut a new hasp, fitted it on the door and a new staple plate on the frame drilled into the frame and not into the spacer, and refitted the door on its own hinges.

Nineteen pounds. He told the man that a hasp on a locker door is a repair that holds a door shut against a person and not against a draught, and that four lockers in that room have had one and that one of them has a staple screwed into a spacer.

---

**And a bench in the same room had been fitted with legs that were about four millimetres undersize at one end, which puts the whole seat out of true.**

He packed the two undersize legs at the floor with a shim, planed the tops of those two by a millimetre each so that each end of the seat came up by two, and refitted it so it sits true along its whole length.

Nineteen pounds. He told the man that a bench a person has to sit at an angle to get off is a bench nobody has sat on for about a year, and that four benches in that building are out of level and that one of them has had a leg shortened twice.

---

**And a set of about nine coat hooks on a wall in that room had been screwed into a length of batten that was itself screwed into a masonry wall with plastic plugs.**

He pulled the batten off, filled the plug holes, drilled the batten's fixings through with a hammer drill, refitted the batten with proper anchors, and refitted the hooks into the batten's own centres.

Twenty-four pounds. He told the man that a batten on plastic plugs is a batten on about four kilograms of plastic, and that four sets of hooks in that room are on plugs and that one of them has come off the wall with a coat still on it.

---

**And the fourth of those four rooms has a woman of about thirty at her chair from half past six, her door shut, and the eighth of eight has been on the back shelf behind her for two hundred and eighty-seven days and has not been read.**

---
