# Chapter 815 — A Friday, A Bench Until Two And A Counter Until About Six, And A Yard In A Second District Where A Man Of About Forty-Seven Was Doing About Nine Of Them By Hand And Getting Paid For About Two And Who Was Asked For The First Time In About Eleven Years What It Costs Him

**A Friday began at a bench, went through a counter, and finished in a yard in a second district, where four hundred people use a thing one man holds and that man was asked on that Friday what it costs him. He answered in about nine seconds and then went back to work.**

---

Fourteen names were written on that Friday's board with fourteen dates, the last at ten to five. That Friday's shutter came down at ten, and ten is the hour it comes down at on each of these days.

**Line seventeen is one thousand two hundred and sixty-seven days, a hundred and eighty-one weeks to the day. The post at that corridor end is one thousand and forty-three days, a hundred and forty-nine weeks to the day. The separation is eight hundred and seventy-five days, a hundred and twenty-five weeks to the day.**

---

**Nobody had asked that man anything about it in about eleven years and about nine people in that district had told themselves they knew, and on that Friday a woman of about thirty-four asked him in about four seconds and he answered in about nine.**

He said that it costs him both hands, that it has cost him both hands for nine years, and that it got worse by four weeks every winter for six winters.

He said that a release takes eleven seconds and that nine of them a day is about a minute and a half of holding, and that the holding is in the left hand and that the left hand is the one that goes first.

**He said that nobody has ever asked him this and that he had told nobody and that he had worked out on his own which of the two hands was going first and that this had taken him about two years to be sure of.**

A man of about thirty-eight said in nine seconds that this was not the first time a thing like that had been worked out on its own in that district and that it was the first time it had been said out loud in a yard.

---

**And a man of about fifty-two said the half of it that nobody had said, which is that the practice is not the man's and has not been the man's for about eleven years.**

He said that about four hundred people use it and that nine times a day is nine times a day because four hundred people turn up and not because one man has decided to be at that gate nine times.

He said that a man who holds a thing for four hundred people is not paid for holding it and is not named for holding it and has never been asked whether he wants to keep holding it, and that the last of those three is the one nobody has ever done.

**He said that a thing a man is not paid for and is not named for and has not been asked about is not a post and is not a service and is a favour, and that about four hundred people have been receiving a favour every weekday for about eleven years.**

Nobody in that yard corrected him and two of them said afterwards that they had been receiving it and had not known that was the word for it.

---

**And a man of about thirty-eight asked him the second question, which is what happens on the day he stops, and he asked it in about nine seconds and it was the only question in that yard anybody had asked anybody all morning.**

He said that the man of about forty-seven answered that four hundred people find out on a Tuesday, and that this is what a woman of about thirty-four said in a room before the spring, and that nobody has said since that the Tuesday has not come yet.

He said that it is nine weeks now and that the yard has not stopped and that the man has not been told to stop and that this is the arrangement, and that the arrangement has no name, no date on it and nobody responsible for it.

**He said that the difference between a service and this is that a service has a man who is responsible when it stops, and that this has four hundred people and nobody at the end of it.**

The man of about forty-seven said in about four seconds that he has not thought about the Tuesday, and that he has thought about the left hand, and that these are not the same thought and that he has had one of them eleven weeks longer than the other.

---

**And a woman of about thirty-four said what the thing is, in about nine seconds, and then said four words and refused to say another four.**

She said that it lets a gate go back when a vehicle has come through it and that nobody in that district has ever described it in a room because it takes eleven seconds and a description takes about four minutes.

He said that she could write it down in four minutes and she said in nine seconds that she could and that she had worked out in four seconds that she would not, and that this was the fifth time she had decided that in eleven years.

Nobody in that yard asked her to write it down and nobody in that yard asked her why she would not, and two people in that yard said afterwards that the reason was the true one and two said they did not know what it was.

---

**And a woman of about thirty-one who keeps a haulier's books said the thing about the arrangement by which that district used to hold the work, and she said it standing at the gate.**

She said that the yard is still swept at six and not at seven and that the bus is still let out of the bay at the hour it always was and that nobody in this district stopped doing any of that on the Monday the gate opened at five instead of six.

She said that what stopped was the part where the district paid for somebody to stand there and be paid nothing, and that nobody ever wrote down which part that was, and that four hundred people had to be told by somebody walking past a gate which part had gone.

**She said that a man who walks past a gate every morning at ten past six can tell four hundred people which part has gone in about four seconds, and that nobody asked her to and that she did it anyway and that nobody thanked her.**

A man of about thirty-four said in nine seconds that this was the second time in a fortnight that a person had done something in this district that nobody had arranged and had not been thanked for it, and that nobody had said the number of those out loud and that he was not going to be the first.

---

**And the other half of that Friday was about nine weeks, and there are two of them and nobody has compared them.**

A man of about fifty-two said in nine seconds that the yard would go on for about nine weeks after nobody had told it to stop, and that this was not a plan and was a habit, and that a habit does not stop on a Tuesday.

A woman of about thirty-four said in four seconds that the second nine weeks is a different nine weeks and belongs to a different figure, and that nine people in this city have a nine weeks in their head and that none of them has ever compared the two.

Nobody in that yard asked her to compare them and nobody has compared them since.

---

**And the last half hour of that Friday in that yard was about who pays, and nobody answered it, and four hundred people use that thing and nine of them have asked since.**

A woman of about thirty-one said that the district paid the yard to hold the work and that the district has not paid it since the Monday its gate opened at five instead of six, and that the yard has not asked anybody for anything and has not mentioned it.

She said that this is a thing about four hundred people know and that not one of them has said to another one, and that a thing four hundred people each know alone is not four hundred facts and is one fact with nobody in it.

A man of about fifty-two said in nine seconds that a fact with nobody in it is the same shape as the thing that body cannot hold, and that this city has now two of those and that it has had one for eleven years.

**He said that nobody in that yard was going to be the second end of anything this morning and that this was not a criticism and that he had said it because about four of them were about to leave.**

---

**A man of about twenty-two was in that yard for about forty minutes and said nothing and was asked nothing, and he has said since that he watched a man hold something eleven seconds long nine times, that he counted them, and that his own hands would not hold steady for two of them.**

He has also said that he was asked one thing by the man of about forty-seven and did not repeat it, and that the thing he was asked was whether a person could do that job with hands that will not hold steady, and that he said no in four seconds.

He has said that this is the first time in about eleven years he has been asked a question he could answer about his own hands and that nobody asked him a second question about it.

---

**The rest of that Friday was a counter until about six and about two hours of work in a first district, where a set of weighbridge loops on a road had been buried in mud at the kerb line.**

Weighbridge loops sit flush in a carriageway in a cast frame with a slot round them, and the slot collects whatever the road collects, and a slot that has not been cleared fills and then lifts the frame.

He lifted two frames out with a bar, got a vacuum and a wire out of the slots and about four shovels of grit out of the frames, and cleared the same four slots at the ends of the deck, and put the frames back on new bedding.

Thirty-one pounds. He told the man that a weighbridge loop is a trap for everything that comes down a road and that four sets of loops on that road have slots packed and that on one of them somebody has tarred over the slot.

---

**And the second fault on that road was a kerb line where about nine metres had been patched with a cold compound four winters running and had come away at both ends.**

He sawed the whole length out to sound material, squared the ends, and relaid it on a bedding of the same compound laid thinner and with a haunch at each end, because a patch in a kerb fails at the ends and not in the middle.

Thirty-one pounds. He told the man that a kerb that has been patched four times has never once been patched to last, and that four lengths of kerb on that road have been and that one of them is now a ramp.

---

**And a set of four steel bollards at a yard entrance in the same district had been set in a shallow hole in made ground with about four inches of concrete under them.**

He dug out each bollard, went down to the bottom of the hard ground underneath, bedded each one on a pad of concrete that reaches it, and grouted the collar.

Twenty-four pounds for four. He told the man that a bollard in four inches of concrete in made ground is a bollard that leans in about four winters, and that four bollards at that entrance have been reset twice and that nobody in that yard has counted the resettings.

---

**And a set of about eleven metres of palisade fencing along that yard had been put up with its posts in the same shallow ground, which is the reason four lengths of fencing in that district lean.**

He took eleven posts out, went down through the made ground to the hard ground, and refitted each post in a concrete collar of about four hundred millimetres depth, and re-tensioned the mesh along the length.

Thirty-one pounds. He told the man that fencing is not lighter than a bollard because fencing is thinner, and that about four lengths of fencing in that district are in made ground and that on one of them two posts have come out and gone under a lorry.

---

**And the fourth of those four rooms has a woman of about thirty at her chair from half past six, her door shut, and the eighth of eight has been on the back shelf behind her for two hundred and eighty-four days and has not been read.**

---
