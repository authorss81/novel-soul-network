# Chapter 813 — A Wednesday, A Bench Until Two And A Counter Until About Six, And A First Floor Above A Line In Saltmarket Where A Green Book Was On Sixty-Six Lines Before That Evening And On Sixty-Seven Lines After The Hour And On Sixty-Six Lines Again By About Half Past Seven, And Nobody In That Room Said A Word About It

**A Wednesday began at a bench, went through a counter, and finished on a first floor above a line in Saltmarket, where about nine people came up a stair between half past six and seven. One line went into a green book that evening and nobody in that room used the word opening, and the tin on the same table did not move.**

---

Thirteen names and thirteen dates went onto that Wednesday's board, and the thirteenth name at twenty to five. At ten on that Wednesday the shutter came down, which is the hour it comes down at on every day of this stretch of days.

**Line eighteen is one thousand two hundred and four days, a hundred and seventy-two weeks to the day. The man of about fifty-one against that north wall is one thousand and ninety-two days, a hundred and fifty-six weeks to the day. What is still owed is one thousand one hundred and seventy-six days, a hundred and sixty-eight weeks to the day.**

---

**The count was said once, in about nine seconds, in her face, by the woman of about sixty, and it was sixty-four of which fifty-nine, and she said it the way she says the date at the top of a page.**

Nobody in that room said a number back to her and nobody asked her where the number came from and about two of the nine have said since that she has never once been asked and that this is not a rule anybody wrote down.

A man of about thirty-four said in about nine seconds that he had brought a page, and that the page had been in his coat since before the spring, and that he had put it into the book at the top of that evening without saying so and had told nobody.

The woman of about sixty said in four seconds that she had known, and that she had known since about the second week, and that she had not asked him for it because he had not offered it and because a person who is not offered a thing does not have to be thanked for not having it.

**Nobody thanked him for it and nobody said that the page should have been brought earlier and four people in that room thought both of those things and said neither.**

---

**And the page was read out in that room on that Wednesday evening by four people in turn, and what was on it is not set down here, and nobody in that room has said one word about it since.**

It took about nine minutes. It was not in a hand anybody recognised and it was not signed and it carried no date of its own, and about four people in that room said afterwards that a thing with no date on it cannot be checked and that a thing with no name on it cannot be asked about.

A man of about fifty-two said in about nine seconds that a page with no date and no name is worth more in that book than a page with both, and that the reason is that nobody can take it back out on the grounds of a mistake.

A woman of about forty-eight said in four seconds that she kept a book of about nine years and that in it there is a column for the date and a column for the name and that a page with neither in it would be the first thing she has ever had to decide about and that she has not decided.

**Nobody in that room decided it and nobody in that room asked anybody else to decide it, and the book is on sixty-seven lines and the woman of about sixty has not said one word about the sixty-seventh line.**

---

**And a woman of about thirty-four asked the one question anybody asked that evening, and she asked it in about nine seconds and she asked it to the room and not to the man who had brought the page.**

She asked whether the four hundred people in that district were told that the page had gone in, and the man of about thirty-four said that they were not and that about nine people had been told and that he had not told those nine either.

She said that a page going into a book in a room nine people are sitting in is not the same thing as four hundred people knowing, and that the difference between those two things is about nine years in this city and that the nine years is the whole of the distance.

A man of about thirty-eight said that the four hundred have been told four times since the spring and that the fifth thing in a book on a Wednesday evening is not going to be the one that reaches them, and that he was not saying that to be clever.

**Nobody in that room asked him how the fifth thing is going to reach them and about four people in that room have said since that they wanted to ask him and did not.**

---

**And the tin on that table was not opened on that Wednesday and nobody in that room asked to have it opened, and two of the nine have said since that they have never once wanted to.**

A man of about thirty-one said in about nine seconds that the tin is a thing that gets counted and that nobody has ever been shown what is in it, and that this has been the arrangement for two months and that it is the only arrangement in that room nobody has ever argued about.

The woman of about sixty said in four seconds that the lid stays on because the lid is how she knows the count is the count, and that a count a person can look inside is a count a person starts arguing about, and that she would rather have one number nobody could check than two that half the room could.

**Nobody in that room put the two numbers on that table together in any way, and four people in that room have said since that they wanted to do that arithmetic for two months and have not.**

A man of about fifty-two said in four seconds that the place behind her chair had stood empty since a week in the spring, and that nobody in that room has ever asked her about it, and that this is the longest a question has gone unasked in a room where questions are the only thing anybody does.

---

**And what four people did after they read that page out is on the record here and the record is the whole of it.**

Nobody in that room wrote the page down. Nobody in that room described it afterwards to anybody outside it, and two people in that room have said since that they have described it to each other in a corridor and that the two accounts of its length do not agree.

A man of about thirty-eight said in about nine seconds that a thing nobody writes down is a thing that has to be remembered by nine people, and that nine people remembering a thing is nine chances of it changing, and that this is what has happened to every other thing in this city that was never written down.

A woman of about twenty-nine said in four seconds that the same nine people are the reason it has not changed as fast as it would have with one, and that four of the nine would have got it on their own and that nine would have got it together.

Nobody in that room agreed with her about which of those two numbers mattered and nobody in that room said that it mattered.

---

**A man of about twenty-two was in the corridor outside that room for about fifty minutes with the door open and did not go in, and he has said since that he could hear nine people breathing and nothing else.**

---

**And about half an hour of that Wednesday evening was in that corridor, and four people stood in it and did not go in either, and one of the four was a man of about twenty-nine.**

He said that he had come up that stair with a thing to say and that the thing to say was for the room and that the room was the wrong room for it.

He said that about half an hour ago a page went into that book without anybody saying so and that nobody has ever told anybody in that room that a page exists, and that he had assumed the page would be the thing that brought nine people in.

He said in nine seconds that a page does not bring anybody anywhere and that a date does, and that four hundred people have a date and nine of them have a page.

**Nobody in that corridor said the page out loud and nobody in that corridor asked about the sixty-seventh line, and four people in that corridor have said since that they wanted to and that it was not the moment.**

He has also said that he was told one thing by a man of about thirty-four in the corridor and did not repeat it, and that the thing he was told was that the page had been in a coat since before the spring, and that a woman of about thirty-four had already put the same fact to him in a corridor earlier that evening.

He said that being told the same fact twice by two different people in one week is not a coincidence in this city and that it is a sign that a thing has started.

---

**The rest of that Wednesday was a counter until about six and about two hours of work in a second district, where a shop's back door had been closing into the reveal and catching on the corner of a new shelf.**

A back door that closes into a reveal has to clear the reveal. A shelf fitted into the reveal takes about thirty millimetres off the swing and the door will close for nine days and then it will not.

He took the door off, planed the hinge stile back by six millimetres over the height of the door, packed the hinge side with three washers, refitted it, and ran it through its swing six times with a rule against the reveal.

Twenty-six pounds. He told the man that a door closing into a reveal is a door that has been closing into a reveal for about eleven years and that four doors in that lane have a shelf in the reveal and that one of them has been off its hinges twice.

---

**And the second fault was a set of hooks on a fire door that had been screwed into the face of the door and not into the frame stile.**

A hook for a coat on the face of a door is on the moving side and it carries the coat's weight across the gap every time the door opens. He took all four hooks out, filled the holes, fitted two new ones through the frame stile on the hinge side where they take weight on the frame, and left the other two where they were because they were holding nothing.

Nineteen pounds. He told the man that a coat hook on the face of a door works until somebody hangs a wet coat on it, and that four doors in that building have hooks in the face and that one of them has a door that is out of square because of it.

---

**And a length of worktop in the same shop had been butted against the wall without any form of fixing at the joint, and the joint had opened about a quarter of an inch along its whole length.**

He took the whole run down, planed the wall side of it back to sound, fitted a scribed timber cleat along the wall behind it, and refitted the run onto the cleat at three points and left the joint itself as a glued and scribed joint.

Thirty-one pounds. He told the man that a worktop end butted to a wall with nothing behind it is a joint that opens about a quarter of an inch in two winters and that four runs in that shop are butted and that one of them has a paper knife in it.

---

**And a window in the same shop had been painted shut, which is the fourth time in about nine years that any window in that building has been painted shut.**

He broke the seal with a plastic wedge and a thin piece of card, cleaned the rebate out with a wire brush, and repacked the sash with a length of tapered wood shim rather than with paper.

Eleven pounds. He told the man that a painted window is a window somebody has decided is not a window, and that four windows in that building have been painted and that nobody in that building has a list of which four.

---

**And a length of drain pipe on the back of that shop had been let into a wall joint instead of into a hole, and had been packed with a strip of rag to stop the sound of it.**

He took the pipe off the wall, cut a proper hole with a rotary cutout, fitted a slotted drain bracket round the pipe so the back cannot sit against the wall, refitted it, and ran a length of sealant round the joint where the wall had been cut into.

Twenty-four pounds. He told the man that a pipe let into a wall joint is a pipe let into a crack, and that four downpipes on that lane are in cracks and that one of them has been packed with rag and that the rag is wet and will stay wet.

---

**And the fourth of those four rooms has a woman of about thirty at her chair from half past six, her door shut, and the eighth of eight has been on the back shelf behind her for two hundred and seventy-five days and has not been read.**
