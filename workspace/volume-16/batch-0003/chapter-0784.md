# Chapter 784 — A Friday, A Bench Until Two And A Counter Until About Six, And A Man Of About Fifty-Two Who Asked Four People The Same One Question And Was Given Four Pieces Of Paper In About Nine Minutes, And Four Places In This City Where A Practice Is Written Down Where Every One Of The Four Was Written Down By Somebody Doing Something Else

**A Friday began at a bench, went on at a counter, and finished in a room off a road in a second district. A man asked one question of four people and came away with four documents in nine minutes, and not one of the four believed him before they had answered.**

---

The board for that Friday carried ten names and ten dates, and the last name was taken at twenty to five. The shutter came down at ten, and the ten names on that board were the same ten as the Monday before, and nobody has ever found out why.

**Line seventeen is one thousand two hundred and eleven days, one hundred and seventy-three weeks to the day. The post at that corridor end is nine hundred and eighty-seven days, one hundred and forty-one weeks to the day.**

**Eight hundred and nineteen days is the separation, one hundred and seventeen weeks to the day, in a one-line box about sixteen months old, and that number has gone on being correct since and does not know what it is counting.**

The four rooms off that service road stand at one thousand four hundred and thirty-nine days, two hundred and five weeks and four days.

---

**The question the man of about fifty-two asked was the same question four times. It was seven words long. It was not a good question and it worked.**

He asked four people: the woman who sweeps that yard at six, the man who reads the dial on that line, the man of about thirty-four who keeps the haulier books, and a woman of about thirty-eight who signs things for that district.

The question was: in this city, where is his name written down in a thing that is not about the yard.

**None of the four believed he was asking a real question. The woman who sweeps thought it was about a form. The man who reads the dial thought it was about money. The man of about thirty-four thought it was about that notice. The woman of about thirty-eight did not say what she thought it was, and has not said since.**

They all answered anyway, in nine minutes between them, and all four of them were wrong about what he was doing and exact about the answer.

---

**The first of the four places is a return sheet, and the return sheet is a piece of paper about a load of bar stock and about eleven crates of hinges.**

A supplier's paper has a space at the bottom for the name of whoever took the delivery. That space is on every one of four hundred sheets a year and it is filled in by whoever happens to be at the gate. For about eleven years the name in that space has been the man of about forty-seven, and it is there four times a week in his own hand, because he is at that gate when the bar stock comes.

**His name is on two hundred returns in a filing arrangement in a building he has been inside nine times in eleven years. It is on them as the man who took the load. Not one of those two hundred returns carries any other mark, and not one of them carries a line on it.**

The man of about fifty-two read four of them out of a drawer that took him about a minute and then put the drawer back the way he had found it, and he has said that he put it back that way deliberately.

---

**The second of the four places is a gate rota, printed on a sheet of stiff paper for a week, made seven days ahead, and hung on a nail in a hut at the gate that opens at five.**

There is a column for the man on the early gate and a column for whoever covers him. In the Thursday cell of a rota from four years ago there is his name, and beside it there are two initials in a different hand, which are the initials of the man who covered him for one Thursday because of a hospital appointment.

**Those two initials are the only other person ever recorded next to him on any piece of paper in this city. Whoever wrote them was filling in a rota. The man who wrote them has never been asked about it, has said nothing about it, and has not been near that hut in four years.**

The man of about fifty-two read that rota standing up inside the hut in two minutes, put it back on its nail, and turned it so that the front faced the wall, which is the way it hangs.

---

**The third of the four places is a school sports sheet, and it is the worst of the four and the one that made the room go quiet.**

It is a fixture list for a day in the spring before last. On the front there are fixtures, times, and a hut to be opened. On the back, in a hand that belongs to somebody about eleven years old, there is a list of names written down to settle an argument, and the last name on that list is the man of about forty-seven, and beside it, in the same hand, there is a time.

The time is the time the yard is cleared. It is the only one of those times on the back of that sheet that is not a bus.

**Nobody wrote it there in order to record anything. Somebody wrote it there because a child on that sheet was told by a parent that a man held a line at a yard on that road and did not believe it, and wrote it down to win, and the child has since grown up and does not remember writing it and would not claim it.**

The man of about fifty-two was shown that sheet by the woman of about thirty-eight, who had it in a bag. He did not take it away. He asked her to put it back where it came from, and she did.

---

**The fourth of the four places is a page in a hard-backed book about a gate, and that book is in the hut at that gate, and this work has been in that hut, and the man who wrote it is on no list in this city.**

The book is four years old. It has one column for the time the gate was opened, one for the time it was shut, and one for the man on duty, and a man has filled it in every working day for four years, and he does it because the gate is his and nobody asked him to.

On one page, in the column for the man on duty, the man of about forty-seven's name sits as it sits on two hundred other pages. Beside it, once, in the same hand, there are five words.

The five words are: *and the line, same as always.*

**Nobody can say when that was written. The man who keeps that book has been asked about it once, in about nine seconds, by the man of about fifty-two, and he said that he wrote down what he did, and that if the man on duty also did something else then that was also what he did.**

He is on no list. He has never been on a list. He is a man who opens a hut at about a quarter to five, fills in a book about a gate, and is paid for the hut.

---

**And then the woman of about thirty-four said the thing that turned four searches into a fact, and she said it in nine seconds, and she said it to the table.**

"**In none of those four was it written down on purpose,**" she said. "**Two hundred returns, a rota, a child's argument and a gate book. In this city the man has been written down four times by accident and never once on purpose, and the reason nobody can find it is that nobody has ever been looking for it, because nobody has ever needed it to be found.**"

Nobody in that room had anything to add to that. Four people wrote it down, two of them as a sentence and two of them as a heading.

---

**What is also a fact, and what came out of that room on that Friday, is that two people in this city have known all four of those places for years.**

They have never put them together. They told a man of about thirty-eight the four of them, in that order, at some point in the last two years, and he did not believe them and has said so, and one of them keeps a book and the other one does not.

**Nobody in that room asked who the two are. The man of about thirty-eight was asked and said he could name them and was not going to, and nobody argued with him about it, and two people in that room have since said that he should have.**

---

**A man of about twenty-two was in that room for an hour and forty minutes, was told about all four of the places, said nothing about any of them, and was asked about none of them.**

He went out at about half past seven, walked to the end of that road, and stood at the gate for four minutes. Then he went home. He has said since that he wanted to go into that hut and look at the book himself and did not, and that the reason was that the man who writes that book fills it in every working day and would have known.

---

**The rest of that Friday was a counter until about six and forty minutes on a gate closer at a school in a second district, which is that school, and which is where the sports sheet is, and where nobody said one word about the sheet.**

He put a new pivot in and charged twelve pounds, and the caretaker told him about the argument on the back of the fixture list without being asked, and he did not tell the caretaker that he knew.

On the way out he stopped at the office window and looked at the fixture list on the board inside it, and the list on the board is a different sheet from the sheet in the bag, and the caretaker said so, and he said he had assumed as much.

---

**And the man who keeps the gate book is a man you can describe entirely, and that is the reason the fourth place is the fourth and not the first.**

He is at that hut by a quarter to five because he is a quarter to five is when that gate is unlocked. He has a kettle. He has a stool. He has a shelf with three things on it, of which one is a box of tea and two are parts of the gate he took off two winters ago and has not thrown away, because they might go back on and because throwing a part away is a thing a man does once and then cannot do again in his own head.

He has never been asked to keep the book. He keeps it because there is a column in it for the man on duty and he is the man on duty. When the man of about fifty-two asked him about the five words he said that he wrote down what he did, and he has since said, to one person on a road, that if the man on duty also did a second thing then that was also what he did, and that this is not a decision and is only arithmetic.

**Nobody has ever told him that four people in this city know where that book is. He would not know what to do with that, and has said so in advance, in about four words, to a man who was selling him a kettle.**

---

**And the school sheet has a second thing on the front of it that nobody has ever asked about, and the man of about fifty-two saw it and said nothing about it to anybody that week.**

The front of that fixture list has a column for the person who opens the hut, and against every match day of the spring before last the man of about forty-seven's name is in that column, and beside it, in the same column, in a different hand, somebody has written the word *again* four times.

He read it standing up in the corridor of that school and he read it twice. He has said that a man who puts the word again in a column four times in one season is a man who has been asked to do a thing he does not want to do, four times, and has run out of ways of saying no that are still polite.

He did not write it down. He has said that he did not write it down because he could not think of a piece of paper that a thing like that belongs on, and that he has thought about it since and has still not thought of one.

The caretaker has since told two people that the word again in that column was not about the man at all. Neither of the two has asked what it was about, and the caretaker has not offered, and four people in this city have now heard both versions and have not put them together.

---

**The fourth of those four rooms has a woman of about thirty at her chair from about half past six with her door shut, and the eighth of eight has been on the back shelf behind her for two hundred and twenty-three days.**
