# Chapter 175 — It Was Never Withdrawn

Off a street in Lattice Ward there is a room that anybody may walk into and fill in a slip, and on the Wednesday morning of week fifty-six a woman of forty-five with a braid of tools in her pocket came into it and put a slip on the counter with four lines on it and a name at the bottom, and the name was Sera Quill, and it was the first slip in nine years that had come off a card.

The card said *Marrow Falls, emergency protocol, working copy, four volumes*.

She keeps her own book. Four hundred return contacts in eleven years, every one with a location and a date on it, on a bench in Lattice Ward, in her own hand, in a book that has never been on a system. She said one sentence about that at the counter and did not say it again.

Wendla Ossick is forty-one and she is in that room on the Monday, the Wednesday and the Friday, and she put her hand flat on the slip and read the four lines and did not pick the tray up.

"**A box does not come down without a slip, and a slip is a piece of paper and a person.** That is the rule and I say it to everybody in the first four minutes and I am not going to make an exception of you and I am certainly not going to make one of him."

---

The box came off the top of the trolley on its castors, and the third of those castors has been bad since before she came, so when the box came down the whole trolley moved about an inch and four inches of the third shelf down came with it.

There is a bound volume on that shelf. It is not in a box and it is not in a drawer and it has no card, because it is a book anybody may read, which is why it has been on the open shelf of that trolley for nine years.

It came to the edge. Nobody touched it.

"That is the Civic Compact," said Wendla Ossick. "**The printed one. It is not in a box and it does not need a slip and anybody in this city can walk in off that street and read it, and it is three shelves down, and it has been three shelves down for nine years.**"

---

The working copy is four volumes in a box and the box was on the counter and the lid came off, and the second volume had a card in it with a line on the card and a place and a Wednesday on it, and at the fourth section of the second volume there was a page stapled in over a printed line, and the staple was not new.

The printed line underneath it said: *4. Every technique must show its exit, in a place the participant can find it, before the technique begins.*

The page on top of it said, in a different printing and in no other hand at all: *4. Where a technique does not answer within the time set for it, the last approved civic sequence for that practice shall stand in its place, and a participant who does not answer shall be treated as not having withheld.*

There were no initials on it. There was no date on it. There was no name on it anywhere on the page, and the staple that held it had gone into the sheet before the sheet was printed, because the sheet is a working copy and working copies are run off again, and the staple is older than the ink around it and both of them are older than the plate on a door in Bower Street.

---

Wendla Ossick read both lines out, because she reads things out.

Then she said the thing that is the whole of this volume, and she said it to the room.

"**Nobody has broken a rule.** That page is in a working copy. The requirement is not withdrawn, it is in print, in the original words, in this room, three shelves down, on a book that anybody can walk in off a street and read. It has been in print the whole fourteen years. What has happened is that this city has been operating outside a live requirement for fourteen years, and the reason it is fourteen years and not four is that the only copy anybody has ever opened is the copy in the box."

Nobody said anything for a while. A trolley with a bad wheel is a very quiet object.

---

Sera Quill had not touched either volume.

She had read the printed line first, on the open shelf, in two seconds, and then she had read the stapled page twice, and then she had put her hand flat on the counter on the side of the box away from the page and left it there.

Nobody asked her how she knew. A man of twenty-two, who has a slip of his own in that tray with his name at the bottom of it from a Thursday a fortnight ago and who had no reason to be in the room on a Wednesday, did not ask her, because he has known for a term and a half that the answer to a question of that shape is a sentence that cannot be unsaid in a room with a counter in it.

"And I am not going to tell you why," said Sera Quill.

"You do not have to."

"**I want to be clear that I am not going to, and I want you to notice that you did not ask twice, which is the first time in a term and a half that you have not asked twice.**" She did not move her hand. "**And the reason I am not going to is the same reason it was the last time, and it is a thing about a person in this city and not about a practice, and four hundred people in this district would know the reason inside a week, and nine of them would be frightened, and there is nothing in that sentence that is for you.**"

That is the same nine words and the same refusal and she did not better either of them, and nobody in the room asked her a second question, and the name was not said in that room and was not said anywhere else.

---

Then she did the thing that made the rest of it possible, and she did it without being asked.

"**Take the other one down.**"

Wendla Ossick did not move.

"Miss Quill, there is a rule about what comes off that trolley and I am the one who says what comes off it, and that book has been on that shelf for nine years and it does not need a slip because it is not a box, and if I take it down and put it on that counter then for about eleven minutes this room is a room with a working copy and a public copy on it at the same time, and nobody has ever read them in the same room, and I have been in this room for nine years and I have not read the public one."

"You have not read it."

"**I have not read it. I have read the card catalogue, four hundred times, and I have brought the tray out to about forty people who asked for a binder in nine years and put every card back, and I never once took the book off the shelf three shelves down, and I did not do it because it is a big book and it is heavy and there is a bell on this counter and it is nine years of my own habit and nobody ever asked me about it.**"

She said the last part looking at the counter.

"**That is the whole of it. A rule is only as real as the copy people open, and I have been standing behind this counter for nine years with the rule three shelves under my elbow, and the person in this city who has said the most about that rule in a term and a half is a man of twenty-two who has never once been in this room when it was open.**"

---

The registrar reached over, lifted the printed volume off the edge of the shelf and set it down beside the open box, and the two of them lay about nine inches apart on a marble top, and for about eleven minutes in a room off a street in Lattice Ward there was a printed requirement that every technique must show its exit and a page saying that where a technique does not answer, the last approved civic sequence shall stand in its place, and both of them were in print, and neither of them had a name on it, and one of them had a staple in it older than the ink around it.

Then the woman of forty-one, who is the only person in that room, said what she was going to do about it before anybody asked her and in front of the two of them, and she was not fast about it and she was not slow.

"**I have not got a column for this and I am not going to rule one. I have got a counter, a bell, a trolley, about four hundred boxes, four hundred cards and twelve slips in a tray, and none of those is a place for two documents having been on a counter at once, and if I write it down anywhere then it is a piece of paper with the name of a woman of forty-five on it, and a piece of paper with that name on it is the worst object in this city and I have been in this room for nine years and I know that.**"

"Then what is going to happen."

"What is going to happen is that I am going to say out loud, to the two of you, and to nobody else, and once: a woman of forty-five and a man of twenty-two had two documents out of this room at the same time this morning. **And that is the record. It is in a head in a cardigan on a Wednesday and it is not going anywhere and in about four years one of us will have forgotten it and the other will not, and that is the whole of the arrangement I have.**"

---

They put the box back on the high shelf and the book back three shelves down, and the trolley moved about an inch on its bad wheel, and the two of them were four inches apart again on a Wednesday morning and they will be for as long as that trolley stands in that room.

And a woman of forty-five with a braid of tools in her pocket said one more thing at the door and it was not to him.

"Nine hundred doors in four buildings in this city have got a company's number on them," she said, "**and there is a set of plans in a basement that says the other number, and you have now found out that the rules are still in print, and I want you to understand that neither of those things is a surprise to anybody who has ever read a form. What is new is that you have both of them in one room and neither of you can do a single thing with it.**"

---

At eleven that night there were ten people in a back room, and the tin in the middle of the table had no lock on it, and the book lying in front of Bea Nunn had four columns and forty-four lines in it, and it had not been opened since the Wednesday of week forty-eight.

She said the rule before anybody asked, in one go, the way she says it.

"**A book of what a caller said cannot be written by the people who did not call.** That is the rule, and it is older than me, and it was here before the tin, and it is not a mood and I am not going to be asked whether it is a mood. The book is forty-four lines. It is not a register. It is not going to be forty-five tonight and it is not going to be forty-five this year."

A woman who had been in the room in week fifty-two said that she had come back to ask a different question, and that it was a small one.

"**Has anybody ever read it.**"

"No," said Bea Nunn. "Not in nine years. The fortieth line was written three years ago by a man who rang me and said nine words and signed his own name at the bottom of them, and he is the last one, and line forty-four has a hole in it where a woman of sixty-eight in bay four should be, and she was never written down and she is not going to be."

"You said in the spring that a practice can write about itself in the first person and nothing else can."

"I did, and it means what it says, and you came four miles for it and I have not got a better sentence now than I had then and I have had about four months to find one." Bea Nunn turned the tin a quarter turn. "**And here is the other half and nobody has asked me for it. Forty-four lines and a fortieth one from three years back and a hole in the forty-fourth is the most honest object in this city, and it is honest because it is nearly empty, and every week it stays nearly empty is a week somebody sat in a room and decided not to say a thing in order to be in the room. That is not a rule I made. That is only what a book is.**"

"Are you going to say that to anybody else."

"I said it to you. **I am not going to be a woman that people come to be told things by, and about twenty people have telephoned me in four weeks to ask whether a person can say anything in this room, and I have said yes twenty times, and I did not know what I was agreeing to the first eleven and I know now. A room where a person can say anything turns into a room where a person comes to be told something, and I do not have a rule for that half, and the half that is not a rule is the half that will do the damage.**"

"What stops it."

"Nothing. I do not decide who is a caller, and the other half of my rule is not a rule, and I have been told that by a man of thirty-four in this room and I agreed with him. **And if that second half ever starts to sound like a rule then somebody in about four years will be able to point at a rule I made up at eleven at night because nine people were in a room, and I am not going to be that.**"

"And nobody writes in it."

"Nobody writes in it who has not called. That is the whole of the rule and it is the reason it has been open nine years and shut six weeks." She put her hand on the cover. "**And since the Wednesday of week fifty-two nine more people have telephoned me to ask whether it is true that a person can say anything in this room, which makes it twenty, and I have said yes, and I did not know what I was agreeing to then and I know now.**"

The fifth chair was still against the wall and nobody moved it. The tin went back in the middle of the table with nothing in it. Nobody called and nobody signed.

---

There is a printed requirement in a room off a street in Lattice Ward that every technique must show its exit, and it is in the original words, and it has been in print for fourteen years, and about eleven thousand premises pay a levy that bought a third line about somebody who is not a named party to a share, and about four hundred and thirty-two cards are in about nine places in one district.

Not one person in this city has broken a rule. Everybody in this city has been living outside one, for fourteen years, on the strength of a copy nobody opens, and the only person who has now said so out loud says it at a counter three mornings a week, on her own time, with her hands flat on it.
