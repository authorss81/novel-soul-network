# Chapter 179 — Not One Of Them Wanted A Box

A room off a street in Lattice Ward, and a counter in it, and nineteen slips in a tray on the Wednesday morning of week fifty-seven, and a woman of forty-one who had counted them for the second time in a month and had decided to say the number out loud.

Four drawers under that counter with about four hundred cards in them, a trolley against the far wall with a high shelf and a wheel that has been bad since before she came in, and about four hundred boxes, and a bell on the counter that is a push and does not ring by itself.

Nobody designed any of what has happened in it. A foreman of fifty-eight came in in the week of week fifty-three with his own name at the bottom of a slip and has not been back. A man of thirty-four who works nights came in and stood. A boy of nineteen came in twice. A woman of forty-three came in from a room in Crown Terrace with a hall table at the end of a question.

---

The man of thirty-four came first, and he had asked for the room and not the stairwell, and he had said why on a Friday in week forty-seven over a tray of milk jugs.

He filled nothing in.

"You can say anything," said Wendla Ossick. "All of it, in your own words, in whatever order it comes out, and nobody in this room will write it down and nobody in this room will stop you. **There is no rule in this room against talking. The rule is against the book.**"

"I know that now," said the man of thirty-four. "**Somebody rang a woman of sixty-one in four weeks and told her the same thing, and twenty people have rung her, and I am one of them, and I did not ring to be told. I rang because I wanted to be told by somebody who has never heard of me.**"

---

He stood at the counter for four minutes and then asked one question, and it was not about a card.

"I have got nine words in biro on the back of a work card from the spring and I cannot hand them to anybody and I have tried for two months and I have not got a way of handing them over."

"Then it is a piece of paper."

"That is what I have," he said. "**And I have been standing in a corridor at ten past four in the morning this month with two sentences from two people who are both right and neither of them will give way, and I have got a rule and it is the same rule in a room with a counter in it and in a stairwell off a service road, and I do not know what to do with it, and I have come here because somebody said a person can say anything in a room, and I have now said it, and nothing has happened, and that is correct and I would like it written down that I knew it would be correct.**"

Nobody wrote it down. The bell on the counter is a push and it did not go.

---

The woman of forty-three came at about eleven and gave her name and where she works before she asked, because that is what she did in a room with a microphone on it and she does it now in a room without one.

"**I asked a question in Crown Terrace and it cost me a hall table and I have been told that I should have wanted it to cost me something, and I have not worked that out. I want to know whether a room like this is a place a person goes when they are frightened.**"

Wendla Ossick did not answer for four seconds, and then she answered in one go.

"No." She put her hand flat on the counter. "**Not this one. I work Mondays, Wednesdays and Fridays and I am in this building three days a week, and I am going to keep those hours whatever anybody says in this room, and on the other four days the door is open and there is nobody behind the counter and there is a strip light on a timer that goes off at half past four whatever is in the room.** A person who is frightened at ten o'clock on a Tuesday would come in here and find a bell and a tray, and that is worse than not coming, because they would have been somewhere."

"Then what is it for."

"It is for about four hundred boxes and a card for each one, and a box does not come down without a slip, and a slip is a piece of paper and a person. That is the rule, and it is a rule and it is not a mood, and it was a rule before I came." She squared the tray. "**And I am not going to be the reason a frightened person comes to a counter with a bell on it, because if I am, then in about four years I am a post, and there is no post in this city whose job it is to be here, and there is not going to be one, and the only thing I have got to stop that with is that I am a woman who works three days a week and I have said so out loud to a man with a carpenter's pencil in his ear and to nine people since.**"

---

The boy of nineteen from the shop in the Quiet Quarter came in at half past eleven and he had been in the room once before, in the week of week fifty-three, and he had asked a question then and been told the correct answer.

He asked it again.

"**Has anybody ever written down what a person said in a room like this and been believed.**"

"Two," said Wendla Ossick. "**In nine years. I liked four people and I wrote down two of them and I was wrong both times and both of them found out inside about a month, and I have never found out how, and I have never asked either of them.**"

"That is still the correct answer."

"It is the same answer," she said. "**You asked it in the week of the fifty-third and I have not got a better one and I have had a month. I have got nineteen slips in a tray and not one of them is about a box, and two hundred people have been through that door since the spring, and I am not going to tell you that is a good thing, and I am not going to tell you it is a bad thing, and if you want it to be one of those you are going to have to do it yourself and you will not be able to.**"

---

Then she said the thing she had come to say, which she had worked out on the Friday and had not told anybody.

"I have decided I am going to keep answering questions in this room that are not about a box." She put both hands on the counter. "**I have been not answering them for a fortnight, and I have written down on a piece of paper at home the four reasons I have for not answering them, and the fourth one is that the argument went round this city on the Tuesday of the week before last in a corridor off a service road and I was not in the corridor and I have not been in that corridor in my life.**"

Nobody in the room knew which corridor.

"Then somebody said it to you."

"Somebody said a version of it to me," said Wendla Ossick, "**in about nine words, and I have had a week to work out that my fourth reason is the same reason, and that means it is not mine, and that if I do not answer a question in this room because I am frightened of what a room like this becomes, then a woman of forty-five and a man of twenty-two have already had the argument and they have not let go of it either.**"

"So they are not the only one."

"**They are not the only one, and I am not going to find out whether they have been told, and I am going to answer the next question that is not about a box on the Friday, and if that is the whole of it then in about four years there will be nineteen slips in a tray with twelve of them about nothing at all and I will have said a number out loud twice.**"

---

The bell on that counter is a push and it does not ring by itself, and at about twenty past twelve the room was empty for about nine minutes and the strip light was on a timer and there were nineteen slips in a tray and about four hundred cards in four drawers and a trolley with a high shelf and a bad wheel.

She said the last thing of the morning to nobody, at the counter, with her hands flat on it, and it is in this book because she said it and I could not have bettered it.

"**Three weeks ago in this room there was one thing in the tray that was not about a box and it was mine, and this morning there are nineteen and none of them are about a box, and in about four years somebody is going to write a piece of paper about what this room is, and the piece of paper is not going to be right, and there is nothing in this city that can make it right, and the only thing standing in the way of it being badly wrong is that I am not going to be here on the other four days.**"

Before she said that, the woman of forty-three had told the rest of it, and she told it in a room with nobody in it but four people, and it took about six minutes.

"**I do the stairs at three addresses and one of them is a house I go into four days a week and the leaflet that went out in the spring has the address of that house on it. There is a hall table in that house with a card on it and four steps and a fifth line and my employer's name at the top, and the fifth line is the reason I can end something at work, and I have used it twice, and both times the person I ended it with said thank you and one of them said it in front of a colleague.**" She did not stop. "**I asked a question in a room in Crown Terrace and it cost me that hall table, in about nine words, and what I have worked out since is that the leaflet did not take the table and the microphone took the table, and I would like somebody to be able to tell me the difference between those two things, and nobody has, and I am not going to be the one who says it is not a big difference because it was a big difference.**"

A man of thirty-four left with nine words in biro and a rule and nothing. A woman of forty-three left with a no and a hall table. A boy of nineteen left with an answer he had come in twice for. And a woman of forty-one said a number out loud for the second time in a month, to nobody, at a counter, having said the first time to a man with a carpenter's pencil in her ear.

Nineteen slips in a tray, none of them about a box, behind about forty read-outs in nine years and about two hundred people through a door since the spring, and three mornings a week, and a bell that is a push and does not ring by itself, and a strip light on a timer that goes off at half past four whatever is in the room.
