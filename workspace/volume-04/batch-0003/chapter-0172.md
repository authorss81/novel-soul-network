# Chapter 172 — The Difference Is Who Pays

The packer lamp on the yard off the Meridian corridor had gone again on the Friday and it was still off on the Tuesday morning of week fifty-five, and the ballast was the ballast, and a man of twenty-two said both reasons out loud in a hut with the panel open because he has said them once already to the man who owns the panel.

There were nine men in the corridor behind him and the heater was on and the kettle was on and the site diary was flat on the desk under a beer mat.

A woman of twenty-four came in at half past ten with a bag, because there is a person in the welfare block on that yard she visits and the prefab is the only warm room on it, and she was in the corridor and not in the office, and that was where it happened.

---

It took four minutes to get to the middle of it and none of the four minutes was about the two of them.

"You have made a thing work for about a term and a half with nine people in it," she said, "and not one of them was told to do anything, and four hundred leaflets and a thousand sheets and a ring file and eleven slips in a tray are all of it running on somebody being in a room. **That is the thing I have spent four years telling rooms is possible. Not a service. A person. You have built the only version of it I have ever seen work and you have done it by refusing every single thing that would have made it a job.**"

Nine men in a corridor do not go quiet. They get something else.

"You want to know what I think of the method," he said.

"I have given you what I think of it. That was not the question."

"Then the method is this. **The difference is who pays.** Nobody pays. That is the whole of the finding and it took me a term and a half and nine days to get it down to one sentence and I have got it down to one sentence in a corridor and not anywhere else. The day somebody is paid to stand behind that counter, it is not a rule, it is a post. A post can be given things to do. A rule cannot, and the reason a rule cannot is that nobody is on the hook for it, and if nobody is on the hook for it then it is a habit, and a habit is the best thing in this city and it is also the most fragile thing anybody has ever built."

---

"That is not a difference, that is a wedding speech."

"It is the difference between a thing and a thing that people are paid to keep doing."

"No," she said. "**It is a difference between a thing that exists and a thing that has somebody's name on the invoice, and you have spent a term and a half being unfindable and I have spent four years being findable, and neither of those turned out to be a way of paying anybody.**"

Nobody in that corridor said anything. One of the nine put a mug down.

"Give me a ward," she said. "**Not a principle. A ward. There is a bay in a residential home in this city with four people on it and a woman of thirty-eight who works nights, and on the Tuesday of week thirty-eight she ended her own share in four seconds and nobody has ever asked her whether she was willing and nobody is ever going to, and the only route out of that bay is a counter on the ground floor of a building in Bower Street.** What is your version of that, and what does it cost, and who signs it."

He took a while.

"The version I have got is a man on a Friday at grade three going out to eleven jobs, and about eleven people in a week being taught to wait for somebody, and the number is on the back of my own card and it is not on a form anywhere in this company, and the reason it is not on a form is that the day it goes on a form it becomes a rate." He turned the pencil over. "**And that is a cost, and it is mine, and I put it there on purpose in a room with nine people in it in the Monday of week forty-two, and I have been paying it since and I am not going to tell you it is cheaper than yours.**"

"That is not the same problem."

"No," he said. "**It is the same shape with a different door on it, and I have been told that about myself in a corridor by a man of fifty-eight and it is the best thing anybody has said to me in a term and a half, and I did not believe it at the time.**"

"The reason your sentence is the better one," she said, "is that it is true. The reason mine is the better one is that yours has never had anybody standing behind it, and when you are not standing behind it in about two years it will be a woman of forty-one who is not standing behind it either, because she works three days a week, and then it is a room with a counter and a bell on it."

"And I know that. I have known it since she said it out loud to me in her own room, and she said she was going to keep saying it, and she was right, and I have not got an answer to it and I have had about four days to get one."

"Then say that instead of the sentence."

"I am saying it instead of the sentence."

---

He did not improve hers and she did not improve his, and neither of them said the word, and it was a corridor with nine men in it and a kettle going and a duty board with a fault on it and nobody was owed anything at the end of it.

She said the residue out loud before she went, which is what she does and which she does not do quietly.

"**There is going to be a room in about four years where somebody can say the name of the woman who said that, and there will be a hundred and forty leaflets on the table and about nine of them will be mine, and I will have said a true thing in a corridor off a service road and it will have been carried into a building by a boy who types.** And I have done that on purpose twice now and I am not going to do it a third time, and the reason I am not is that you are standing here and I would have to be fair to you about it first, and I have not got the time and the kettle is on."

He said nothing to that and she went up the stairs.

Then she said the cost, out loud, to the corridor, because she has never once said a cost quietly.

"**A rule that only works while a person is in the room is not a rule, and the reason I can say that without being a coward about it is that four hundred people in this district can name a man in a lanyard and nine of them can name a rule, and I have spent four years in rooms being the second one, and in about four years there is going to be a room where somebody can say the name of the woman who said it and there will be a hundred and forty leaflets on the table.**"

"And me."

"You are the other half of it and I have been in nine hundred leaflets' worth of rooms and you are the sentence in them." She shifted the bag. "I am going to say the other half back and I am not going to be fair about it, and then I am going upstairs, because I have a person in a welfare block and I did not come here for the argument."

"Go on."

"**Your rule is only real while you are the one standing behind it, and you have not got anybody else standing behind it, and you have spent three weeks refusing to have anybody else standing behind it, and so the honest position is that there is one of you.**"

---

He turned the panel back with the dead ways marked and did not leave it tidy, and a woman of twenty-four went up the stairs to a welfare block, and neither of them said one word about any of the four months before this week.

Then a man of thirty-four who works nights came in off the yard at ten to eleven and had four hours before he starts, and he had asked for the corridor, and he had heard the end of it.

"You have both said a true thing," he said. "**And I have got four people on my own floor who are taught every Friday that a release waits for a man, and I say it to them at ten past four in the morning, and I have got two sentences to say and both of them are true and one of them says a man is a man and the other one says a room is a room, and I am not allowed to use either of them twice in a week.**"

"So what do you want," Marek Senn said.

"I want one of you to be wrong," said the man of thirty-four. "**I have been two years in a trade where a person who is wrong about a card is a person you can go and find, and I have spent a term and a half working out that the opposite is true, and I have now been handed the opposite twice in a corridor by two people who do not know each other, and neither of them can give it up, and I am the one who has to say it at ten past four in the morning.**"

Nobody offered him a third sentence.

---

There are nine people in this city who can say a number out loud and one of them is a lift engineer with a cardstock and one of them is a man of thirty-four in a corridor who is about to have to say something at ten past four in the morning, and there are nine people in a corridor off a service road who have just heard two people who are both right, and there is no form in this city for that and there is not going to be one.

The kettle went on again at about eleven and a man of forty-four put the panel back the way it goes back and did not leave it tidy, because a tidy panel is a panel nobody opens for four years.

And at ten past four in the morning, about four hours after all of it, a man of thirty-four went out on his own floor and said one of the two sentences he had been given, and it was the one that says a room is a room, and he has not decided that it was the right one and he has not been told.
