# Chapter 170 — When She Is Not There

There is a ring file of about a thousand condition sheets in a cupboard on a ground floor in another part of this city and it has been nineteen years old and it belongs to nobody in particular, and one sheet from the week of week fifty had fourteen lines in pencil on the back of it, upside down relative to the front, in the space where the conditions go.

The woman who does the sheets up is thirty-six and has done it for about nine years and she turns every sheet over, because everybody turns a sheet over, and on the Tuesday of the week of fifty-four she asked what the pencil was.

---

Ines Kolar said what she had said on the Thursday of week fifty that she would say.

"There was a discrepancy and I wrote it down in pencil on my own sheet and it has not been actioned," she said. "**That is true and it is not enough and it does not help you, and I am sorry about the second half of that.**"

The woman of thirty-six turned the sheet over again and looked at the fourteen lines.

"Fourteen," she said. "**Nine in the first building and three in the second and two in a stairwell in Saltmarket that a company plated in the year the stairwell was new, and that is the worst of them because that one is not anybody's mistake.**"

"**You have read it properly. Nobody has ever read that sheet properly.**"

---

"And what happens to it."

"**I am going to tell you what I have got and then I am going to tell you the part that is going to cost me, because I worked the second half out on the Monday of that week and I have not stopped working it out since.**"

She put her hand flat on the edge of the ring file, which is not a thing she does.

"**I have got a book. It is a book about the building and it has got a column for the rooms and a column for the conditions and no column for this, because there is no column for this and there is no form anywhere in this city that takes a discrepancy, and I am the third person to want one in about four years and the first two did not know they wanted it.**"

---

"What will you write in it."

"**I will write in it that there are fourteen items on one sheet in the week of week fifty, that there is no column, and that they have not been actioned.**" She closed the file. "**That is the correct action. I have thought about it for four days and it is correct and it changes nothing, and in four years there will be nothing on that page at all and the pencil will have come off, and that is not anybody's fault and it is not anybody being wrong, and I am going to write it in a book because the alternative is not writing it, and a ring file of a thousand sheets is worse.**"

"Will you ask her to do it again."

"No," said Ines Kolar. "**There is nothing to ask her to do again. She has a trolley and a bucket and four cloths and forty-one doors a week, and a second sheet would be a second thing in a file that nobody opens, and I am not going to hand a woman of forty-six a job.**"

---

And then the woman of thirty-six asked the question that was going to cost it, and Ines Kolar answered it before she had finished asking it.

"**You have not said who else knows about the pencil.**"

"A man of twenty-two asked me a question in a corridor in the week of forty-seventh that my sheet could not answer," said Ines Kolar. "**His name is Marek Senn and he did not ask me to write anything down and I have never told him what I was going to do with it, and I have not mentioned it to him since. That is your second time of asking and it is the last thing I am going to say about it and I am going to say it now so that it is in your head and not in a book.**"

"Who is he."

"**A repairer. He is twenty-two and he has a docket and a lanyard and about eleven people a week are being taught to wait for him, and I have known that since the Thursday and I have not said it out loud to him and I am not going to.**"

---

Nobody thanked her. She is forty-six and she has been putting a sheet in a ring file for nineteen years and she has never once been thanked, and a woman of thirty-six wrote a line in a book and that is the whole of what came of fourteen lines in pencil.

There is a name in a corridor in a city where nobody is going to say whose practice this is, and the only places it is written down are in two women who do sheets up and doors, and a man of twenty-two does not know that it is anywhere, and is not going to find out this term.

---

There is a counter with a bell on it in a room off a street in Lattice Ward, and on the Thursday morning of the week of fifty-four there was a woman of forty-one behind it, and it is not one of her three days.

She said why before he was through the door.

"I have got a key and I have got the day off and I have been in this room nine years on Mondays, Wednesdays and Fridays," said Wendla Ossick, "**and I came in on a Thursday because I wanted to find out whether a person who is not asking me for a box would still find a person at this counter. That is a thing I have been wanting to find out for about two weeks and there was no way of finding it out on a Wednesday, because on a Wednesday I am here anyway.**"

"That is a reason for being here and not a rule."

"**It is a reason and it is not a rule and I am going to be careful about the difference all morning, because there are eleven slips in that tray that I did not put there and I have not got a rule for any of them.**"

---

He filled one in at eleven o'clock and then he did a thing that has not happened in that room in nine years.

He wrote across all four lines in one sentence, in the first person, in his own words, and it did not fit the four things the sheet had been printed for, and he signed it at the bottom.

*What you want:* I have got a card that nobody has made. It has got five things on it and four of them are filled in and the fifth one is the only one that matters, and I have not been able to fill it in for a term and a half, and nobody has ever asked me to. From when: the spring. To when: still. Who is asking: Marek Senn, twenty-two, a repairer, and I am not going to put a company on it, and I have written it long because it is one thing and your four lines are four.

"**That is a request,**" said Wendla Ossick.

"Yes."

"**It goes in the tray and it stays in the tray, and if anybody comes in with a question about a card that has not been made I will put it in front of them and I will not have to decide anything.**" She squared it. "**You have written one thing across four lines and the four lines are about four things, and the room has taken it anyway, and I want you to know that I did not have to be nice about that and I have not been and this is the same rule that has been in this room for nine years.**"

---

"The rule."

"A box does not come down without a slip, and a slip is a piece of paper and a person," she said. "**It is a rule and it is not a mood, and it was a rule before I came, and I have said it to you in about eleven seconds.**"

She put two fingers on the tray without lifting anything out of it.

"**I want you to have both of my numbers, because I have got two and they do not go together. About forty people have asked to see a binder in nine years and I have brought the tray out to every one of them and put the cards back, and that is the whole of what this room was built for. About two hundred people have been through that door since the spring, and about a hundred and sixty of the two hundred wanted a box, and eleven of them filled in a slip before you walked in and not one of those eleven is about a box, and four of them asked me a question and I answered all four, and I have not got a rule for any of it, and I have said this rule to all two hundred of them and I do not enjoy saying it.**" She looked at the slip under his hand. "**There is a foreman's name at the bottom of one of these from the Wednesday before and there is a repairer's name at the bottom of this one, and nobody has ever come in here and written one thing across four lines in the first person and signed it at the bottom, and I have been at this counter for nine years and I did not know until this morning that it was allowed.**"

"What does the room have for me."

"**It has about four hundred boxes and about four hundred cards, one for each box, and every card has got a box number on it and a date and a place and a hand in a shorthand and a shelf, and the hand is a person who put it there a long time ago. A card does not leave this room. A box does, and a box needs a slip, and you have got a slip.**" She closed the drawer. "**And there is no card in here for a card that has not been made, because there is nothing to write on the top line of it and there is no shelf to put it on, and there is not going to be one, and that is not a refusal. That is what the room is.**"

"Then it is nothing."

"It is a piece of paper with your name on it in a tray with eleven other pieces of paper," said Wendla Ossick, "**and in four years somebody will be able to say that a man of twenty-two came in here on the Thursday of the week of fifty-four and asked, and that is a thing that has never been able to be said about this room before, and I have not got a single thing I can do with it and neither have you.**"

---

At about eleven twenty she said she was going to the post, which is in the same building and takes nine minutes, and she said it out loud to him and to the bell and to nobody, and she went out and left the door open behind her the way she always does.

He stood at the counter with a room on his own.

The bell on that counter is a push and it does not ring by itself, and the strip light over it is on a timer and goes off at half past four whatever is in the room, and at about half past eleven on a Thursday morning there was no sound in there at all.

There are about four hundred boxes in there and about four hundred cards in four drawers and a trolley with a high shelf and a counter with a bell on it that nobody was going to push, and a tray with twelve slips in it, and the room is open to anybody who walks in off a street, and the whole of what it does happens when a woman of forty-one is standing behind the counter.

There is no form for it. There is no post. There is no minute and there is no notice and nobody has been asked to do anything.

It is a habit in a room with a counter and a person behind it, and a habit can be stopped by that person walking out, and she has said so out loud to a man of twenty-two on a Thursday morning and it is the only true thing anybody in this case has said about a room.

---

He wrote the day down at his own bench at ten at night with the shutter up and the lamp on, and he wrote it the way he writes a job down, and the day was two figures and a rule and a thing somebody said out loud in a room he was not in, and not one of the four of them is a number anybody is going to be able to quote.

There is a jacket on the back of the chair by that bench and there has been since before the term turned over, and there is a corner of card in the inside pocket of it about two inches by three that has not left a room in a term and a half, and nobody has ever held out a hand for it. The original is in the fourth pocket of the tool roll and the fifth line on it is blank, and it has been blank for longer than anybody has been asking him about it, and no document has made the decision and nobody is going to.

A woman of forty-six walked four buildings on the Tuesday with a trolley, stopped in an office, and said his name to a woman of thirty-six who does sheets up and turns every sheet over. That woman has not said it to anybody, and he does not know that either of those two things happened. What the woman of forty-six did put in front of him was on the Thursday of week fifty, with a kettle on and about four days before anybody asked her, and it was what the pencil was going to cost her, and he wrote that down in his own book in a pencil's line, and there was never a way to take that back either.

On a counter four miles away a form has a box ruled on it in pencil with a woman's name in the box, and no reference number on it and nothing typed, and it cannot come out, and nobody is going to fix it.

About nine hundred doors in four buildings carry a number off a list that whoever printed it does not own, and the drawing that would settle it is rolled in a drawer on a hook in a basement in the fourth building where there has not been a person since the spring, and a foreman of fifty-eight is carrying a key to that building and told a stranger in a public room on the Wednesday that he has not turned it.

A tin is on a table in a back room and the book in front of it is at forty-four lines with a hole in line forty-four where a woman of sixty-eight in bay four should have been, and it was not opened on the Wednesday, and about eleven people have telephoned a woman of sixty-one in about three weeks to ask her whether a person is allowed to speak in that room, and she has said yes eleven times and could not tell you what she was agreeing to.

A register in Crown Terrace has about nine hundred lines in it and the fourth column has not had a person's name in it in fourteen years, and a woman of thirty-four at a table there has now said out loud what she thinks of that column, in a room, to two people, and it is in nobody's file. And there is a town with four letters in it with a counter in it and a woman at that counter who will not say a name and is right.

Sixteen Fridays are on the back of a card in a wallet in Nine Bridges and in his own head in Lattice Ward, and a man of fifty-one was rung in nine seconds by somebody who was not him, and it was not a rescue, and there is still a leaflet on that man's table.

And about eleven people a week are still going to be taught that a release waits for a man in a lanyard, and a room off a street in Lattice Ward is standing open with a bell on its counter and nobody behind it, and the whole of what that room is can be stopped by one person putting her coat on and going out to the post.

Nobody has said so.
