# Chapter 165 — Eleven Who Asked First

There were nine of them in the back room at eleven at night on the Wednesday of week fifty-two, the tin in the middle of the table had no lock on it, and the book lying in front of Bea Nunn had four columns and forty-four lines in it and had not been opened since the Wednesday of week forty-eight.

There is a fifth chair against the wall with nobody in it. It has been empty since the spring. Nobody in that room moved it and one of the nine said she would move it if there were a reason to.

"There is not a reason to," said Bea Nunn. "**The chair is not the room's business. The room is the table and the tin and the two minutes and not a chair.**"

---

Nobody had called.

She said so before anybody asked, in the way she says things, and she put her hand flat on the book and did not open it.

"**Nobody has called, and it is a Wednesday, and there is a rule in this room and I am going to say the rule in one go and then I am going to sit down, and the rule is that a book of what a caller said cannot be written by the people who did not call.**"

Somebody said: that is the same as last time.

"It is the same rule and it is older than me and it was here before the tin," said Bea Nunn. "**You can say anything. All of it, in your own words, in whatever order it comes out, and nobody in this room will write it down and nobody in this room will stop you. There is no rule in this room against talking. The rule is against the book, and the book is forty-four lines and it is not a register and it is not going to be one, and it is not going to be forty-five tonight.**"

---

The woman who had asked the question had come because she had read a leaflet.

She was thirty-one and she was on a housing list and she had read the leaflet in a clinic and had rung the number on it twice and had been told that it met on a Wednesday, and she had not told anybody she was coming and she had not brought anything.

"**I rang a number,**" she said.

"**Then you are a caller,**" said Bea Nunn.

"**No I am not. I rang to ask whether it was true. I did not ring because I wanted something.**"

"**Then you are not, and I am not going to argue it, and if I am wrong then we will find out in about four years and it will be my fault and not yours. Sit down, you have four minutes and I am not going to take them.**"

---

She talked for three of them and none of it was written down, and what she said was that she had read about a practice in this city that belonged to nobody, and that she believed it, and that she had a question and it was not the question anybody in this room was going to want.

She asked whether a person who said something in this room, in their own words, with nobody writing it down, was protected. And she said that she had been told, by a woman with a lanyard, at a counter, in about four seconds, that a practice can write about itself in the first person and nothing else can, and that she had come four miles to find out what that meant.

"It means what it says," said Bea Nunn. "**A person signs their own words or their words are not in a book. That is the whole of it. I have said it in this room before and I have said it in eleven seconds and I have never once had anybody ask me what it means and I have got nothing better than that.**"

"You are the only one who can say it."

"**No, and I have not got anybody to give it to. Nine years I have been in this room and I have liked four people and written two of them down and been wrong both times.**"

---

The man of thirty-four who works nights was the seventh person to sit down.

He had been in a stairwell with Marek Senn the day before and he had asked for the stairwell and not the canteen, and he had four minutes, and he used two of them.

He said that a woman with a lanyard had asked him to take the word on his own floor, for his own four people, so that they would have somebody to wait for, and that he had said no in a second and a half, and that he could say why about one no he has ever given and not about this one.

Then he said: "**That is a person saying a thing in a room in his own words and he has not rung anybody, so it is not going in the book, and I am not going to ask anybody to make it go in the book.**"

"No," said Bea Nunn. "**And thank you for saying that in the room rather than afterwards, and I am not going to ask you the other thing, which is whether you would ring me on a Sunday if I asked you to.**"

"I would not."

"**Good. Nine years and that is the first one I have been told in advance.**"

---

Then she said the thing that was new, and she said it to the table, and nobody had asked her for it.

"**I am going to say a number and it is not about the book. In about three weeks eleven people have telephoned me and asked me whether it is true that a person can say anything in this room, and I have said yes eleven times, and I did not know what I was saying yes to and I am not sure now.**"

Somebody said: what is the risk.

"The risk is that a room where a person can say anything becomes a room where a person comes to be told something," said Bea Nunn. "**And being told something is not nothing. Being told something by a woman behind a counter at eleven at night in a room with no job in it is worth a very great deal to somebody who has been frightened for four months, and I have not got a rule about that, because the rule is that a caller signs their own words and the other half of the rule is that I do not decide who is a caller.**"

"That is the answer to the question she asked," said the man of thirty-four, "**and it is the same thing as the other answer, and you have said it twice in four minutes and neither time you said a rule.**"

"No," said Bea Nunn, "**because the first one is a rule and the second one is not, and if I let the second one sound like a rule then somebody in four years will be able to point at a rule that I made up at eleven at night because nine people were in a room.**"

---

At twenty past midnight the room began to empty and the fifth chair was still against the wall, and the book was still shut, and the tin went back in the middle of the table with nothing in it.

She had not opened the book. She was not going to open it on a Friday either, and nobody had asked her to, and there is a reason for that one as well and she has never said it out loud in that room.

"There is a book in this city with forty-four lines in it," said Bea Nunn, "and the fortieth of them is three years old, and there is a hole in line forty-four where a woman of sixty-eight in bay four should have been, and in about four years somebody is going to lift that cover and find forty-four lines and a hole and a woman of sixty-eight who was never written down, and there is not going to be a forty-fifth."

Nobody wrote it down either.

---

There are eleven people in this city who have telephoned a room in about three weeks to ask whether a person can say anything in it, and a woman of sixty-one has said yes eleven times, and the people who asked are the people with the worst thing to lose, because the only thing they all had in common was that they had read a leaflet and believed it.

A man of thirty-four used two of his four minutes on a thing he said in a stairwell the day before and asked for it not to go in the book, and a woman of thirty-one came four miles to find out what it meant to be protected and went home with a rule and no protection.

Nobody has said so.

*168. Wednesday of week 52, ten at night in a repair shop in Lattice Ward, and this entry is a tin that is not locked and a book that was not opened for the second time in six weeks, and a woman of sixty-one who has said yes eleven times to a question she did not know she was answering.*

Line forty-four has its hole in it and always has. Nobody in the room signed anything, nobody rang, and the woman of thirty-one did not become a caller by ringing a number to ask whether the room was real, and that argument was not had and is not going to be had.** Entry 168.
