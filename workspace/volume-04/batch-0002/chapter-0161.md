# Chapter 161 — The First Question

The register is on a table by a counter on the ground floor of a building in Crown Terrace and there is no rope round it, and on the Thursday morning of week fifty-one a man of twenty-two and a woman of thirty-eight who works nights walked up to that table together and asked a question that was not about him.

Doreen Abbiss had been awake since five and had come in the daylight on purpose, and she had said on the Thursday of week forty-eight that the daytime was six weeks of it and then it was not, and she had said that before she said yes and not after.

It was a quarter to ten and the woman of thirty-four was already at the table with her cardigan on and a cup of tea and a folder she had not opened.

---

She would not do it in the corridor.

She said so in the corridor, in eleven seconds, with her back to the stairs.

"No," she said. "**Not out here. I said on the Tuesday that I was not going to make one up in a doorway and I have thought about it for three weeks and I am not starting again in a corridor. If you have a question then ask it at the table, and give me nine minutes, and do not help me.**"

"You do not know what I am going to ask."

"No, and that is why I have said yes to it."

---

He asked the first one.

The whole of the difference was one word, and she had told him the difference at a threshold on the Tuesday afternoon of week forty-eight, and he had been carrying it for three weeks and had spent it exactly once.

"**What do you think of the fourth column,**" he said.

"**Say the other one again so that I know you know the difference.**"

"What is in it."

"And what do you think of it," she said. "**Right. Sit down, because I am not answering that standing up and I have not got a pen and I am not having this in a file.**"

---

Imogen Bray has been at that table for four years and nobody has ever asked her the second question. About four people in fourteen years have wanted a line out of that register and two of them were told it was a commercial matter and one of them was her.

She read the four column headings out because she always does, and she read the fourth one twice, and then she put her cup down and left it where it was.

"**Here is what I think of it. I have never said it, I am going to say it once, and you are not going to have it afterwards in a better sentence, so do not look pleased.**"

She turned the cup a quarter turn on the table.

"**I think it is the only column in that register anybody has ever read out loud. Date, place, hand, authority. Three of those are about what happened. That one is about who is meant to have done it, and there are about nine hundred lines in it and every one of them is a job title, and not one of them has ever been a person, and I have been reading them out for four years and I have never once read one and had anybody go quiet.**"

"Not one in four hundred."

"Not one. And I have thought about that for three weeks and I have got two answers to it and I do not like either of them," said Imogen Bray. "**I do not think anybody put it there on purpose. I think a job title in a column is a sentence with the person taken out of it, and I think that was easier, and I think it has been easier for fourteen years and nine people a year have seen it and not one of them has written to me.**"

---

She stopped there and drank tea that had gone cold, and set it down again.

"Now the half you did not want," she said, "**and this is the half that costs me, and I have worked out what it costs and I am going to tell you what it is, because you have not asked me and I would like it said in the room.**"

He said nothing, which is what she had asked for.

"**I have read that fourth column out to four hundred people in four years and not one of them has ever asked me to change it. Not one of them has said it ought to have a name in it. Four hundred people and a queue and a cup of tea, and I cannot tell you whether that is agreement or whether a queue is a queue. That is what I think of the fourth column. I think four hundred people have looked at the thing that is in charge and gone away, and not one of the four hundred has come back and said what they thought it was.**"

"That sounds like handing me a column with a complaint in it."

"It is not one, and I am not going to write it down, and I am not on anybody's distribution, and you are not going to be able to give it to a district. You have got it in your head now and that is the whole of where it is going to live," said Imogen Bray, "**and I have thought about that for three weeks and I have decided a thing nobody has ever asked me about is not a thing I am going to protect by being dull about. I have been dull at that table for four years and it has not protected one single thing. It has only made me one more person who has read it out.**"

---

Then she said her name, into a table.

"**Imogen Bray. It has not been on that register and I am not putting it there. I am saying it to you because you have asked me a question in a room and I have answered it, and in about four years a person is going to come and ask me whether I said it, and I would like it to be true that I said it.**"

"Then it is findable."

"It has been findable since eleven o'clock," she said, "**and it was findable the moment I answered, and I am not going to be dull about it. A name on a register is fourteen years of a column with a job title in it. A name in a room is a Tuesday.**"

---

Doreen Abbiss had been standing at the end of the table with her coat on and her hands in her pockets, and she asked the second question, and it was hers.

"**Has anybody ever been anything for a line in that book.**"

"No," said Imogen Bray. "**Not once in fourteen years. Nobody has been disciplined over a line in it and nobody has been praised for one either, and I have checked both. I will tell you the reason for the second one, which is that there is no line in that register with anybody's name against it, so there is nothing to be praised.**"

"That is worse than the one I came for."

"Yes," said Imogen Bray. "**I have known that for four years.**"

---

They were on the stairs at twenty past ten.

He did not thank her, and she had told him on a half landing at twenty past midnight on the Wednesday of week forty-eight that he was not going to and that she was not going to be thanked, and he took nine seconds over it and said the thing that was true instead, which was that she had given him the day.

"You are not going to say thank you," she said.

"No."

"**Good. Now say the other thing, which is that I am now a person who has been asked something, said out loud, in front of a stranger, in a building, on a Thursday morning, and I gave you the cost before I said yes and the cost has arrived. In about three weeks somebody in this city is going to want a person who was asked, and it will be me, and I will come, in the day, and the daytime is six weeks of it and then it is not. I have told you that once and I am not telling you again.**"

"I have written it down."

"I know you have. You have got a pencil in your hand and you have written it down, and that is the whole of what you do," said Doreen Abbiss, "**and it is a good thing to be and it is not a use.**"

---

There is a register in Crown Terrace with about nine hundred lines in it and the fourth column has not had a person's name in it in fourteen years, and four hundred people have had that column read out to them, and one of them has ever been asked what she thought of it.

She said it, and it is in nobody's file, and there is no distribution for it and no reference on it and no district it can be put in front of, and the only copy of it is in a head in a cardigan, by a counter, on a Wednesday and a Friday and a Monday.

The word is on the back of a docket in a jacket on a chair in Lattice Ward and it did not stop a woman reading a column out, and nobody has asked it to.

On the Friday of this week there are eleven jobs and a condition that is a condition and not a sentence anybody gets to improve, and a man of forty-four who wrote *no name* in two boxes is getting letters about it.

About eleven people a week are still going to be taught that a release waits for a man in a lanyard, and a man of twenty-two has been told on a staircase, by a woman of thirty-eight, that writing things down is not a use.

Nobody has said so.

---

*164. Thursday of week 51, ten at night in a repair shop in Lattice Ward, and this entry is a woman of thirty-four who has read a column out loud for four years and has been asked about it once, by me, with the right word, three weeks after she told me to use it.*

*What the table was.* **A bound register, canvas, four columns, about nine hundred lines, fourteen years, on a table by a counter on the ground floor. No rope and no sign, as on the Tuesday. A cup of tea that went cold, a cardigan, a folder not opened. A quarter to ten, a Thursday, and the woman standing at the end of the table with her coat on works nights and has been awake since five and came in the daylight deliberately.** She would not do it in the corridor and gave the reason in eleven seconds.

*How the question was put, and that it is one word.* **I asked what I think of the fourth column. I asked what is in it first, on purpose, in the corridor, and she made me say both, and then she made me sit down, and she said she was not answering it standing up and had not got a pen and was not having it in a file. She said give me nine minutes and do not help me. I did not help her and I have not improved a word of it and I am not going to try in this book.** The two questions are not the same question and four people in four years have asked the first and she has given the second one's answer every time.

*What is in that column, and it has not moved.* **About nine hundred job titles, no person, fourteen years. The hand column is blank in four lines in ten and filled in twice, and both times the person had left. The assessor is a woman of thirty-four, four years at the table, four hundred read-outs, asked twice about the column, told once that it is how the register is written, and once did not ask because the first time was enough.**

*Her answer, in her own order, and I am not reordering it.* **One: it is the only column anybody has ever read out loud, and nobody has ever gone quiet while she read one. Two: she does not think anybody put it there on purpose, and she thinks a job title in a column is a sentence with the person taken out of it, and that it has been easier for fourteen years and nine people a year have seen it and not one has written to her. Three, and she called it the half I did not want: four hundred read-outs, nobody asking to change it, nobody saying it ought to have a name in it, and her not able to tell whether that is agreement or a queue.**

*What it cost her, said out loud, unasked.* **She said a thing nobody has ever asked her about is not a thing she is going to protect by being dull about, and that being dull at that table for four years has not protected one single thing and has only made her one more person who has read it. She said the answer is in my head and that is the whole of where it is going to live: no distribution, no minute, no form, no reference, no district, and I cannot hand it to anybody. She gave her name into the table and said it is not going on the register, and the reason is that a name on a register is fourteen years of a column with a job title in it and a name in a room is a Tuesday.**

*And the second question, which was not mine.* **A woman of thirty-eight asked whether anybody has ever been anything for a line in that register. The answer was no in fourteen years, on both counts, and the reason nobody has been praised is that there is no line in it with a name against it. She said that is worse than the one she came for, and the woman at the table said she has known that for four years.**

*What happened on the stairs, in two minutes.* **I did not thank her, she said I was not going to and told me not to, and I said she had given me the day. She said the cost has arrived: she is a person who has been asked something, said out loud, in front of a stranger, in a building, on a Thursday morning, and in about three weeks this city is going to want a person who was asked, and it will be her, and she will come in the day, and the daytime is six weeks of it and then it is not. She said I have written it down, and that writing it down is what I do, and that it is a good thing to be and it is not a use.**

*What I have not done.* **I have not put her answer anywhere. There is no distribution, no minute, no form and no reference on it and there is not going to be one, and the only copy is in a head in a cardigan on a table by a counter that is open three days a week. I did not ask for it in a better sentence. I have written it down as a set of facts because that is what a book is.** Entry 164. The book is at forty-four lines and the next Exchange is the Wednesday of next week, and a column has an opinion in it now, and the opinion is not on a form, and about eleven people a week are still going to be taught to wait.
