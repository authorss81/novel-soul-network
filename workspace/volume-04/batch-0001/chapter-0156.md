# Chapter 156 — The Book Is Not A Register

Nine people were in the back room at eleven at night on the Wednesday of the forty-eighth week, and on the table in the middle of them there was a tin that is not locked and a four-column book that has forty-four lines in it.

Four minutes an item, and what goes in is the caller's own sentence in the first person with nothing changed and a signature at the bottom of it, and not one vote for anybody, ever.

The fortieth line is three years old. Line forty-four has a hole in it where a woman of sixty-eight in bay four should have been.

There is a fifth chair against the wall. It has been empty since the spring and nobody has moved it.

---

The woman who runs the room is Bea Nunn and she is sixty-one, and nine years is how long it has been since anybody thought to ask her to.

She did not do the two minutes. She put her hand flat on the book and said:

"**Nobody has called.**"

"It is Wednesday," said somebody.

"**It is Wednesday and it is eleven at night and nine people are in a room, and nine people in a room is not nine callers,**" said Bea Nunn. "**I have not opened that book in about six weeks and I have not added a line to it in about six weeks, and there is a hole in line forty-four that has been there since the spring, and if I open it tonight I will be writing down what somebody said in this room, and the rule of this room is that I write down what a caller said, and a caller is a person who rang.**"

"She could write it in pencil."

"**She could, and I will not, and I have thought about it for four days, which is longer than I have thought about anything in this room since the spring.**"

---

The one who could not sign was already sitting down.

Doreen Abbiss had come to a Wednesday and had read the leaflet, and she had not told anybody she was coming, and she had not brought anything.

"You are the one who puts a name in a box," she said to the room, and not to anybody in it.

"On Wednesday morning," said Bea Nunn, "**on a counter, with a clerk who ruled it and did not want to. Yes.**"

"**I put it there and I want it out and it cannot come out, and I have been carrying that for two days and I have not told anybody I have been carrying it, and I am telling nine people on a Wednesday because you are the only room in this city where nobody has a job in it.**"

Somebody said: "You have not rung here."

"No," said Doreen Abbiss. "I read a leaflet."

---

Bea Nunn gave her the rule and gave it in one go, the way she gives it.

"You can say it," she said. "**All of it, in your own words, and in whatever order it comes out, and nobody here will write it down and nobody here will stop you and there is no rule in this room against talking. What there is a rule against is the book. The book is a book of what a caller said. A caller is a person. A person signs their own words. A practice cannot sign anything, and that is the only reason this book is different from the sheet of typing paper that three people wrote on four weeks ago, and that is the only reason either of them exists.**"

"So I cannot go in the book."

"You can go in the book if you ring me," said Bea Nunn, "**and you are not going to, and I would rather you did not, and I am not going to ask you why not because I have got nine minutes and I am going to spend them on the reason I am not writing tonight.**"

---

She said the reason, and it took eleven seconds, and it is a rule and it has been in that room since before she ran it.

"**A room of people who did not call is not nine callers, and a line in a book is a promise that somebody stood at a telephone and wanted something and got it, and if I write tonight then in about four years somebody is going to read line forty-five and find out that a woman of thirty-eight who did not want to be found said a true thing in a room with nine people in it, and that is not a book of callers, that is a book of people I liked.**"

"You liked her."

"**I have liked four people in nine years and I have written two of them down and been wrong both times,**" said Bea Nunn. "**The book is at forty-four lines and it is not a register and it is not going to be one, and the whole of the difference between the two is that a practice can write about itself in the first person and nothing else can.**"

---

So Doreen Abbiss talked for nine minutes and none of it was written down.

She said that there were four of them on a bay and one of them answered and it was not her. She said that she took the share off in four seconds without asking anybody, and that a bay went off eleven minutes later and nobody was hurt, and that she has not slept a full night since the Tuesday of week thirty-eighth and has not told anybody, and that she has four hundred and thirty-two cards in her coat at home that a printer of fifty-eight printed and cannot get back, and that a man of twenty-two with a lanyard on said in a corridor that a name on the front of a thing is the end of it, and that she believed him at the time and still does, and that she put her own name on a page four days later anyway, in about nine seconds, on a telephone, to a stranger.

At about eight minutes she said: "**I am not asking anybody for anything. I want that said while I am still talking and not after.**"

"Noted," said Bea Nunn, "in this room and not in the book."

---

At twenty past midnight the room began to empty and the fifth chair against the wall was still empty and four people in the room had noticed that it was still empty, which is the same four as last month.

Bea Nunn put the tin in the middle of the table and did not open the book.

Marek Senn was on the half landing with a docket in his hand and a piece of card about two inches by three in the inside pocket of a jacket over a chair in a repair shop in Lattice Ward, four miles from where he was standing, and he had not gone up.

Doreen Abbiss came down past him and stopped.

"**You were outside a door on Wednesday morning for about forty minutes,**" she said.

"I was."

"**I could hear somebody outside that door and I could not hear what they were saying and it did not matter at the time,**" she said, "**and it matters now, because that is the only reason I know there is a box at all, and you were on the other side of it, and you did not come in.**"

"It was not my counter."

"No," said Doreen Abbiss. "**It is not anybody's counter. That is the thing I have not been able to say to anybody since Wednesday, and I am saying it to you on a half landing at twenty past midnight because you are a stranger and I have not got to look at you while I say it. A form with four fields and a tick on it is the best thing anybody in this city has written and it does not work, and it does not work because of a box that was not on it, and now there is a box and it has got my name in it and I cannot get it out, and nobody drew that box on purpose and nobody is going to fix it, and there is no post.**"

"There is no post."

"Then here is what I am actually saying," said Doreen Abbiss. "**The next time you want a name on a piece of paper, ask the person whose name it is, before you have got a pencil out. That is all. I have got about nine words and I have used them, and I am going home.**"

She went down the stairs.

---

Bea Nunn came out last and shut the door behind her and stood on the pavement for a minute in the rain with the tin under her arm.

"You did not come up," she said.

"No."

"I do not mind and I want to say that I do not mind," said Bea Nunn, "**because people come up for the wrong reasons and you have not got one, and I have wanted to say that to somebody since about the week of the fortieth and there has not been anybody.**"
