# Chapter 151 — The First Name In It

The box has been on the counter for two Wednesdays and on both of them it was empty.

The counter is on the ground floor of a building in Bower Street and it has outlasted two of the people who have stood at it, and about two hundred of the district's withdrawal pages are on it in four stacks with a rubber band round each one, and nobody has come in for one of them since the spring.

Four fields. One of them a tick. And one of them, on the top page of the first stack, a box about the size of a stamp ruled in pencil under the fourth field with three words over the top of it in a clerk's hand.

The three words are *who ended it*.

There is no reference number on the page. Nothing was typed. It is a form and it is not a system, and it is the only box in this city for the person who removed a share.

---

She is Petra Lindqvist, she is fifty-three, and there have been nineteen years of that counter behind her, and in all nineteen of them nobody has ever handed her a decision to make.

Petra Lindqvist had put the rubber band back on the other three stacks on the Wednesday morning the box was made, and she has not mentioned the woman who asked her to rule it since, and this is the second Wednesday she has looked at the three words and not said anything.

"You are going to keep looking at it," said Ianthe Roke.

"**I have got four stacks and one of them is different and I have been looking at it since the spring,**" said Petra Lindqvist. "That is not a decision either. I do not decide things. I noticed."

---

The telephone on the counter rings about four times a week and it is a different telephone from the one in the room upstairs, and about two thirds of the things that come down that line are somebody asking whether there is anybody here.

"Bower Street, forms counter," said Petra Lindqvist.

Then she said: "Say it again."

"I have got one of your pages," said a woman's voice. "I have had it since before the spring. I have had it in a drawer at work and I have moved house with it in a drawer at work and I have moved house twice."

"Which page."

"The one with four things on it and one of them a tick."

Petra Lindqvist put her hand over the mouthpiece and looked up the stairs.

"Ianthe."

---

Upstairs there is an exchange room and the woman who runs it is thirty-one and called Ianthe Roke, and what she has is a key, a diary and a list, and what she does not have is a door anybody can find, because the room is not where the building says it is.

She came down at a fast walk and stood on the other side of the counter and put her diary on the marble and opened it, and Petra Lindqvist said the mouthpiece thing again, and Ianthe Roke took the telephone.

"**Say what you have got in front of you.**"

"**A page. Four fields. A date and a time at the top. A tick. And a telephone number with a man on the Friday and me underneath it.**"

"And the four fields."

"What was shared, who it was shared with, whether it was a narrow share or a full one, and whether anybody has been told."

"**That is one of ours,**" said Ianthe Roke, "and I have never once been able to tell one of them from another and there are about two hundred, and the one I keep in the drawer upstairs is not yours, because that one is from the Tuesday of week thirty-eight and it was filled in in somebody else's hand, and yours has got a telephone number on the top of it, and neither of them has got a box on it and there is no box on any of them, and you have rung this counter twice already and been told the same thing twice."

"I have rung it twice and been told it twice."

"Then I am going to say the one thing I have got, and it is a new thing this morning and it has got my name at the bottom of it and nobody's at the top, and it is on a piece of paper and not on a system, and I am going to say that before I say it."

---

Nobody in the corridor heard any of that.

Marek Senn was outside the door with a docket in his hand and about nine minutes of a job to collect a page for, and he was on grade two that day and it was a Wednesday and the form counter was the only place in the district where a page like that was kept, and the woman of thirty-one came past him on her way up and said the counter was busy and would he wait, and he said he would wait.

He could hear the telephone through the door. He could not hear the voice on the other end of it, or the woman's, or either of the two people in the room, and he did not knock, and he did not ask, and he stood in a corridor and listened to the shape of a conversation he could not have identified afterwards if anybody had asked him.

The word was at nine a minute by then, which was the morning's number, and it did not stop him hearing a voice and it did not stop him asking a question about a voice, and he did not ask one.

---

"**Say your name,**" said Ianthe Roke.

There was a pause of about four seconds on the line, which is a long time on a telephone.

"Doreen Abbiss."

"Spell it for me."

The woman spelled it.

"Doreen Abbiss," said Ianthe Roke. "I have got a date on the top of this page and a time and a tick, and a box under the fourth field that a clerk ruled in pencil this morning fortnight because I asked her to, and I am going to write your name in it, and it is the only one of these in this city and it is not on a system and it has got no number on it and it is not going anywhere except back to this counter."

"Then it is about me."

"**It is about the person who took a share off,**" said Ianthe Roke. "I am going to ask you one question and I am not going to ask you two, and then I am going to stop, because there is a page in a drawer upstairs with four fields filled in on it and no person on it anywhere, and I have looked at that page every day since the spring, and I am not going to have that be the last one."

"Go on."

"**Were you willing.**"

---

She did not answer it.

What she said was: "**I held it.** I was the one holding it. There were four of us on that bay and one of us answered and it was not me and I took it off anyway, and it took four seconds, and a bay went off eleven minutes later and nobody was hurt, and I have not told anybody and I am not going to and there is nobody to tell."

"Then I have got the wrong person."

"**You have got the person who took it off,**" said Doreen Abbiss, "and that is the person the box is for. Put my name in it."

---

Ianthe Roke wrote it in pencil, under the fourth field, in about the size of a stamp, in her own hand, in a room with a counter and about two hundred other pages in four stacks with a rubber band on each one.

She said the time out loud while she wrote it and Petra Lindqvist heard her and did not look up.

Then she said: "**It is in. Do you want it out.**"

"**I want it out.**"

"**I have to tell you the thing before I say anything else, and I am not going to soften it, and it is eleven words and then I will stop.**"

"Go on."

"**A name written in pencil is a name on a page in a building.**" She let that sit. "I cannot take it out. Not because I will not. Because the paper goes back in that stack with a rubber band round it and there are about two hundred of them and nobody has collected them since the spring and a district picks those up and nobody knows when. If I tore it out, then there is a hole in a stack, and a hole in a stack is a thing somebody asks about. And if I do not tear it out then your name is on a page in this building for as long as the page exists, and the page has got no number on it and there is no way to find it again, which I know sounds like the good news. It is not the good news. It means I cannot take it back and neither can you and neither can anybody."

"Then I have made a mistake."

"**You have made a phone call,**" said Doreen Abbiss. "You did not make a mistake. I made a mistake and I made it in about nine seconds and I am going to have to live in it, and I would like it noted that nobody asked me whether I wanted to put my name on anything and that I would have said yes to being asked."

---

Petra Lindqvist had not said anything since she said the name of the building down the telephone, and she said it now, to the counter.

"**I ruled that box,**" she said, "because I was asked to rule a line and three words and put nothing in it, and I have not been told since what it is for, and I am not going to ask. I am going to say one thing and then I am going to go back to the four stacks."

She put the pencil down.

"**Nineteen years of that counter and everything that has left it was better printed than that page, and none of it was any use to whoever took it away. And now there is a name in a box on the top of one stack, and in about four years somebody in a district is going to lift that rubber band and read it, and they are not going to know that a woman of thirty-one in this building asked a woman of thirty-eight in a telephone whether she was willing, and they are not going to know that she did not answer, and they are not going to care.**"

Nobody said anything.

"Say it in the minute," said Ianthe Roke.

"There is no minute," said Petra Lindqvist. "Say it in the book."

"**There is a book and it is in another building and it takes what a caller said and not what a person was asked, and you are right and I know you are right and that is the whole of what I have got.**"

---

Doreen Abbiss said one more thing and she said it to nobody in particular, which is how she said it.

"**There is one other place in this city where I could put my name in my own words, and it meets on a Wednesday, and it is four minutes an item, and I know it is a Wednesday because I have read the leaflet.**"

"Then go on Wednesday," said Ianthe Roke.

"**No,**" said Doreen Abbiss. "Because a caller signs their own words, and the last time I signed anything in this city with my name on it, somebody found me, and that is the whole of the reason I have said yes to being on a page and no to a book, and it is a stupid reason and it is a real one."

The line went dead.

---

At ten past eleven Marek Senn got his page.

He had been in the corridor for about forty minutes and he had heard a telephone ring four times and a door open and a woman's voice go up a staircase, and he had not heard a single word of any of it, and when he came in off the corridor the counter had a form on it and the woman of thirty-one was closing a diary and the clerk of fifty-three was looking at about two hundred pages in four stacks.

He said thank you and he took the page and he went out, and on the stairs he noticed that the top page of the first stack had been squared off against the others and had a rubber band stretched a little tighter than it had been that morning, and he did not think about it for about a week.

There is no mechanism in this city that can take a name back off a page.
