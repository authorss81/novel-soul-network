# Chapter 192 — The Fifth Chair

The back room has the shutters up, a table in the middle of it, a tin on that table with no lock on it, and a book lying open in front of the woman who runs it: four columns, forty-four lines.

This is the fifth Wednesday on which the book has not been opened, counting from the Wednesday of week forty-eight, and the sittings are four weeks apart, so that there are three Wednesdays in between those five and four gaps between them. The sixth falls four weeks from tonight. Nobody has asked her about it and she has not asked anybody.

Four minutes an item, and nobody in that room has a vote, and nobody in that room is on the payroll of anybody who wanted an item out of them.

---

She gave the rule before anybody had asked her for anything, in one go, the way she has given it every time anybody has ever come through that door.

"**A book of what a caller said cannot be written by the people who did not call.** It is older than her. It was in this room before anybody bought the tin. **It is a rule and not a mood, and she is not going to be asked which of the two it is, and she has said so in about four rooms and is not going to soften it for anybody in this one.**"

"Forty-four lines, and the fortieth of them is three years old. A man put that one there: he rang this number, he said nine words, and he signed his own name underneath them. There has not been a line since. **The forty-fourth has a hole in it where a woman of sixty-eight in bay four ought to be, and she was never written down and she is not going to be.**"

---

There were ten people in that room on the Wednesday of week sixty-four and two of them had telephoned her.

She said the number out loud at half past nine, which is what she does, and it took longer than it usually takes because it had not changed.

"**Twenty-nine, and it has not moved in four weeks.** Nine of them in the spring. Twenty on the Wednesday of the fifty-sixth. Twenty-nine on the Wednesday of the sixtieth, and the same tonight, and I am going to tell this room why, because I have not told a room why before and it is the more interesting half."

"Go on."

"**Because I have not telephoned anybody in four weeks. Twenty-nine people in this city have been told yes by me and two of them are in this room and I have not rung the other twenty-seven, and the last time I rang anybody it was the fortnight after a Wednesday in the spring, and I have not been able to work out since whether the second time was a habit or whether I was frightened of the third.**"

"You could ring one."

"I could ring one, and the reason I have not is not a good one, and the reason I have not is that the second time I rang a frightened woman and told her it was all right to feel frightened, and it was, and I was the only person in this city who knew that she had rung, and that is in my head and it is not on anything, and I would rather sit in a room with ten people in it and say the number out loud than pick up a telephone." She turned the tin a quarter turn. "**So it is twenty-nine, and the number is not going to move, and it is going to move when I am a different sort of woman and not before.**"

---

Two items, and neither of them went in the book, and both of them were four minutes.

A man of forty-four asked for a room and got nine minutes and said that he had wanted a room and had got a sentence and would take the sentence. He said his sentence out loud in a room with about ten people in it and it was about a sheet of paper in a metal box on a high shelf that anybody can walk in off a street and ask to see, and about forty people in nine years who have asked to see a binder in that room, and about the fact that in about four years somebody is going to be able to say that about forty people asked and the number of them who asked for that one is a number that is smaller than the number of them who asked for a different one.

He said that last part twice, both times slowly, and it was not true and he knew it was not true, and he said it was the reason he could say it out loud.

Then a woman of about thirty with a notebook asked whether anybody who rang and then did not come ought to be written down, and Bea Nunn gave her the other half of the rule in front of ten people.

"**I have not got a rule for that half and I am not going to invent one at eleven at night with about ten people in a room, because the second that sounds like a rule then in about four years somebody is going to be able to point at a rule I made up on a Wednesday because a room was full and people were being kind to me.**"

---

The fifth chair was in the row.

It is against that wall at nine o'clock and it is a plain chair with a seat that has been repaired with something, and nobody in that room moves furniture, and the woman who moved it had a key to the room and had not told anybody she had one and had told two people in a corridor in the spring, and she was thirty-one and had a diary and no standing.

"You put it out."

"I put it out at about half eight," said Ianthe Roke. "**And I have thought about it on four separate occasions since and the reason is that a chair against a wall is not an answer to anything and a chair in a row is, and I do not have a rule for who is expected.**"

"There is a rule for who can be in the room."

"There is a rule for who can be in the room and it is the same rule as the one about the book and I am not going to split it into two so that I have got one." She sat down in it, which she had not meant to do. "**You are the only person in this city who has ever split that rule, and you did it in about nine seconds on a Wednesday, and I have thought about that more than I have thought about the chair.**"

---

Nobody wrote anything down. The tin went back into the middle of the table with nothing in it, and the shutters went up at about ten past midnight, and the book did not open.

Bea Nunn moved the fifth chair back against the wall herself, at the front, with both hands, while nine people watched and one of them said that it looked like something out of a room that has stopped being used.

"It has not stopped being used," she said. "**It is used about four times a month by people who ring, and the chair is where the room keeps the person who did not ring, and there is no name for that person and I am not going to invent one, and that is the fifth thing I have said out loud in this room in a term and none of the five is a rule I made up tonight.**"

---

*195. Wednesday of week 64, ten at night in a back room with the shutters up, and this entry is forty-four lines, a fifth chair, and a number of telephone calls that did not move.*

*The book, as a count.* **Four columns, forty-four lines, four minutes an item, no vote for anybody, a tin with no lock. Fifth sitting shut. The sittings are the Wednesdays of weeks forty-eight, fifty-two, fifty-six, sixty and sixty-four, counted as five and not as the four intervals since week forty-eight, and there is no Wednesday in the run that is not four weeks from the one before it. The fortieth line is three years old. Line forty-four has a hole in it where a woman of sixty-eight in bay four should be. The book did not open.**

*The number, said out loud, and the reason it had not moved.* **Twenty-nine at the spring, twenty on the Wednesday of week fifty-six, twenty-nine on the Wednesday of week sixty, twenty-nine on this Wednesday, and four weeks with no movement. Ten people in the room and two of the twenty-nine in it. Her own account of the stillness: that she has not telephoned anybody in four weeks, that the second time she rang anybody it was a frightened woman and she told her it was all right to feel frightened and that she was the only person in the city who knew the woman had rung, and that she cannot tell whether the second call was a habit or the third was the thing she was afraid of. She offered to ring one and did not.**

*Two items, and the two halves of one rule kept apart in front of ten people.* **A man of forty-four, nine minutes, about a metal box on a high shelf anybody can ask to see, about forty people in nine years who have asked to see a binder, and about a number that is smaller than another number and is not true and is the reason he could say it out loud. A woman of about thirty with a notebook, asking whether a caller who did not come ought to be written down, and the answer given: no rule exists for that half and none will be made at eleven at night in a full room.**

*The chair, as an object and not a symbol.* **Plain, a repaired seat, against a wall at nine o'clock and in the row at half past eight, moved by a woman of thirty-one with a key and a diary and no standing who has told two people about the key and nobody else. Her reason, given before she was asked twice: a chair against a wall is not an answer to anything and a chair in a row is, and she has not got a rule for who is expected, and the rule about who may be in the room is the rule about the book and she is not going to split it into two so that she has one. She sat down in it and did not mean to. Bea Nunn put it back against the wall herself at the front, with both hands.**

*What the entry deliberately does not contain.* **No caller, no signature, no forty-fifth line, no new rule, no post and no office. The two people who have been in this room in consecutive movements and were promised a turn at it — a man of sixty-ish who said in nine seconds that he had not telephoned, and a man of fifty-one who comes to a trestle in a market and not here — are not in this room and were not asked to be. Entry 195.**
