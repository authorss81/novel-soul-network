# Chapter 198 — Fifty-Eight In A Tray

There is a room off a street in Lattice Ward that anybody may walk into, and a counter in it with a bell on the counter that is a push and does not ring by itself, and four drawers under the counter with about four hundred cards in them, and a trolley against a wall with a bad third wheel.

There is a tray on that counter. On the Friday of week fifty-nine it held thirty-one slips and not one of them was about a box. On the Thursday of week sixty-six it held fifty-eight, and nine of them had a person's name written at the bottom of them, and the first one with a name on it had arrived a fortnight before.

Nobody had answered any of the nine.

---

She said the number out loud at about ten past nine, once to a man of twenty-two with a docket and once to a woman of forty-six with a trolley, and the second time she said it slower, because the second of those two had not heard the first one.

"Fifty-eight," said Wendla Ossick. "**And it was thirty-one on the Friday of week fifty-nine and it is fifty-eight now, and I have counted it every Wednesday since and I have said the number out loud every Wednesday since, and nobody has ever asked me to and I have never once been asked whether it should be smaller.**"

"How many of them have got somebody on."

"**Nine. There have been nine for about a fortnight and there were none in the spring and there were none in the summer, and the nine are all different people.**"

"And the other forty-nine."

"**A slip with a person on it is a request and it goes in the tray.**"

"And one without."

"**A slip without a person on it is a piece of paper, and there is no tray for that either.**"

"And the thirty-one."

"The thirty-one are pieces of paper. **I do not know who put them in there and I have never asked and I am not going to, because the day I ask who put a piece of paper in my tray then this room has got a column, and the room does not have a column.**"

---

Four of the nine came in on that Thursday and they came in one at a time between about half past nine and about eleven, and none of them knew the other three had been, and not one of them asked her for anything.

The first was a foreman of fifty-eight with a site diary in his hand and a telephone on a wall in a prefab office off the Meridian corridor, and he has held a stranger's box number since the spring and has not been asked to be anything.

"I have got five lines in pencil in this diary and no reason in them at all," he said, "**and I have been putting them in since the week before last because a thing happened in a stairwell and I could not find anywhere else to put it, and I have come to ask whether I am allowed to keep doing that, and that is the whole of it and it has taken me a fortnight to say.**"

"You are allowed to keep a diary."

"Then what am I allowed to keep."

"You are allowed to keep a diary," said Wendla Ossick, "**and a diary is a diary, and the answer to what you are allowed to keep is in a document somewhere and I have not got it and I am not going to guess at a counter.**"

She wrote his name at the bottom of a slip and put it in the tray and she said the number out loud again, to nobody, because that is what she does.

---

The second was a woman of twenty-nine with a card in her wallet and sixteen Fridays written on the back of it in her own hand.

"**I have got sixteen Fridays on the back of this card and I have never told anybody what they are for, and I have come to ask whether a card is a record, and if it is a record then somebody somewhere is keeping the other end of it, and if somebody is keeping the other end then I would like to know that before I am sixty.**"

"It is a record if somebody keeps it."

"Then nobody keeps it," said the woman of twenty-nine, "**and that is the first useful thing anybody has said to me about that card in six years, and I am going to go away and think about how annoying it is.**"

She said the first half of it and laughed, and Wendla Ossick did not, and the slip went in the tray with a name on it.

---

The third was a care worker of thirty-eight who works nights in a residential home and who came in at about ten and had not been in that room before, and she had come in it because a woman of thirty-four told her about it in a corridor four weeks ago and told her once.

"I gave my name to a piece of paper in the spring," she said, "**and I have had four months of not knowing who has read it, and I am not asking to have it back and I am not going to ask anybody to be told that it does not exist, because I know it exists and there is a woman of fifty-three behind a counter in another building who has never once been asked to know anything and I would not do that to her. I am asking one question and it is: can a person find out who has read a page.**"

The room was quiet for a moment.

"**No," said Wendla Ossick. "And that is not a rule of mine and it is not a mood, it is that there is a box in a building in Bower Street with about two hundred pages in it in four stacks and no system behind any of them, and if a page is not in a system then there is nothing in it that could answer a question like that, and I have been behind a counter for nine years and I have never been able to answer it once.**"

"And I did not sign that."

"You did not sign it," said Wendla Ossick, "**and I wrote your name on it, and there is a difference and I have got about two hundred pages in this city that a person gave a name to and somebody else wrote, and one of them is the one you are asking about, and I am not going to be the person who hands either of them back or the person who says I cannot.**"

"Then I have got a no."

"You have got a true thing," said Wendla Ossick. "**And the difference between a no and a true thing is that somebody can be angry with a no, and I would rather you were angry with that.**"

---

The fourth ran a print shop of her own in the Quiet Quarter, four feet of it, and came in with a bag of card under her arm and a hundred and forty-one pounds sitting in an envelope under a till that she has no way of sending anywhere.

She is still the person who decides what a card is for, and nobody has offered her a way of not being that, and she has not asked for one.

"**I have come to ask whether a person can print something and then not be the person who decides about it,**" she said. "**And I know how that sounds at a counter. I have been the person who decides what that card is for since the spring and four hundred and thirty-two of them are in about nine places in one district and about four hundred of the new ones have my shop's name on the back, and I cannot take a single one of them off anybody, and I have looked for a way of not being the decider for about four months and there is not one, and that is the whole of what I have come with.**"

Wendla Ossick wrote her name on a slip and did not answer the question, and said so.

"I am not going to answer that, because I do not know the answer, and I would rather say that than say something you would repeat to somebody in about four years as though I had thought about it."

---

There was also a folder on the high shelf of that trolley, and it had been put there on the Wednesday, and it did not have a card on it and it did not need one.

She said the one thing about it out loud, at about eleven, to the man with the docket, in front of two other people, because she reads things out.

"**A box does not come down without a slip, and a slip is a piece of paper and a person.** Nine years of that, and it is not a rule I made up in a spring when somebody stood in front of me and looked unhappy."

"And a folder is not a box."

"**That is about a box coming down off that shelf, and what went up onto it on Wednesday was a folder, and those are not the same movement, and I am not going to start a record of what goes up, because a record of what goes up is a list, and a list is a column, and the room does not have a column.**"

"Whose folder is it."

"It is a sheet of paper that came out of a drawer in a building in Crown Terrace on the Thursday of week sixty-five, in a room under that building with no lock on it, and it is in there because a woman of forty-one carried it out of the building and up four flights of stairs on her own time and put it on a shelf in a room anybody can walk into, and that is the whole of it. I have not read it and nobody in this room has read it. It is not a box, it is not on a system, and it is not a thing I have taken in."

---

And about forty people in nine years have asked that woman to see a binder, and about forty of them were given a binder, and not one of them asked for the one on that counter, and she said the reason out loud at about a quarter to twelve and she said it once.

"You cannot ask for a binder nobody has told you about."

"No," said Wendla Ossick. "**You cannot ask for a binder nobody has told you about, and about forty people in this city have come through that door in nine years and every one of them asked for a binder they had been told about, and not one of them has ever asked for the one on that counter, and that is not because it is a secret, it is because nobody has ever said out loud that there is a second one.**"

And nobody said anything for a while, and the nine slips were in the tray, and the tray was not emptied, and nothing was adopted and nothing was distributed and nothing was minuted and nothing was noticed, and about nine people in this city had begun asking questions in a fortnight that nobody had asked before, and there is no committee and there is no minute and there is no notice and there is no leaflet, and the tray is a drawer.

---

*201. Thursday of week 66, ten at night in a repair shop in Lattice Ward, and this entry is a tray with fifty-eight slips in it, nine of which have a person's name at the bottom, and a folder on a high shelf that is not a box.*

*The count, said twice, the second time slower.* **Thirty-one on the Friday of week fifty-nine and fifty-eight on this Thursday, counted and said aloud every Wednesday in between, and nobody has ever asked her to count it and nobody has ever asked whether it should be smaller. Nine of the fifty-eight have a person's name written at the bottom of them; the first arrived about a fortnight before this Thursday and there were none in the spring and none in the summer. The other forty-nine are pieces of paper and she has never asked who put them in the tray and is not going to, because the day she asks who put a piece of paper in her tray the room has got a column and the room does not have a column. None of the nine has been answered.**

*The four who came, in the order they came, as questions and not as arrivals.* **A foreman of fifty-eight with five lines of pencil in a site diary and a second holder of a box number since the spring, asking whether he is allowed to keep writing them, and told that a diary is a diary and that what he is allowed to keep is in a document somewhere behind a counter and is not going to be guessed at. A woman of twenty-nine with sixteen Fridays on the back of a card and no total ever written, asking whether a card is a record, and told that it is a record if somebody keeps it, and that nobody does. A care worker of thirty-eight, nights, six years, who came because a woman of thirty-four told her about the room once in a corridor, asking the single question whether a person can find out who has read a page, and told no, with the reason, and told that the difference between a no and a true thing is that somebody can be angry with a no. A printer of fifty-eight with four feet of shop of her own and a hundred and forty-one pounds she cannot move out from under a till, asking whether a person can print something and then not be the person who decides about it, and told, correctly, that the registrar does not know the answer and would rather say so than say something that would be repeated in four years as though she had thought about it.**

*The folder on the high shelf, and the rule that does not move.* **A plain folder with a sheet of paper in it, put there on the Wednesday, with no card and no slip, because a slip is required for a box coming down off that shelf and this went up, and the registrar is not going to start keeping a record of what goes up because a record of what goes up is a list and a list is a column and the room does not have a column. She has not read it and nobody in the room has read it and she said both of those out loud in front of two people.**

*The sentence that is the stone of this volume, said once, and not argued.* **That a person cannot ask for a binder nobody has told them about; that about forty people have come through that door in nine years and every one of them asked for a binder they had been told about; and that not one of them has ever asked for the one on that counter, and that this is not because it is a secret but because nobody has ever said out loud that there is a second one. This entry does not say what will happen to the nine, and the tray is a drawer, and nothing was adopted, distributed, minuted or noticed, and no committee, minute, notice, report or leaflet exists anywhere in this Thursday.**

*What the entry deliberately does not contain.* **It does not contain an answer to any of the nine, and it does not contain the correction of anything, and it does not contain a name on the front of anything except the nine names that those nine people wrote themselves. A woman of forty-six with a trolley was in the room and did not put a slip in the tray, and the reason is not given here because she did not give one. Entry 201.**
