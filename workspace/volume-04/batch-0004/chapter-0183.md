# Chapter 183 — Nobody Took It From Her

About two hundred of the district's withdrawal pages were on the counter on the ground floor of a building in Bower Street, in four stacks, with a rubber band round each one, and there has not been a collection from that counter since the spring.

Four fields on a page. One of them a tick. A date and a time at the top. And on the top page of the first stack there is a box about the size of a stamp, ruled in pencil under the fourth field, with three words written over the top of it in a clerk's hand.

The three words are *who ended it*.

The clerk is Petra Lindqvist. She is fifty-three and there are nineteen years of that counter behind her and she is the only person alive who knows that the line at the foot of the district instruction and the box on that page are the same document.

A man of twenty-two was four miles away on a docket on the Thursday morning of week fifty-nine. He was not in the building and was not told.

---

Doreen Abbiss came in at about eleven and she had not been in that building in her life.

She is thirty-eight and she works nights in a residential home and has done for six years, and the daytime is the only part of the week that is hers, and she was in that building at eleven in the morning on a Thursday because she is a person who has been asked something, and she said on the stairs that she had told the woman who rang her that the next person this city needs is her and that she would come in the day.

She had a page in a plastic bag in her handbag. She had had it since before the spring and she had moved house with it twice.

---

"You are Doreen Abbiss," said Ianthe Roke. "I put your name in that box in the spring and I have thought about it every week since, and I am not going to say I am sorry, because I would be saying something I do not think is true."

"Who is behind the counter."

"Her." Ianthe Roke did not turn round. "**She ruled the box. I asked her to rule a line and three words and put nothing in it, and she did it in a morning and told nobody, and it is the week of forty-five and there is a line printed at the foot of the instruction that says no local additions and she read it every day for nineteen years.**"

"I did not know that," said Doreen Abbiss.

"Nobody does. That is not an accusation. I found out about a fortnight ago and I have been telling people about it in about four rooms and nobody has taken it anywhere."

---

The clerk took the page out of the plastic bag and put it on the marble, and then she took the top page off the first stack and unhooked the rubber band and put that one next to it, and the two of them lay about nine inches apart on the counter and were not the same sheet.

"Read me mine," said Doreen Abbiss.

Petra Lindqvist read it out in the voice she uses for the counter.

"Date, a Wednesday, before the spring. Time, quarter past two. What was shared: a bay. Who it was shared with: four people. Narrow or full: narrow. Anybody told: yes. **And a telephone number at the top with a man on the Friday and you underneath it.**"

"And that one."

"**That is a different page and it has got a box on it and the box has got your name in it, in pencil, in her hand, in the spring, and the date on the top of it is a Wednesday and the time is a quarter past two, and the tick is there, and the four fields on it are correct.**" She squared the two sheets. "**Neither of them has a person at the top of it. I have read them both about nine times this morning and neither of them has anybody's name at the top.**"

---

"What I want is for mine to have my name on it as well."

"No," said Doreen Abbiss, before anybody had answered her. "**Wait. Say that again slower and let me hear whether it is a yes or a no from somebody who has been behind that counter for nineteen years.**"

"It is a no," said Petra Lindqvist. "**And I have been telling people it is a no for about four years and there is a line printed at the foot of the instruction that says the district may not add a box for it, and I read that line every morning, and there is no version of me putting a name at the top of a page in that stack that is not a person inventing a thing because somebody stood in front of her and looked unhappy.**"

"And the one with my name in the pencil."

"That is worse and that is the part I have not got a sentence for."

---

They stood at that counter for half an hour and there were four other people in and out of the corridor behind them and nobody came to the counter at all, which Petra Lindqvist said afterwards had never happened.

Ianthe Roke said the thing she had come down to say, and she said her standing first, because she always says her standing first.

"**I have got a key to the room upstairs, and a diary, and a list, and I have been told twice this term that I have no standing, and I am going to put a second name in that box if the person it is for asks me to, because there is one rule about that box and I have never broken it: the person it is for asks, or it does not happen.**"

"That is not a rule," said Doreen Abbiss. "That is the opposite of a rule. A rule is a thing that happens whether anybody asks or not."

"It is the only rule I have got and I have had it since the spring and it is mine and it is not on anything."

---

"Then ask me," said Doreen Abbiss.

Ianthe Roke did not ask her.

She stood at that counter with a pen in her hand and about four seconds went by, and then she put the pen down on the marble, flat, the way a person puts a thing down when they are not going to pick it up, and she said:

"**No. And I am going to tell you why in a way that is a reason and not a mood, because you have come in here on your own time and you have not got anybody to hand.**"

"Go on."

"**The box is a box that nobody has ever offered to anybody, and the only reason your name is in it is that I telephoned a counter in the spring and a woman answered and gave it to a stranger in about nine seconds. That is the whole mechanism. It is a person ringing a counter. And the moment I ask a second person for a name, then I am a person who asks people for names, and in about four years I am a woman at a counter with a diary and a list who asks people for their names, and there is no post in this city whose job it is to do that and there is not going to be one, and it would not be a post, it would be a habit of mine, and a habit of mine is the most dangerous thing in this case because I am the only one holding it.**"

"And if somebody else asked me."

"Then somebody else would be a person who asks people for names, and there would be two people who had started it, and in about four years there would be nine, and the ninth one would be a woman with a clipboard in a district saying she was only doing her job."

---

Doreen Abbiss picked the pen up off the marble and put it back in her handbag, and then she asked the question that was underneath all of it, and she asked it to the clerk.

"How many of these have got a name in them."

Petra Lindqvist counted. It took her a while because she counted the way she counts, which is by lifting the corner of the top sheet of each of the four stacks and looking at the fourth field and putting it back, and nobody in the corridor said anything while she did it.

"**One,"** she said. "**Out of about two hundred. Yours.**"

"And nobody put it there because they were asked to."

"No. **You gave it and she wrote it and I ruled the box and all three of them did the right thing and there is not one person in this building who has done anything wrong, and I ruled that box in the week of forty-five and I told one person about it on a Thursday three weeks ago and I have not said so out loud to anybody until now.**"

---

The woman of thirty-one wrote it out on a second sheet of the same paper, at the counter, in pencil, and pushed it four inches away from herself, and Doreen Abbiss read it and put it in her bag.

It had a date on it and a time and a place and what had been shared, and who it was shared with, and a line in a clerk's hand that said the name had been given by the person and written by another, and Doreen Abbiss's own name at the bottom of it in her own hand, which is a thing she can do.

"**That is true,"** said Doreen Abbiss. "**And it is not a correction, and it is not going to help with the one in the stack, and I am going to keep it, and I want everybody in this room to be clear that I know the difference between those two things and that I am not confusing them to be polite.**"

"You are not," said Ianthe Roke.

"I am not. And I am going to say the other thing because I have worked nights for six years and I have been awake since half three and this is the only part of my week that is mine." She put her hand flat on the marble next to the two sheets. "**Nobody took it from me. I gave it. She wrote it. Both of those were the right thing to do at the time and I would like it written down somewhere that I said so, and I am aware that there is nowhere, and I am saying it at a counter at eleven in the morning because if I do not say it here then in about four years the only thing anybody knows about me is that a name of mine is in a box.**"

"You do not have to say it here," said Petra Lindqvist.

"**I know that. I have known that since the spring and it is the reason I have not said it anywhere. And the fact that nobody has to is not the same as nobody will, and I am going now because my bus is in eleven minutes and because I have said a thing at a counter and there is not one thing in this city that is going to happen because of it.**"

---

She went. The clerk put the two sheets back the way they were and hooked the rubber band on and it was a little tighter than it had been that morning, and the woman of thirty-one stood on the other side of the counter with the diary closed and did not open it.

"You did not ask her," said Petra Lindqvist.

"I did not ask her."

"You said the reason before she asked for it. That is the second time somebody has said a reason to me before I have asked for it and both times it was you and one of them was about a line at the foot of an instruction."

"**I have got about eleven weeks of that pencil and four days of nothing else to do with it, and I am going to keep that habit and you are not going to stop me and I am not going to be grateful to you about it.**"

---

It was a quarter past ten in the shop and the docket had four job numbers on it and he had been at the same bench since seven with a cup of cold tea going cold on the same spot on the shelf since seven.

A man of twenty-two was four miles away from a counter in Bower Street for the whole of it, on a fault, and he was not told, and he did not ask, and the reason he did not ask is that he has known since the Wednesday of week forty-seven that the only reason a name was in that box in the first place is that he was stood in a corridor outside the room while it happened and did not knock.

He has not written any of it down. There is a page in a drawer on the first floor of a building in Bower Street with four fields filled in on it and a tick and a date and a time in a hand that is not hers, and there is no box on it because there was no box to rule, and there is no person on it anywhere, and there is not going to be.

There is one box in this manuscript. Nobody has been able to empty it, and nobody has ever been asked to.

*186. Thursday of week 59, ten at night in a repair shop in Lattice Ward, and this entry is one page out of about two hundred with a box in pencil on it that cannot be corrected, and the reason it cannot, in the words of the woman who is owed it.*

*Who was in the room and who was not.* **A counter on a ground floor, four stacks, a rubber band on each, no collection since the spring. A care worker of thirty-eight, six years of nights, who had never been in that building, who came at eleven on a Thursday because the daytime is the only part of her week that is hers and she told the woman who rang her in the spring that the next person this city needs is her. A woman of thirty-one with a key, a diary and a list, who stated her standing before she said anything else and had been told twice this term that she has none. A clerk of fifty-three who ruled the box in the week of forty-five and had told one person about it. Marek Senn was four miles away on a docket for the whole of it and was not told and did not ask, and the entry does not say what he would have done.**

*The two sheets, and the distinction.* **The page the woman of thirty-eight had carried since before the spring, in a plastic bag, with a telephone number at the top, a man on the Friday and her underneath. The top page of the first stack, with the box on it, the box ruled in pencil under the fourth field, three words over it in a clerk's hand, a name in it in pencil in a woman of thirty-one's hand, four correct fields, a date and a time. They are the same form and not the same sheet, and neither of them has anybody's name at the top, and the counter clerk read both out twice.**

*The refusal, and the reason, which is a reason.* **A second name may go in that box if the person it is for asks. The woman of thirty-one refused to ask, said so with the pen in her hand, put the pen down flat, and gave the reason: the only mechanism that put the first name there was a person ringing a counter, and the moment she asks a second person she is a person who asks people for names, and in about four years she is a woman at a counter with a diary and a list who does that, and that is not a post, it is a habit of hers, and a habit of hers is the most dangerous thing in this case because she is the only one holding it. If somebody else asked, there would be two, and then nine, and the ninth would be a woman with a clipboard in a district saying she was only doing her job.**

*The count, which is the finding.* **One name in about two hundred pages. Lifted by the corner of the top sheet of each of the four stacks, looking at the fourth field, putting it back. The person it is for gave it, another person wrote it, a third ruled the box, and all three did the right thing, and the clerk said out loud that there is not one person in that building who has done anything wrong, and that she has not said so to anybody since she said it to one person three weeks ago.**

*What she was given instead, and what it is.* **A second sheet, in pencil, at the counter, with a date, a time, a place, what was shared, who with, and a line in a clerk's hand saying the name was given by the person and written by another, and her own name at the bottom in her own hand. She said, unprompted, that it is true and that it is not a correction and that it is not going to help with the one in the stack, and that she is keeping it and that she is not confusing the two to be polite.**

*What was said out loud at the counter, by the person it is for, which nobody has to have said.* **That nobody took it from her, that she gave it, that another person wrote it, that both of those were the right thing to do at the time, and that if she does not say it at a counter then in about four years the only thing anybody knows about her is that a name of hers is in a box. Nothing was minuted, nobody wrote it down, and the rubber band went back on a little tighter than it was that morning. Entry 186.**
