# Chapter 85 — Twelve Pounds

The Saturday had started at seven and finished at half past three and had paid twelve pounds, and at about ten past four on the Friday afternoon of week twenty-two he was standing outside a gate in Ashfields with his rucksack on and the hard hat in his hand rather than on his head, doing the thing where you make yourself stand somewhere for four minutes before you do a thing you have already decided to do.

The twelve pounds was not a wage. It was a Saturday, and Saturdays had been a Saturday for about a month, and a Saturday was a card rate and a card rate was a number, and the number was the reason he was standing at a gate at ten past four on a Friday instead of on a Sunday night.

He had done the arithmetic on the Tuesday, on the back of a docket, in about nine seconds, and it had not taken nine seconds the second time and it took him about forty minutes the third time, and forty minutes was the honest figure.

He counted to two before he dialled it, standing outside a gate in Ashfields with his rucksack on and the hard hat in his hand rather than on his head, because a woman had put that in his file somewhere and he was not going to give her the other version.

The number rang four times and a woman said, "District service, Lattice."

"My name's Rafi Pell. You rang the shop about me on the Wednesday of last week and I got a message on the Thursday and I have had six days to think about it and I have thought about it every one of them, and I am going to be straight with you about why I am ringing on a Friday afternoon and not on a Sunday, because I would rather you had the real reason than a tidy one."

"Go on."

"Because a card means release callouts, and release callouts are not twelve pounds an hour, and Saturdays are." He said it in one breath and did not soften it. "That is the whole of my reason and I would like it on your file in those words, and if that is the wrong reason then hang up now, because I will not do it for the other reason and you would be able to tell inside about nine seconds."

There was about two seconds of nothing on the line and then a chair.

"My name is Nia Bramhall and I do the training diary for this district and I have got about nine hundred of these to book between now and the end of the term, so I am going to be quick," said the woman. "**That is not the wrong reason. That is the reason about forty per cent of the people who ring me have, and the other sixty per cent say something about safety, and the ones who say safety hang up more often.** Right. Three questions and I am going to read them off the sheet so that we do the same thing. One: have you ever been assessed on a boundary release."

"No."

"Two: have you ever worked at height."

"Some. Wagons, mostly, and a gantry once."

"Three." She stopped, and then did not read it off the sheet. "**Have you ever had a fourth beat at all.**"

Rafi Pell stood outside a gate in Ashfields with a rucksack on.

"No," he said. "Not ever. Not once. And I want to say why, because you have asked me three things and you have not asked me any of them nicely, and I would like to give you one for nothing."

"You do not have to."

"I know I do not have to, that is why I am doing it." He put the hard hat in the rucksack. "**My balance step came off a courier crew and out of a doorway in Nine Bridges and a man called Gus Haldane shouting *keep it up the rail* at me when I was nineteen. There was no card on it and there was no voice on it except him, and he is a person and not a system, and when I was twenty-one I made a voice up out of a wall to replace him, and I have counted to two every time I have ever used it since, and I have never once been able to stop it before it happens.** So no. Never. Not in any room anybody has ever put me in."

There was a pause on the line of about a second and a half.

"**Nobody has," said Nia Bramhall. "That's what the course is.**"

Rafi Pell did not say anything.

"That's not me being nice and it's not me being hard, it's the actual state of it. In this district about four hundred people have done modules one and two and the number of people who came into them with a fourth beat already in their hands is zero, and I have asked every one of them, and about a third of them have a story like yours and the other two thirds have nothing and think they have something." A keyboard. "**So you are not unusual and you are not a project. That is genuinely the good news of this course and I have watched about four people not hear it.**"

"Right."

"Two days. A Tuesday and a Wednesday. Eight to four both days, on the clock, and the card is typed and issued at the end of the second day and you need to bring a form of identity and you will not get it without one." She read something off a screen. "I have got a Tuesday of week twenty-four. That is ten days. And I have got a Wednesday of week twenty-four but I have got eleven people on it and about four of them will not turn up, so do not plan around the Wednesday."

"I'll do the Tuesday and the Wednesday."

"You will do the Tuesday and the Wednesday." Another keyboard. "And the modules are free and the card is free and nobody in my office is allowed to sell you anything, and if anybody rings you about a discounted version of this course, and they will, because you are a good-looking young man on a Saturday crew, then it is not from this company and I want you to ring this number and say so."

"Who rings people about a discounted version."

"Everybody's competitor and one outfit in the next district who does a weekend version with no assessment and a certificate that is not a card, and the certificate is a beautiful piece of card and it is worth nothing and I have watched nine people take it." She let a second go. "**And the last thing, and then I am going to book you, and I am going to say it because you were honest with me and because you asked me a real question in the first ten seconds which is why I have not put you on hold.**"

"Go on."

"You asked what happens if you come and then you do not come back. Nothing happens to you. **A card says what you can do and what you can confirm to somebody else. That is the whole of what a card is. It is not a certificate that says you cannot work without it, and people work without it every day of their lives and they are perfectly safe, and I have got a foreman on the Meridian who has not got one and has never wanted one and would tell you it is a waste of a morning.**"

Rafi Pell stood outside a gate and looked at a wall.

"That's what I was asking," he said.

"I know that's what you were asking and I answered the question you asked and not the one underneath it, because the one underneath it is not a question I am entitled to answer for you." A drawer. "**Now go and do your Saturday. Come in on the Tuesday of week twenty-four with some boots.**"

---

He said it in the shop on the Friday evening of week twenty-two at about half past seven, and he counted to two before he started, out loud, standing in the doorway with the rucksack on, and he did not sit down.

"I said yes."

Marek Senn put the meter bag down.

"Right."

"Two days. A Tuesday and a Wednesday, week twenty-four, eight to four, on the clock, and the card comes at the end of the second day, and it is free, and she told me nobody has ever had a fourth beat and that is what the course is for, which she said in about nine words, and she said it like a woman reading a bus timetable." He shifted the rucksack. "And she said there is a man on the Meridian who has not got one and has never wanted one and thinks it is a waste of a morning. She offered that to me. Unasked. At about four minutes past six on a Friday afternoon."

"She is right about that man."

"I know she is. **I have known that since I was nineteen.**"

The shop was quiet for a bit, and it was the shop, and the extractor had come on to its day pitch and the shutter was down and there was nobody else in the building.

Rafi Pell counted to two again, in the middle of the doorway, without being asked and without meaning to, and then said, "You have not said anything."

"No."

"You have gone quiet in a way you have not gone quiet." He took the rucksack off. "**That is the answer, that is it, isn't it. You have gone quiet and that is the whole answer and you are not going to say it out loud, and I have watched you not say things out loud since the summer and I have never once minded, and this is the first time it has cost you something in front of me.**"

Marek Senn stood at the bench.

"Yes," he said. "It is the fourth beat."

"Right." Rafi Pell did not move for about three seconds, which was about nine of his. "Then say the next bit, because you have got that face on, and the next bit is the part you would want to say and cannot."

"No."

"Because you cannot, or because you will not."

"Because I cannot," said Marek Senn, "and it is not the fourth condition and it is not a rule and I want you to hear the reason, because you have just handed me the only thing I have wanted to hand you in three weeks and I am not going to be allowed to do the one useful thing with it, and you are entitled to know why before you find out in about a month."

"Go on."

"**If a man in a lanyard says a thing like that in a room, then it is a company saying it about itself.** That is all. That is the whole reason and it took me a term to work out and it is not about the department and it is not about the share. **A company in this city has spent a term being described by a technician who was good at a thing in a corridor, and that sentence did the first damage anybody has done, and the sentence is *the technician said*, and if I say this one then the sentence becomes the technician said that and there is a company behind it and a man behind the company, and in about four years there will be a document somewhere with a company's name at the top of it and my name in it, and everything anybody has done in a market or a market kitchen or a book in a shop gets read as a campaign by that company.** That is why I cannot say it. Not the condition. Me."

Rafi Pell put the rucksack down on the floor of the shop.

"Right," he said. "Then I am going to say the bit I have not said and then I am going to go and do a Saturday."

"Go on."

"**I have been the easiest person in this city to get hold of since the middle of the summer and I have known it and I have not done anything about it, and I want it in front of you now while you cannot say anything, because it is yours as much as mine.**" He was not angry, which was worse. "I have no fourth beat. That is not a hole in me, that is the good news, that is what I have said to about four rooms since the summer. And a boy with nothing is the easiest thing in a city to give something to, because he cannot lose it, and he has got somewhere to put it, and somebody has spent eleven years making a card that is free and portable and means that there is a voice in it, and he has done it by being generous, and every single person in this who has ever been kind to me about it has been kind to me about it."

"You are not the reason that course exists."

"I know I am not the reason it exists. **I am the reason it works on me.** And I have been keeping a state, and I told you I was keeping it, and I want to say that the state is finished and I want to say that I know exactly which day it finished and it was about four minutes past six this evening on a Friday when a woman read me three questions off a sheet and then stopped reading and asked me one off her own head." He picked the rucksack up again. "**Nobody has asked me for anything. That is what I asked you to hold for me, and you have kept it since the Saturday of week twenty and you have never once used it, and I am releasing it. Not because of you. Because I have said yes to a Tuesday and I cannot be a man who is not asked for anything and is on a list.**"

"You did not have to tell me any of that."

"No. And I have told you anyway, and I would like you to notice that I have told you in the first person, with my name at the end, at the door, on a Friday evening, the way I have done it twice before in front of witnesses, and I have never once done it to make a room go quiet, and I am not going to start."

He was gone up the street with the rucksack on his back and the hard hat in his hand.

"Right," said Marek Senn, to a closed door.

He stood there for about a second and a half and then he said the thing he had been not saying for about four minutes, out loud, to a shop, which he had done twice in a term and both times had been followed by about an hour of not writing it down.

"**A boy of twenty-one has just been given a thing that is free, and it is going to make him safer, and it is going to cost him the only thing he has ever had, and I am standing in a repair shop knowing both of those, and the entire reason I know them is that I have spent fourteen weeks writing down that this is what it does.**"

Then he picked the meter bag up off the bench and did about an hour and a half of work that did not require thinking, and he got it right, and at about nine o'clock he washed his hands, which he does before he writes anything in the book, and sat down with it, and the lamp over the bench had been on since about half past four.

Marek Senn stood in a shop with the meter bag on the bench and did not write anything down for about twenty minutes.

He wrote the entry from about nine and the lamp was on and it is a short one and he has decided that a short one is the right length for tonight.

*88. Friday of week 22, nine at night. The turn of this movement happened at four minutes past six this evening on a telephone in Ashfields, and it finished at half past seven in a doorway in Lattice Ward, and I have been standing in this shop for two hours pretending I do not know what it was.*

*The twelve pounds, and the forty minutes, and the fact that I am not going to pretend it was a smaller number.* He did the arithmetic on the Tuesday on the back of a docket in about nine seconds, and it did not take nine seconds the second time, and it took about forty minutes the third time, and forty minutes is the honest figure. A Saturday is a card rate. A card is a condition of a release callout, and a release callout is not twelve pounds an hour and is not nine either and the difference between the two numbers is the whole of the reason he was standing at a gate in Ashfields at ten past four on a Friday afternoon instead of in a kitchen on a Sunday night, and he told the woman that in the first ten seconds of the call without being asked, and then told her that if that was the wrong reason she could hang up. **She did not hang up. She said it was the reason about forty per cent of the people who ring her have, and that the ones who say it is for safety hang up more often. That is a true sentence and I have verified it and it is also the sentence that this whole case exists to make into a lie, and it is going to be a lie in about a year in about four hundred kitchens.**

*What he got.* Two days. A Tuesday and a Wednesday of week twenty-four, eight to four, on the clock, a card typed and issued at the end of the second day, and it is free. And a woman with a training diary and about nine hundred of these to book told him, unasked, at about four minutes past six on a Friday afternoon, that **nobody in this district has ever come into modules one and two with a fourth beat already in their hands, and that is not me being kind, that is the actual state of it, and he is not unusual and he is not a project, and she has watched about four people not hear it.** She read three questions off a sheet and then stopped and asked a fourth one off her own head and the fourth one was the only one that mattered and it was not on the form. **That is the whole case, in one question, asked by a woman doing a diary, and she is not lying and she is not the author and she is not a villain and she is going to be right for the rest of her working life and she will be right in a way that does not help.**

*And what he said about it, on the way out, and it is the part of tonight that I am actually carrying.* A card says what you can do and what you can confirm to somebody else, and it is not a certificate that says you cannot work without it, and people work without it every day and are perfectly safe, and there is a foreman on the Meridian who has not got one and has never wanted one. She offered that to him because he had been honest in the first ten seconds. **She is right. And a foreman who has never wanted a card is the man who stands on a wall leg at half past seven and finishes a release with his own voice, and there is no record of that anywhere in the world except in the head of a woman on a bank, and he has no card, and he does not need one, and about two years ago a woman in a sling was asked on a telephone to confirm on paper that she did not have one, and confirmed.**

*And what I could not say, and the reason, and the reason is me and not the condition.* He asked me for the next bit. He said: you have got that face on, and the next bit is the part you would want to say and cannot. **If a man in a lanyard says it in a room then it is a company saying it about itself, and the first person anybody can hear it from is me, and in about four years there is a document with a company's name on it and mine in it, and every market and every kitchen and every book gets read as a campaign.** He said right. He counted to two in the middle of my shop without being asked and without meaning to, and then he told me the rest of it, and I am going to write the rest of it down because I have never had it said at me before.

*He said: I have been the easiest person in this city to get hold of since the middle of the summer and I have known it and I have not done anything about it.* A boy with no fourth beat cannot lose one, and he has got somewhere to put it, and a card is free and portable and it means there is a voice in it, and every single person in this who has been kind to him about it has been kind. **I have spent a term refusing to be the fourth thing in a room and the way I have managed that is by being in rooms, and a man of twenty-one has just worked out that he is the fourth thing in a room I have not got to yet, and he has said it to me at my own bench because I could not answer him, and I have had that sentence available to me for nine weeks and I did not use it on him because I was saving it.**

*And the state, which I was holding for him.* **He released it. He named the minute: about four minutes past six this evening, when a woman read three questions off a sheet and then stopped reading and asked one off her own head. He said he is not doing it because of me. He said it because he has said yes to a Tuesday and he cannot be a man who is not asked for anything and is on a list.** I have kept that state for about two weeks and never used it, and he released it in a doorway, in the first person, with his name at the end, and he did it in the way he did it in a market in week sixteen and he told me he has never once done it to make a room go quiet.

*And I have got nothing.* That is the entry. **I have got a Tuesday of week twenty-four in my calendar and I have not got a sentence, and I have not got a sentence because there is no sentence in this city that is true and that does not come out of a man's mouth as a company's, and the only other thing available is to be a good man at a bench, and I have been a good man at a bench for five weeks and it has turned out that is a way of being a man who is not in the room, and I was extremely comfortable in it.
