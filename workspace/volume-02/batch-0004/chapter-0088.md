# Chapter 88 — The Hand Up

Week twenty-three was the first week since the middle of the summer in which nothing happened to Marek Senn at all.

He was on the road four days. The out-of-hours line rang nine times in five days and every one of them was something a person could do themselves off a plate, and he wrote none of them down. The Mondays were not his and he was not on a bank. There was no bundle, no letter, no reference, no man at a gate who was pleased to see him, and no woman with a bar who rang him to tell him she had not said anything to anybody. He did about thirty-one jobs in five days and got twenty-nine of them right first time and slept.

He noticed it on the Friday and wrote four words about it in the corner of a docket and then did not write the fifth word, which was *and*, because he did not know what came after it.

---

Rafi Pell did not come into the shop on the Tuesday morning of week twenty-four, because he was on a building on a trading estate at eight o'clock with a form of identity in his coat, and Marek Senn did not offer to drive him, and neither of them said anything about either of those things, and Rafi Pell noticed both.

"You weren't going to offer."

"No."

"Good," said Rafi Pell. "I was going to ask you not to, and I had a whole thing ready, and I don't have to use it now and I am going to think about that for about a week." He shifted the rucksack. "**I'm not saying you're wrong. I'm saying I had a reason ready and you hadn't and I would have had to have one in a second and a half and you'd have used mine.**"

"I would not have used it."

"You'd have stood there," said Rafi Pell, "and I would have ended up in the van. Goodbye."

He was gone up the street at twenty to seven with the hard hat in the rucksack and no lanyard, because a company that has not given you a card has not given you a lanyard for its own building, and a pair of boots he had bought himself in a market in the Saltmarket for eleven pounds and a coat with a form of identity in it.

At about two o'clock in the afternoon the shop telephone rang and it was a man he had never spoken to.

"Mate, is that the out-of-hours."

"It is."

"Right." A long pause, and a television somewhere behind him. "**My name's Dai Pritchard. I know you know that, because I'm on the rota, and I know I'm on the rota because nobody's rung me about not answering it, which I've been waiting about a fortnight for, and I've worked out that not being rung is a decision somebody has made and I'd rather be told it was a decision.**"

"You are right that it is a decision," said Marek Senn. "I know the name on the rota. I do not know the reason and I have not asked you for it, and I have been on it for a fortnight and I have never once picked up, and I have thought about it about nine times a day."

"Right." Another pause. "**I did not answer it because of the paper.**"

"I know."

"No, you do not, because I am going to say it and you are going to have to be the man who heard it. It was the day that bundle came in. Somebody put two hundred names and eleven of ours on a wall and I read mine on a counter in a canteen with my tea in my hand, and the line about me said card status, and I have been doing this nineteen years and I have never once had a card and nobody has ever asked me for one, and I sat in my van for about an hour on the Wednesday and decided I was not going to be a man you could ring. I have still not answered it. I want that said on the phone. I have not stopped reading it as a thing about me and I have not stopped being the man you could not ring, and I want to know what the rota says on Monday."

"The rota says your name on it on Monday."

"Good," said Dai Pritchard. "Then I will decide on Sunday night. And tell your manager I have not resigned and I have not stopped doing my days and I have not been rude to anybody, and I have had about three weeks of this and I would like it to be over in whatever way it is going to be over." A chair. "**And I am not going to explain the paper to you, because you are a technician and you did not put it on the wall, and I have had that argument with about four people this month and I am tired of it.**"

"You do not have to explain it to me," said Marek Senn. "But I am going to say one thing back and then I am going to go and do a job. **A company is not a wall. A company is four men on a rota and a woman in an office and about nine hundred other people who have never read a page of anything, and a piece of paper with your name on it is not a decision about you, it is a decision about whether a company can be invoiced.** I do not know whether you will believe that and I am not going to argue with you, and you are not wrong that a company stopped answering a service line in the same week that a company published a list, because that is exactly what happened, and it happened for two different reasons, and neither of them was a person deciding anything about you."

There was nothing on the line for about four seconds.

"That," said Dai Pritchard, "is the first time anybody in that outfit has said the wall and the rota in the same sentence. **Right. Monday.**"

Click.

Marek Senn sat in the shop for about a minute. Then he got a piece of A4 out of the drawer under the bench and wrote four lines on it and read them back and put it in the drawer under the bench, with the letter that has a reference number in the corner and page four that has no page before the page on it.

Four lines and no date, because a date on it turns a man's patience into a company's exposure.

Marek Senn got in the van and drove to a customer in Nine Bridges and did a job he could do in his sleep and then sat in it for about four minutes at half past eight.

---

He came back to the shop at about ten past six on the Tuesday evening of week twenty-four with the smell of a different building on him and he stood in the doorway and did not come in, and Marek Senn looked up, and Rafi Pell counted to two.

"Don't," he said. "I know. You cannot say anything and I have decided that I do not care, and I have decided it in a canteen with about nine other people in it and I have been thinking about it for eight hours, and I am going to stand in this doorway and tell you what happened and you are going to stand there and not say anything, and at the end I am going to tell you the thing I actually came to tell you and you are not going to be able to do a single thing about it."

"Go on."

"They're good." He said it flatly, the way a man reports a reading. "**They're genuinely good. That's the first thing and I'm saying it before anything else so that you cannot tell me later that I softened it. The woman running it has been doing it eleven years and there is not one thing in that room that is decoration, and about half of the eight hours is a man with a lanyard saying a word to a person, and that is the course, and that is all the course is, and I have been in a college for two years and nobody has ever put it like that.**"

Marek Senn stood behind a bench and did not say anything.

"There's four steps and then there's the wait. **And the wait is the bit. That's what I didn't know. Everybody thinks the four steps are the practice and the wait is a bit of politeness before the last one, and it is not, it is the practice, and it is the whole of the practice, and it is about eleven parts long and she says the same eleven parts out loud about four times a day.**"

"Which eleven parts."

Rafi Pell looked at the floor of the shop.

"**Scope. He is in scope. I confirm your scope. Do not release on your own judgement. If I do not say it, you withdraw and you report it.**" He counted them on his fingers without meaning to. "That's about eleven and it's about the same eleven every time, and she says it the same way every time, and after about an hour you stop hearing the words and you can hear whether she has said them or not, and that is the bit that got me, Marek, that is the bit that actually got me, and I have been doing it for about two years."

The extractor came on. Neither of them said anything for about four seconds.

"They ask you things," said Rafi Pell. "At about eleven, after the morning, in about nine minutes. And the question was: who in this room already does a practice that nobody taught them. And it was not a trick and there was no right answer, and about four hands went up and mine went up before I had decided it, which I have been thinking about for eight hours and I have decided that is fine and I am not going to be embarrassed about it."

"Go on."

"I said: I work on a wagon and I have a step for the rail, and I did not learn it on a course, I learned it off a crew and out of a doorway in Nine Bridges and a man called Gus Haldane shouting *keep it up the rail* at me when I was nineteen, and for about two years I have had a voice in my head that is not his and not anybody's, and I made it up out of a wall, and I count to two before I use it, and I have never been hurt by it and I have watched about nine other people not be hurt by it."

Marek Senn put his hand flat on the bench.

"And she stopped," said Rafi Pell. "**And she stopped the room. Not dramatically, she just said hold on a second and everybody stopped talking, and she came over to where I was and she crouched down, and she is a small woman and I am a big one, and she said: say that again about the wall.**"

"And you said it again."

"I said it again and I said the same words, because I have said it four times now and it does not improve." He shifted the rucksack off his shoulder. "**And she said: that is the fourth beat. That is the fourth beat, you have been doing it for two years, and it is the only thing in this room that actually matters, and every one of you is going to be asked to do that and most of you cannot, because the four steps are easy and the wait is not, and the wait is a voice, and you cannot buy a voice.**"

He stopped there and stood in the doorway for about three seconds.

"And then she said the other thing and I have not been able to put it anywhere." He looked up. "**She said: you have been doing it without a card, and nobody has ever been hurt, and you have done it two thousand times, and you have had nobody to confirm your scope to, and if you get hurt on a Saturday at two in the morning the only person who can say that you were in scope is you, and a man who is in scope and hurt is a report, and a report is a company asking you questions about the week you were hurt.**"

The shop was very quiet.

"And then she said the last bit and she said it to the whole room, not to me." Rafi Pell counted to two. "**She said: most of you are going to go out of here with a card and you are going to be safer, and it is not because the card makes you safer, it is because the card is the only thing in this city that puts a person between you and a decision you should not be making on your own at two in the morning. And in about four years somebody is going to tell one of you that all of this was a paper exercise and that the cards are a way of keeping hold of people, and I want you to be able to say that you were in a room on this date and a woman with a lanyard said it out loud, and you will be able to say it because there will be eleven of you in it and eleven is not a number anybody can be alone in.**"

Marek Senn did not move for about four seconds and then he said, "What did you say to that."

"I said, good," said Rafi Pell. "**And I counted to two, and then I said the other thing. I said: I have been the fourth thing in five buildings in six weeks because a man in a lanyard told me so in a market, and this is the first room I have been in where being the fourth thing was the point, and I would like it minuted that I said that in front of about nine people and I am not going to take it back.**"

"And she said."

"She said nobody takes minutes in this room," said Rafi Pell, "and that it is a classroom and not a hearing, and that the man who wrote on the wall that there is a fifth room in this city is in a company and not in this city, and that if I want a record of it I should write it down myself and put it somewhere it cannot be taken out of it, and I said I have got a rule for that in a market and she said good, keep it."

He was quiet for a second.

"And then she said, *are you the courier, the one off Weaver Street*, and I said I was, and she said the name came up on a booking sheet and she was not going to read the rest of the sheet out loud in a room, and I said thank you, and that is the entire day, and I have been in a college for two years and a woman in a lanyard just told me the thing I am good at is the thing that matters and did not read a piece of paper out loud to prove it."

Marek Senn stood behind a bench in a shop in Lattice Ward.

"Rafi."

"Don't."

"**I have not got a sentence.**"

"I know you haven't," said Rafi Pell. "I have known that since about half past three this afternoon in a canteen and I have come here anyway, and I have come here because I wanted a person to not have a sentence, and I am aware that that is a horrible thing to want from somebody and I am aware that I have had it since I was sixteen. **You are not the fourth thing in the room. You are the man I told, and there is a difference and it is the only difference I have got.**"

He was gone up the street.

She rang the shop at about ten past nine on the Tuesday evening of week twenty-four, and it was Tuesday, and neither of them said anything about it being Tuesday.

"Marek. One question and then I am going to do a return and I would like to ask it first so that I am not thinking about it while I write."

"Go on."

"What did you do on the Saturday of week twenty-two."

Marek Senn sat down on a stool.

"I was there," he said.

There was nothing on the line for about three seconds.

"I am not going to pretend that is a small answer," said Talia Venn. "**You told me you were not going, and you said it in one word in a second and a half with no reason, and I knew on the Sunday that you were going, and I asked you on the Tuesday morning anyway and I said out loud that I was going to ask, and you gave me the answer, and I have spent fourteen days not asking.** And I am not going to be a woman who says she did not ask. Ask me the next one."

"Who asked you."

"Me. Nobody asked me, which is the entire problem, and it has taken me fourteen days and a return to work out that I did not ask because I was protecting something, and I would like to say what I was protecting, because I have worked it out and it is not flattering."

"Go on."

"I was protecting being the woman who does not ask a man to do anything." She was not being dramatic and she was not being hard. "**I was refused a room in this city four times. I have said *independence* out loud about nine times in two years and I have put it in a form, and on the Saturday morning of week twenty-two there was a chair in a room on the third floor of that building that I could have had, and I could have had it because a woman of sixty-one lends thirty of them to anybody who asks, and I did not ask, because a woman in a wheelchair who is not one of the twenty women and who has a registration number and no fixed employer does not ask a nursery for a chair. And I have spent fourteen days calling that professional and it is not, it is pride, and I have been calling it independence for about nine years and I would like it minuted that I have said so on a Tuesday evening to a man who lied to me in one word on a Tuesday morning.**"

He did not say anything for a second.

"You could have had a chair," he said.

"I could have had a chair."

"That is the price of not asking, and you have just paid it out loud, and I am not going to be a woman who says I told you so, and I am also not going to be a woman who is sorry for you, and I want you to have both of those clearly."

"I know. Do not be sorry at me."

"**Then here is my part and I am giving it to you in one go because if I stop I will not start.** I was not there to be kind. I was there because a card that a woman of thirty-one made at her own kitchen table was going to be in a room and I wanted to see whether a thing made out of nothing was any good, and it was any good, and I am telling you that on a Tuesday evening because you did not tell me on a Saturday, and that is the difference between us and I do not think either of us gets to be the better one."

There was nothing on the line for about two seconds.

"Go on," she said.

"There is a card on that table," said Marek Senn, "and it is correct, and it is free, and it cost a woman of thirty-one four nights at a kitchen table to make it, and by next spring there will be about four hundred of them and nobody has to sell one to anybody, and I stood next to it for half a minute and I could not say one word, and the reason I could not say one word is that the sentence which explains it comes out of a man's mouth as a company's, and if it does that then in about four years somebody will be able to find out that the woman who made it was taught by a company. **And that is why I could not come and tell you about it on the Saturday, and not because of the chairs, and I am telling you that now at ten past ten on a Tuesday night, which is the worst possible time to say a thing like it and the only time I have got.**"

Talia Venn did not say anything for about four seconds and then she said, "**All right. Thank you. I am not going to thank you for it and I am not going to be gracious about it and I have just said thank you, and that is a sentence I have not said to you in two years, and I would like both of us to notice it.**"

"I noticed."

"Ring me on a Tuesday," said Talia Venn. "Do not bring me a document. And Marek — **do not lie to me about a Saturday again.** Not because of the case and not because of anything in it. Because I have spent two years building a practice out of not asking people for things, and you have just told me the reason I did not ask, and I cannot unlearn it in a week, and if you lie to me about one more ordinary afternoon then I will not be able to tell the difference between the part of me that is careful and the part of me that is proud, and I need to be able to tell the difference for a good while yet."

"I will not."

"Good," she said. "Now go and be a good man at a bench. I know you are. It is not the same as being in the room and I have thought about that a great deal this week and I am not going to tell you which way I have landed."

*91. Tuesday of week 24, twenty past ten at night. Week twenty-three was the first week since the middle of the summer in which nothing happened to me at all, and I have not written four words about it in a book until tonight, and I am going to leave it at four, because a week like that is not an event and if I make it into one I am arranging.*

*What happened today, and it is the movement and I knew it was going to be.* A man in a lanyard said about eleven words about nine times a day to about eleven people in a room on a trading estate, and the words are **scope, he is in scope, I confirm your scope, do not release on your own judgement, if I do not say it you withdraw and you report it**, and I have them on paper four feet from me in a drawer and in a book in a shop and on a page four that somebody photocopied and left the rest of out, and I have not been in the room and I have not been asked and I was not going to offer and he noticed in about a second that I was not going to offer and he said good, which I have not stopped thinking about.

*And what he said in a canteen at about eleven in the morning, in the first person, with his name at the end.* She asked who in that room already does a practice that nobody taught them, and four hands went up and his went up before he had decided it, and he told a room of about nine people that he has had a voice in his head for two years that is not a man's and not anybody's, and that he made it out of a wall when he was twenty-one, and that he counts to two before he uses it, and that he has never been hurt by it and has watched about nine other people not be hurt by it. **And then the woman crouched down in front of him and said: that is the fourth beat.**

**Nobody in that room knew. That is the sentence I have been trying to get to for a term and I have got there in the wrong order, on a Tuesday, through a man who is twenty-one, standing up, in front of nine strangers, with his name at the end, as a piece of good news about himself.** I have spent a term looking for the moment a hinge catches a person and I have been looking at cards and forms and eleven years of a page four, and it caught the one person in this city who has nothing to lose and something to be given, and it caught him in a canteen at eleven in the morning, and it did it kindly, and the woman who did it is not the author and is not a villain and is a small woman with a lanyard and eleven years of a job.

**I have been trying to build that number for a term. I have been trying to build it out of four people who can end a share and a woman in a sling and a man of thirty-one and a man of twenty-one and a first-year's forearm in a regional return, and I have done it by refusing things and by putting them in drawers, and the arithmetic has never once produced eleven. She has produced it in one sentence on a Tuesday, out loud, to a room, with no condition, at no cost to her whatsoever, and it will hold for about two years and then it will not, and I am not going to be the one who says so, and that is the correct decision and it is also the most expensive thing anybody has ever not said to me.**

*And what he came to the shop to tell me, which was not that.* He came to tell me that I had no sentence. He said: **I have known that since half past three this afternoon and I have come here anyway, and I have come here because I wanted a person to not have a sentence, and I am aware that that is a horrible thing to want from somebody, and I have had it since I was sixteen.** And then he said that I am not the fourth thing in the room, I am the man he told, and that there is a difference and it is the only difference he has got. **I have been the fourth thing in a room nine times this term and I have thought about the sentence *the technician said* every single time, and a man of twenty-one has just told me there is another category and he is right and it is not in any form I have ever been given.**
