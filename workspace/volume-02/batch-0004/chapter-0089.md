# Chapter 89 — Forty-One Lines

Asha Reed's sentence was the same on the first Wednesday of week twenty-four as it had been on the first Wednesday of week sixteen and the first Wednesday of week twenty, and she said it standing up at the front of a common room on the Ninth Street block with nine first years in it and one voice in the room, which was hers.

"The case is Senn's and the technique is nobody's yet. Four times now. I have said it four times and I am not going to vary it, because the day I vary it is the day somebody in this room can tell which half of it I think is true."

Nine people looked at a table.

"And the other thing, which I have been not saying for three weeks and I am going to say it in about four minutes, so you can all go back to being bored."

Nobody moved.

"In three weeks I have been asked by four separate people whether I think they should be in a room about this. **Four. Two of them wanted a room, one of them wanted to know whether it was safe to be in one, and one of them wanted to know whether it was safe not to be in one, and I have said no to all four and I have said the same no to all four, and I would like somebody in this room to notice that the fifth person to be asked about a room in this case in three weeks is me, and I am nineteen, and I am a student, and I am not a body in a room for anybody.**"

"Why are you saying it," said Toby Ansell.

"Because four weeks ago I said it had made four people in this room better at this and made me worse, and I have had four weeks to check whether that was dramatic, and it was not dramatic, it was a direction." She put her hands on the front of the bench. "**I can now name a place where a member of staff was read a sentence out in front of forty-one people and put his own name against it in a file, and I could not do that in week sixteen. I have got a name and a plate and a supplier and a bundle on a counter, and I have lost the ability to say there is nobody in it who can say no, and nobody took either of those things off me, and I have been asked four times in three weeks to put a body in a room, and I have worked out this week that the only people in this city who are being asked to be in a room are the people who can be found.** That is not an argument for saying yes. It is an argument for understanding what is asking me."

The room did that thing rooms do.

"Thank you," said Asha Reed, "that is all of it and it took three minutes and I have four minutes of it left and I am not going to spend it on the same thing. **One more thing and then you can all go.** If anybody in this room is ever asked to put their name on a piece of paper about this, and there is nobody in the room above you, then the answer is not no. The answer is *who is the last person in this room and are they findable*, and if you cannot answer that in about four seconds then you are not being asked, you are being used, and the difference is about a sentence and you can check it."

He went to the market first, at about half past eleven, because it was there and because he had been going to it for a term and had been in a shop.

There was a copy of the bundle on a trestle table weighed down with a bag of onions, face up, at about eleven o'clock on a Wednesday morning, and four days after the Wednesday of week twenty-one it was on its fifth or sixth table in this city, and it was not being passed about by anybody. **That was the part he had got wrong for four days and had got right on about the Thursday. A bundle with two hundred names in it and no source in it is not spread by a person. It is spread by eleven frightened people who each put it down somewhere and then cannot remember where, and there is no version of that which anybody can be caught doing.**

A woman of about thirty-eight in a district fleece was standing at the table with a shopping bag on her hip, not reading it, just standing at it, the way people stand at a thing.

"You've read it," Marek Senn said, before he could stop himself, and then heard himself and put his hand in his pocket.

"I've read the middle bit about the eleven," she said. "**My cousin's on the eleven.**"

"Right."

"He hasn't got a card. Neither has my cousin and neither have I, and I have been doing this for eleven years and I have never once been asked for one, and that is what I keep getting stuck on when I read it, because the whole of that page is about a box that somebody has typed NONE into and my cousin has never had anybody ask him a single question about his shoulder, and I have worked out in four days that the two of those are the same page and they are not the same problem and I have not got to the bottom of it." She shifted the bag. "Are you the company?"

"No," said Marek Senn.

"You've asked for my name."

"No," he said. "**I have not and I am not going to, and the reason I am answering you at all is that you have told me a thing about your cousin's shoulder in a market and you did not have to, and I would like to be able to tell you that the reason nobody has asked him is not a decision anybody made about him.**"

"It's not?"

"There are about nine hundred of those bundles in this district and every one of them has a box like your cousin's in it, and the box gets what it gets because asking is a phone call and typing is a key on a keyboard, and the decision that was made was not about him, it was about whether a company can send an invoice." He stopped. "And I am a service engineer in a supply company and that is the only credential I have got and it is worth nothing this morning."

She looked at him for about two seconds and then said, "That's the first thing anybody's said to me this week that didn't come out of that page," and put her bag down, and read the middle bit again with her finger on the line, and Marek Senn walked away and stood about thirty feet away in the middle of a market on a Wednesday lunchtime and had the feeling of a man who has just been the fourth thing in a room with one person in it.

The Exchange meeting is on the first Wednesday of the month at ten past one in a back room in the Saltmarket with the trestle tables pushed together and the shutters half up, and about eleven people came, and Bea Nunn had a tin, and the book was at forty lines.

She did not do a photograph and she did not do a round. She took the cards out of the tin and put them face down in a row and then turned them over one at a time.

"Six," she said. "Four minutes each. Same as always. The rule is the caller's words, the first person, a signature at the end, and my name in the fourth column, and I have not changed the rule since the Wednesday of week twelve and I am not going to change it this month, and if anybody in this room has something they want changed about the book they can put it in the tin and it will be read out at the next one in four minutes like everything else in here, including the complaints."

**One.** The card was a piece of A4 with a card in a plastic sleeve on top of it, and the card said thirty plastic chairs and a Saturday, in block capitals, in a hand that took its time, and it was signed.

Ena Truslove read it out herself. She had asked for that, and the reason she gave was that she had said yes in four seconds three times and nobody had said it back to her once, and the fourth thing is a room in which a sentence gets said out loud by the person who said it.

It took four minutes and about nine of the eleven people did not say anything, and when it was read somebody at the back said, "Well, that's a nice thing," in the voice people use, and Bea Nunn wrote his reaction in the fourth column and it said *said that, once, and did not ask*.

**Two.** A boiler. Four minutes. Nobody argued and the man who brought it had written it in the third column before he came, which is against nothing and is what Bea calls being prepared.

**Three.** This is where the man of about fifty-two said it.

He had come to the meeting for the boiler, and he had a district jacket on over a fleece and he had the particular stillness of a man who has been in a lot of rooms where somebody is about to say something. He waited for the chairs to be done and then he said, "That's the third one in a month."

Bea Nunn did not look up.

"That is a chair, mate."

"That is a chair that went to a college in the summer with no name on it, and a chair that went to a district with no name on it, and a chair that went to a company on the Saturday with no name on it, and I have got a man on my crew who has been on a Saturday course with no name on it and a woman on my crew who has been sent on one in a fortnight." He put his hands flat on the table. "**There is no such thing as a district, fellers. There is a technique and it is being handed round and there is nobody putting their name to it, and I am not being daft, I have heard the same from two different people in two different weeks.**"

The room went about two seconds quieter and nobody in it said a word, and Marek Senn, who was sitting at the end of the trestle table on the side with the light behind him and who had been in that room twice in a corridor and never inside it, sat very still and did not say one word, and he had a man of forty-one's voice in his head saying *in about a month a lot of people are going to believe that the danger is a group of people called something, and that a technique is being passed about in secret by people who know what they are doing, and it will be believed, and it will be believed more strongly than the truth*, and he could not say a single word of it, because the only sentence that would answer it begins *a man who is profitable from it told me in advance*, and there is no version of that which does not put a name on it.

"**Item four,**" said Bea Nunn, and she did not look at the man, and she did not agree with him, and she did not disagree with him, and that was the correct thing to do and it cost her the room.

**Four.** A man of about thirty-eight brought a card that he had written in block capitals and it was about a first-year's forearm in a regional return, and it was in the third person and it did not have a signature, and Bea Nunn read it out in full and then said, "That is a report and not a request, and it goes in the book because I have never once refused to read a thing, and it goes in the book with your name in the fourth column and none in the first, and in four years somebody is going to read this book and find a line with no name on it, and that is the correct record of what happened in a room where a boy did not want to be named, and I am not going to apologise for it."

He said, "That's fair," and looked at the table.

**Five.** This one was Ines Sarr, and it was about a college and a first-floor room and what happens to a person who turns up, and the room spent four minutes on it and produced the same four answers it produces every time, and one of the four this time was worse than the others and it was a woman of about forty-four who said:

"The answer is a person's job. Not a department's, not a trust's, a person's. And the reason there is not one is that the last person in that room is always somebody who has already been hurt, and you cannot hire that, and if you try you will hire a person who is going to leave inside a year for a post with a duty of care in it, and then the room is in a first floor with a supervisor in it who has never been hurt, and in four years somebody will read a file about that room and the word they will use is *culture*."

Nobody argued with her, and she was right, and the room was not improved by being right.

**Six.** Bea Nunn's own, which she wrote on a card before the meeting and did not put in the tin and did not hand to the taker, and it was about the book, and she read it out herself and then wrote three words on the back of it in her own hand and did not enter a line, and the reason she gave for that is at the end of it.

She said, and the room did not go quiet, and it is the only item in nine years she has ever taken at less than a reading: **"The book is at forty lines and this afternoon it goes to forty-one, and in nine years I have taken about four hundred of these and every single one of them was a thing somebody was frightened of. The one on Monday was not frightened. The one on Monday was annoyed.** Ena Truslove signed it, and she has not signed anything in nine years, and the reason she signed it is that she did not want anything out of it, and that is a different kind of object in a book and I do not know what to do with it, and I am not going to build a process for it, because a book that gets a process for every kind of card in it becomes a system, and a system is a thing a room can be asked to fill in, and this is not one. **And I am not putting this one in the book either, because a thing I write about the book and then put in the book is a line that gets read out at the next one like everything else in here, and in about four years somebody is going to want to know what this room thought about itself and I am not going to be a document about it.**" She turned the card over and wrote three words on the back of it in her own hand, and nobody in the room could see what they were from where they were sitting, and she did not read them out, and the taker is the person who took the card and there is no fourth column on it because it is not in the book.

Nine people said something at once and Bea Nunn let them, and then she put the tin on the table and said, "Cards."

"**That is the first card in this tin in nine years that has come in from a man with a job, and I am not going to read anything into that, because a man with a job is a man who can be found, and I would rather he had put it in here than in a corridor.** It goes in the book at the next one. The first Wednesday of next month. Four minutes. My name in the fourth column and his in the first, in his own words, with a date on it that he has not written and I am not going to ask him for."

Marek Senn sat at the end of the trestle table for about four minutes after it finished, with a card in his coat pocket that he had written on the Saturday night in about eleven seconds and had not shown to anybody, and the man of fifty-two in the district jacket put his coat on at the door and said to nobody in particular, "I am not being daft, you know.
