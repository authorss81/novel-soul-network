# Chapter 86 — The Third Floor

The building was four storeys of the kind that was put up in this city about forty years ago for an office that has gone, and it had a reception on the ground floor that had a plant in it and a lift with a mirror in it, and Marek Senn went up in the lift on the Saturday morning of week twenty-two with a lanyard on and no work order in his pocket, and he was the only man in the building.

Third floor. A door with a piece of A4 in a plastic wallet with a list on it, and the list had a tick against every name and no names, and about twenty women inside and about eleven plastic chairs and the other nineteen borrowed from a room at the back that somebody else had.

The lift had a mirror in it and he looked at himself in it for about three seconds and what he saw was a man of twenty-two in a work shirt with a company lanyard round his neck going up to a floor of a building in Bower Street on a Saturday morning with nothing in his hands, and he understood, standing in a mirror in a lift at about two to eleven, that there is no version of this that anybody watching the doors open is going to understand.

There was a woman getting in at the ground floor with a wheeled bag and she looked at the lanyard and then at him and then at the buttons, and she did not say anything, and neither did he, and the doors closed.

There is a rule about a lanyard that is not a rule, and it is the only one he has found that works: a lanyard is not a thing you wear into a building, it is a thing that lets a gate open, and the moment you carry it through a door that a booking did not open, you have changed what you are and you have changed what the room is. The gate this morning was opened by a fitter's handwriting in a depot in Lattice. The lift was opened by a button. Everything after the lift was opened by a woman with a list with a tick on it and no names, and there was nothing at all in the chain between him and that room except that he had decided to be in it, and he had decided to be in it on a Saturday, on his own, unasked, and the reason he had decided to be in it was that gratitude has got nowhere to go so it does not move and the only thing anybody can do about that is be somewhere on the day.

He had been in this building once, on the first floor, for about nine minutes, in week fourteen, and a woman in a district coat had told him in four sentences not to be helpful, and he had not been inside since. The practice standards office was on the third floor of the same building and he had never been in that either, and standing in a lift with a mirror in it at about two to eleven he worked out that both of those were true and that neither of them mattered this morning.

He did not knock. He went in and put the chairs against the wall where the other nineteen were, and the woman with the list said thank you and did not look up, and that was the entire transaction.

---

They started at about half past ten. It was a Saturday for about twenty women on a district's books, it was an hour and a half, and it was on manual handling and transfers, and about half of it was a woman of about forty-four talking and about half of it was pairs of women on a slung sheet doing transfers to a low bed.

One of the twenty was a woman of about thirty-one who had come in on crutches and was doing the transfers sitting down and was doing them better than about nine of the people who were standing up, and Marek Senn learned from her, in about four minutes, without a word being exchanged, that the reason the whole room had not broken a hip in a fortnight was that a woman of about forty-four had put a strip of paper on the floor by the low bed that said **one of you says it out loud, and it is not the same person every time, and swap over.** No logo on it. No date. Notepaper.

He looked at that for a long time.

The second half of the morning was the sliding sheet, and the third was questions, and the questions were good, and the woman of forty-four answered them the way you answer them when you have been asked the same ones nine hundred times and have decided that being tired of the question is not a reason to give a worse answer.

There was no card. Marek Senn noticed that in about eleven seconds and then spent the rest of the morning noticing that he had noticed it.

There was a laminated card on the table at the front.

He found out about it at about ten to twelve, in a gap, when a woman of about thirty-one came past with a roll of tape and put her hand flat on the table beside it and said to nobody in particular, "That took me four nights."

It was a piece of printed card about the size of a postcard, laminated at a kitchen table, with a strip of tape across one corner, and it had four steps on it and a line at the bottom, and the four steps were good. The line at the bottom was this:

*WAIT FOR THE WORD. IF NO WORD, WITHDRAW AND REPORT.*

Rafi Pell's step came out of a courier crew. Mal Farrow's hip belt came out of a kitchen. A woman of forty-six on the Meridian had spent eleven months making about nine hundred people good at a thing and had been asked to take a crew into a river on a Saturday at a low water and had written on a form that she did not know and had not said no, and a man in a lanyard had stood a hundred and ten yards away on a hard standing and worked the whole of it out in a second and a half and could not say a word.

The card was not a copy. That was the thing that took him about four seconds to establish and it took him the rest of the afternoon to be able to look at it. **It was not copied from anything. It was not off a company's booklet and it was not off a district's pack and it was not a photocopy. Somebody in this city sat at a kitchen table for four nights and wrote a thing down that was correct, out of her own head, from about nine years of doing the work, and got the four steps right, and got the sentence at the bottom right as well.**

The sentence at the bottom is right. That is the part he could not get past for the rest of the afternoon. She was right. If no word comes then you withdraw and you report, and that is correct, and it is correct because a woman who has moved a person who cannot move herself for nine years has learned that the worst thing on a shift is the pair who both go at once.

And she had written it on a card. And she had laminated it. And she had four nights of it on a kitchen table and about eleven other women in this city now, and by next spring there will be about four hundred, and it is free, and it is portable, and it fits in a pocket, and it costs eleven pence, and **it is eleven years old in the shape of it and she invented it this week out of nine years of work and it is the same slot with a different name in it, and the name on this one is a woman of thirty-one who made it at her own kitchen table and is not anybody's.**

He stood next to it with his hand on the table about four inches from it for about half a minute, doing arithmetic about a stranger's forearm.

The woman of thirty-one came back for the tape.

"Sorry. Am I in your way?"

"No," said Marek Senn. "I was trying to work out whether that needs a corner taken off it, and it does not, and I have no idea why I asked myself that."

"You are a repair man."

"Service engineer. Rigs."

"Right." She looked pleased for a second and then slightly worried. "Is that all right? That card. I did it off my own head. I know there is a proper one because I have been on a course and the proper one is a card you buy off a company and it is forty pound and I could not do that, and I have looked, and I could not find the free one, and I did not want to say the proper one was wrong because it is not wrong, it is a card."

"It is a good card."

"Is it."

"It is a good card and the four steps are right," said Marek Senn. "**And the line at the bottom is the best sentence on that table, and the reason it is the best sentence is that it is the only one on it that tells somebody what to do when the other person is not there, and about nine people in this city have written the other version.**"

The woman of thirty-one did not say anything for a second, and then she said, "Right," and went back to her tape, and she was pleased, and that was the whole of the transaction, and Marek Senn stood in a room on the third floor of a building in Bower Street and let a woman of thirty-one be pleased about a card she had made at a kitchen table, and it was the correct thing to do and it was the worst four minutes of his term.

---

Dessa Kwan was doing the transfers at the back and she had been doing them for about forty minutes by the time he got to the back of the room, and she had four terms and no card and had run about nine of these in four years for nothing, and nobody thanked her for any of them either, and she had never once mentioned it.

She saw him at about twenty past twelve and came over with a roll of tape in one hand.

"You have not asked me one thing," she said.

"You have not given me nine hundred words."

"No," said Dessa Kwan.

They stood about four feet apart in a corridor that was not a corridor, it was the side of a room, for about two seconds.

"Right," she said. "**The nine hundred words are finished. I did them in about four days, in week twenty, on a Tuesday and a Wednesday and a Thursday, and it is about nine hundred words and it is a thing I have been writing since the Saturday of week fifteen about a room in Nine Bridges and about a man at the front of it and about what happens to a person who goes in.**"

"You have not handed it over."

"I am never going to hand it over to you, and that has not changed and I want it on the page in my words because you will not have written it down otherwise." She put the tape in her apron. "**And I am going to tell you the thing I worked out about it, and I have not told anybody, and the reason I have not told anybody is that the person it is about is in a room nine feet from where I am standing and I have taught him a transfer with my own hands.**"

"Go on."

"Nine hundred words is a document. It is a good document and it is true and it is in the first person with my name at the end, and the second it is in a file it becomes a thing that was done, and the day it becomes a thing that was done, that is a man in a room who got somebody hurt, with a nurse's name on the front of it, and the nurse's name is mine, and I put it there, and the thing I was trying to do was to stop that room being a room and I would have handed a room a document with my name on it." She looked back at the room. "**So it is in a drawer in a kitchen in Saltmarket and it is not going anywhere and that is the correct place for it and it is the first document I have ever written that I have been afraid of in the correct direction.**"

"Say the last part again."

"**I have been afraid of it in the right direction.** I have spent a term and more watching you be careful with documents in the wrong direction, and you have been careful in a way that has let about nine things go that should not have gone, and I have watched you work that out nine times and get a little better at it." She said it without heat. "I got it right the first time because I am not you and I do not have a case. You have a case and that is why every document you touch comes out shaped like a case, and I have got nine hundred words and a kitchen and that is why mine comes out shaped like a woman in a room. **You would have made that card on the table tonight a document in about eleven minutes and it would have been right, and there would have been a reference number on it by Friday, and in four years somebody would have been able to find out that the woman who made it was taught by a company.**"

He did not say anything for a second.

"That is the best thing anybody has said to me in a term," he said, "and I am not going to say thank you for it, and I am also not going to say that is fair, and I am also not going to tell you that you have got the wrong end of it, and I want you to have noticed all three of those."

"I noticed all three." She put the tape down on a chair. "Now go and carry some chairs, because you have been standing in my room for twenty minutes and you have not once been asked to do anything, and that is a Saturday that nobody voted for, and the woman with the list does not know your name, and the card on that table is going to be in about eleven pockets by the spring, and I am not going to be able to stop it and neither are you, and you know that, and you have not said it, and I have now said it in a room with no tap running, which is a thing I do not do."

She went back to the transfers.

He carried the chairs down at about half past one and the van took them back to the Fold in one run because the chairs are lighter than people think and there were thirty of them, and Ena Truslove was on the pavement outside the nursery and counted them on her fingers and said, "That is the fourth one."

"That is the fourth one."

"You are the first person who has ever carried them back," said Ena Truslove. "The van drivers will not come past the end of the road, and the district woman is lovely and she carried eleven of them up herself last week and did not mention it, and the eleven are hers and not the nursery's and there is no box anywhere for the eleven, and that is three times I have said yes in about four seconds and I have stopped keeping the list."

"How many times have you been thanked."

"None," said Ena Truslove. "And I have worked out why and it is not unkindness, and I told a man in a shop that on the Saturday of last week and he wrote it down, and I am not going to say it again to a second person because I have got a card in a tin in a market with my name at the bottom of it and it is going to be read out in about a fortnight and it is the first thing anybody has ever done about this that was not nothing."

She looked up the road.

"You are not thanked either."

"No."

"Good," said Ena Truslove. "Then that is two of us."

He wrote the entry from about half past four on the Saturday afternoon in the shop with the shutter up, and he wrote it fast, and it is the second entry in three days that is about him and he has noticed that and has not decided whether it is a change or a symptom.

*89. Saturday of week 22, half past four. A room on the third floor of a building I have been inside twice in my life and both times I was the wrong man for it.*

*What I went there for, and what I actually did.* Thirty plastic chairs from the Fold nursery to a room on a third floor in Bower Street for a Saturday, for about twenty women on somebody's books, and nobody asked me to go and nobody thanked me for going, and I was in that building at about ten past ten in the morning on my own with a lanyard on and no work order, which is the first time in five weeks I have been in a room in this city where nothing I am is relevant. I carried chairs. I put them against a wall. A woman with a list said thank you and did not look up. **Gratitude has got nowhere to go, so it does not move, and I have now been the other half of that sentence twice, and the first time I wrote it down I did not understand it and the second time I understood it completely and it turned out to be a worse piece of engineering than the hinge.**

*The lift, the second floor, and the lanyard, and a rule that is not a rule.* I got in a lift at about two to eleven with a mirror in it and looked at a man of twenty-two in a work shirt going up to a floor of a building in Bower Street on a Saturday morning with nothing in his hands, and there was a woman with a wheeled bag who looked at the lanyard and then at me and then at the buttons and said nothing, and neither did I. **The gate this morning was opened by a fitter's handwriting in a depot in Lattice. The lift was opened by a button. Everything after the lift was opened by a woman with a list with a tick on it and no names, and there was nothing in the chain between me and that room except that I had decided to be in it.** The practice standards office is on this floor. The door was shut and there was a printed sign on it saying weekdays, and I stood in the corridor outside it for about four seconds and did not knock, and I am writing that down because I am going to want it in four years, and because in about two months I am going to be asked whether I have ever been in this building and I am going to have to say yes, and the sentence after the yes is going to be this one.

*And a strip of notepaper on the floor by the low bed, which took me about four minutes to understand and about an hour to get over.* A woman of forty-four had put it there: **one of you says it out loud, and it is not the same person every time, and swap over.** No logo, no date, notepaper, written in about nine seconds at the start of a morning. **A woman of thirty-one came in on crutches and did the transfers sitting down and did them better than about nine of the people who were standing up, and the reason nobody in that room had broken a hip in a fortnight was a strip of notepaper that a woman wrote for her own room, and there is no version of that which anybody could sell, and it will not be in a company document, and it is in exactly the same shape as the thing on the table.** Two of the same shape in one room, an hour apart, one made by a woman of forty-four for her own Saturday and one made by a woman of thirty-one at her own kitchen table, and the entire distance between them is that one of them has been read out loud in a room about thirty times and the other one has been read by nobody, and I have been in this case for fourteen weeks and both of them arrived in a single morning without anybody doing anything.

*The card on the table, and this is the entry.* **A woman of thirty-one made it herself, at her own kitchen table, over four nights, out of about nine years of doing the work, and laminated it, and it is not a copy of anything. It is not off a company's booklet and it is not off a district's pack and nobody sent it to her.** Four steps, and they are correct. And at the bottom: *wait for the word. If no word, withdraw and report.* And she is right, and the reason she is right is that the worst thing on a shift is the pair who both go at once, and she knows that from the floor and not from a document.

**And I have been standing in this case for a term looking for a shape with a person-shaped hole in it, and this is the same shape, and it is eleven years old, and it is portable and it is free, and it costs eleven pence, and the name in it is not a company and not a district and not a man with a lanyard. It is a woman of thirty-one who made it at a kitchen table and got it right, and by next spring it will be in about four hundred pockets, and nobody has to sell it to anybody, and nobody has to author it, and it is going to arrive at about the rate that things that are correct and cheap and portable arrive, which is the rate at which about eleven people in this city have been saying *nobody took a vote*.** She is proud of it. She is proud of it at about ten to twelve on a Saturday morning and I stood next to it with my hand on the table and I could not say one word, and the only sentence I had available was four true sentences about being a service engineer, and I did not use those either, because there was a woman standing there who was happy.

*And what I did instead of saying anything, and it is the whole of what I am carrying.* I said the four steps were right and I said the line at the bottom was the best sentence on that table, and I told her why, and she said right and went back to her tape. **That was a true sentence and it was the worst four minutes of my term, and the reason it was the worst is that I was kind, and being kind is the shape the slot has when it is made by a man, and I have spent a term learning to be silent in rooms and I have discovered this afternoon that silence in a room only helps when the room is full of people who can check me.** A woman with a kitchen table and four nights and nine years of work is not going to be checked by anybody in that room and she is going to make four hundred of these. **I could have said one sentence and it would have been about four months and somebody's forearm and it would have come out of a man's mouth as a company's, and so I did not say it, and the reason I did not say it is the same reason as the yard, and the yard was correct, and this is not the same, and I have not worked out yet why they are different and it is going to keep me up.**

*The nine hundred words, and they are finished.* She told me in a side of a room with about four people in it. Nine hundred words in week twenty, a Tuesday, a Wednesday and a Thursday, about a room in Nine Bridges and a man at the front of it and what happens to a person who goes in. **And she is never going to hand it over to me, and the reason is the best thing anybody has said to me in a term, and the reason is that the second a document exists, the room it is about becomes a thing that was done, and that is a man in a room who got somebody hurt with her name on the front of it, and she put it there, and the thing she was trying to do was stop that room being a room.** It is in a drawer in a kitchen in Saltmarket and it is not going anywhere. **And she said she has been afraid of a document in the correct direction for the first time in her life, and that I have been careful in the wrong direction for a term, and that every document I touch comes out shaped like a case, and that if I had made that card tonight it would have been right and it would have had a reference number on it by Friday and in four years somebody would have been able to find out that the woman who made it was taught by a company.** I did not say thank you. I did not say that was fair. I did not tell her she had the wrong end of it. She noticed all three and she went back to the transfers.

*And what I did not do on the third floor, and it is a thing I did on the Tuesday by telephone and it is now in the air.* **I told a woman in Bower Street on Tuesday morning that I was not coming on the Saturday, and I said it in one word in a second and a half with no reason, and she knew I was coming, and she asked anyway and said so out loud, and she took the answer and priced it for me and put the phone down.** I was on the third floor from ten past ten until half past one and she was four miles away and neither of us said one word about it, and I did not ring her at half past one, and I did not ring her at four o'clock, and she has not rung me, and the last time she rang me was Tuesday. **I have now got a false sentence standing in this city between a woman who told me the price of the true one in advance and me, and it is the first false sentence I have put into this case and I have put it in about a person and not about a company, and I do not know what it is going to cost, and I knew that on the Tuesday morning when I said the word no, and I said it anyway, and I am going to find out on a Tuesday.**
