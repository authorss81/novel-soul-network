# Chapter 81 — The Box Number

He asked for it at ten past seven on the Monday morning of week twenty-two, standing at the end of the bench in the Lattice depot with the roller shutter up and the extractor going and nine hand sets laid out in front of him in a row he had not made, and he had it in four sentences because he had written them on the back of a docket on the Sunday night and he was not going to improve them in a corridor.

Petar Vance put his mug down on the parts cage.

"Go on."

"A service callout. Meridian consolidation point, lot onward, off the corridor. There is about four days of rigging outstanding there and you are short-handed. I have a van and a meter bag and I can look at a rig." He stopped, because the four sentences were the easy part and he had a fifth one, and the fifth one was the reason he had spent a Sunday on it. "And I am asking for it as a thing I want. Not as something you owe me for the Mondays."

Vance did not say anything for about three seconds.

"Yes."

"Right."

"Yes, that is the answer, and I want you to hear how fast it was, because I have been waiting three weeks to find out whether I would say it." Vance did not drink from the mug. "I would have said yes on the first morning. **What I have been waiting three weeks for is whether you would ask, and what you would say when you did, and you have put the word *Mondays* inside the ask without being asked for it, which means you already know what it is, and I do not have to have the conversation about the favour at all.**"

Marek Senn stood at the end of a bench in a depot and found that he had no idea what to do with a fast yes.

"The box number," he said.

"That is the second thing." Vance took a sheet off the clipboard, turned it round, and put it face down on the cage, which he had not done in three weeks. "**I am not writing the lot onward on a company sheet and I am not writing the box number and I am not ringing the customer to ask them for a site contact, and all three of those are the same refusal and the reason is not caution and it is not anything to do with you.**"

"Why."

"Because I have been a fitter for nineteen years and I have driven to about four hundred sites and I could not tell you one of them now." He put his hand flat on the face-down sheet. "A booking is a gate. That is all a site is. You get a booking with an address on it and the gate does not care who is on the invoice, and I have watched that work since I was twenty-two and it is the only clean thing in this business. **And the second a box number goes onto a company sheet it stops being a gate and starts being a record, and then it has got a number on it that is ours, and a number that is ours is a reference, and a reference is a thing that gets kept.** You will not be able to give that number to anybody. I will not be able to give it to anybody. And in about four years somebody will ring this depot and ask for it and one of us will look it up."

"Nobody is asking me for it."

"That is not the part that matters and you know it is not the part that matters." Vance turned the sheet back over. "The part that matters is that the number is not mine to write down. It came off a van. It came off a van in a stores clerk's hand on an intake summary, from a lot somebody sold us, and the moment I put it on anything with our name at the top, a person in four years finds out that a company in this city was interested in a box on a corridor that is not a customer's site and does not have a company name on the door, and they will not be interested in the box. They will be interested in us. **I have spent nineteen years being a man who can be interested in us without lying and I am not starting on a Monday morning.**"

The extractor dropped to a lower pitch and neither of them looked at it.

"Write *Meridian corridor, customer site, service*," said Petar Vance. "That is a true sentence. It is on about nine hundred of the sheets in this building and there is nothing on it anybody can find you in. And when you get there you will find out the thing I could have told you on the first morning, which is that the gate is the easy part."

He put the sheet on the bench and wrote on it in his own hand, and then he stood there while Petar Vance came and looked at it and did not change a word, which took about four seconds and was the whole of the difference between this and every other thing that has happened to Marek Senn in five weeks.

Then Vance said, "Out of hours."

"From this morning."

"Then before you go anywhere." He pulled the rota out of the drawer and put it on the cage and turned it round so that the whole of it was visible, which it had not been in three weeks. "**Five names,** and I am not going to say the fifth one out loud to you on a Monday morning in a corridor. Me, Molloy, Ferris, and you, and one more. Ferris is off sick and has told nobody how long it is going to be. Molloy is back and is on the Tuesday and the Wednesday. **And the fifth man has not been answering it since Wednesday afternoon and has not spoken to a single person about it, and I have left him on it, and I am going to keep leaving him on it, and I want you to hear me say that I am going to keep leaving him on it.**"

"That is not going to help him."

"No," said Petar Vance. "It is going to help you, because in about three weeks he is going to notice that the rota has been run without him for a fortnight and that nobody rang him about it, and a man notices that. **And when he notices it he is going to be a man who walked away from a service line, and he is not one, and the only thing that puts that back is somebody noticing that he was still on it, and noticing it is not my job and it is not his job and it is yours, because it is on your card this morning.**"

He tapped the rota once with a finger and did not say anything else about it for three weeks.

"I am not writing the box number down and I am also not writing you out of the middle of that rota," said Vance. "Those are two separate things and I would like them both in your head at once, because a man who gets one of them and not the other is a man who is going to be a problem in about eleven weeks."

---

The out-of-hours number was his from that morning and it rang at about twenty past eight while he was sitting in a van on a forecourt in Nine Bridges with a hand set in his lap and a customer watching him not answer a telephone.

He let it ring three times and then he said his own name into it the way he had been taught to in his second week, and a woman said, "Oh. Are you the out-of-hours?"

"Since this morning."

"Right." He could hear her deciding. "I have got a roller shutter in a lock-up in the Saltmarket and it has stopped about a foot off the deck with a day's stock in it, and the plate on the motor says there is a manual override and I cannot find it, and I have been standing in there since seven."

He got her to read him the plate off the motor. It took nine minutes and it was not difficult and the whole of it was printed on a sticker about four inches by two. The override was a plastic cap over a manual release on the left-hand end of the barrel. You kept your hand on the cap and turned the key a quarter turn anticlockwise with the key in the down position, and then you put the key back and took your hand off and let the shutter come down onto the rubber lip under its own weight.

"You are going to lose it," he said. "Let it come. If you hold it, the lip takes the weight and the lip is the part that costs."

"It came down."

"Good."

Then a pause, and a different kind of voice.

"Who do I ask for next time. I have had a card with a number on it for about two years and I have never once rung it, and I have just rung it on a Monday morning and got a person."

"Ask for me. Marek Senn. And if I am not on it, ask for whoever is. And if whoever is sounds like they do not know, ring it again, because the rota is four men and it is not the same one every week and that is nobody's fault."

She said thank you and put the phone down, and he sat on a forecourt in Nine Bridges with a hand set in his lap and did about two hundred words of a job in his head and then stopped.

**That number is on the bottom left of about two thousand cards in this district and stuck inside about four hundred fridges, and it has been in a card holder on Marek Senn's own neck for three weeks, and until twenty past eight this morning it had four men on it and three of the four were not answering and one of them had decided that a company which put a piece of paper on a wall is not a company he wants to be reachable from, and he was not being unreasonable.** He had spent a term working out that a slot shaped like a person is portable and cheap and free, and the whole of that had been sitting eleven feet from his elbow on a bench, and it had never once occurred to him that he was the fourth man standing in it.

The phone rang again at about twenty to eleven and it was a man on a rig about four miles the other way with a fault that had gone and come again since Friday, and the fault belonged to the man who was not answering.

That was the part he did not enjoy, and he noticed that he noticed it, which is not the same thing.

"Right," he said, listening to the description. "That is a latch solenoid on the return, and if it has done that three times in a week then it has a mechanical fault and it is not electrical, and the electrical part will pass its test every time you do it and you will go round in circles for a month." He wrote a part number on the back of his hand. "Where is the isolator."

The man told him. There was a long pause on the line.

"Can I ask you something. You're the new one, aren't you. On the phones."

"That is not a thing I can help with."

"No. It's just — the last four times I've rung that number it rang out, and the time before that I got a man who said it wasn't his shop, and the one before him did not turn up at all, and now there is a man telling me about a latch solenoid." Another pause. "**You've been about three weeks, haven't you.**"

"Three weeks on Monday."

"Right," said the man. "Well, that is the first time in a fortnight I have rung that number and got anybody, and I am not going to pretend that is not why I have rung it five times in twenty minutes." A machine came on behind him. "So what do I do about it today, then."

"Write it on the sheet," said Marek Senn. "Day and date, what it did, how many times, and the fact that you rang four times and nobody answered. Send it to the depot in the morning and it goes on a review, and if it is a mechanical fault the review will find it in a fortnight, because it is on paper and because there have been three of them in a week."

"That is it?"

"That is it," he said. "**It is a stupid answer and it is the only answer there is and it works about eighty per cent of the time, and the twenty per cent is the twenty per cent I have just been for you, and you could not have got that from anybody, so write it down anyway and put both halves on the sheet.**"

He put the phone down and sat in the van with the engine running.

That was the fourth thing that had arrived in that building in five weeks without anybody planning it, and he had been one of the four, and the rota had a name on it for a man who had not been answering it and the rota was still going out with his name on it in about three weeks' time, which meant that in about three weeks a man was going to be phoned at home by a company because of a piece of paper that a fitter had decided was the correct thing to do.

He wrote nothing down. That was the correct decision and he made it in about a second. Her name, the lock-up, the shutter, the nine minutes — it goes in a book in a market, and a book in a market gets read out in four minutes in front of about nine people, and a shutter in the Saltmarket is not a site and a shutter is not a technique, and if it is ever a technique then somebody in four years is going to want to know how many of these there were.

---

At ten past six that Monday evening he was at the bench in Lattice Ward with the lamp over it on and the extractor off and the load book shut in front of him, and he looked at the clock on the wall above the parts cage, and that was the whole of it.

The Weir is off Fell Road. It is a scaffold bar and eleven steps at the Cutbank and the whole thing takes forty minutes, and it starts at ten past six, and for three weeks it had started at ten past six with him on it.

There was no version of a man who said *I have asked to come off the Mondays from the Monday of week twenty-two* to a woman with a bar on a Friday morning, and then turned up at ten past six on that Monday with a meter bag. He had known that on the Friday. He had known it when he wrote the four sentences on the back of a docket on the Sunday.

At about a quarter to nine the shop telephone rang and it was Nkechi Osei.

"You did not ring me," she said.

"No."

"It is a quarter to nine on the Monday and you have had about eleven hours and you did not ring me, and I have been sitting here since half past eight deciding whether that was a decision or a habit, and it was a decision, and I have been waiting since about eleven o'clock this morning for you to do it and you have not, and I am not going to be a woman who is pleased about it."

"How did it go."

"That is the question, is it not." He heard a cup go down on something metal. "He is a man of about twenty-six and he has been on about nine of those banks in his life and he has never done the wall leg, and the wall leg is a gap, and you have left a gap, and the gap got filled in about a second and a half, which is what gaps do. **He did fine. That is the whole of what I am going to tell you about it and I am telling you because you did not ask me for it.**"

"I did ask you for it. I just did not ask you on the telephone."

"That is a lawyer's sentence and you have never once been a lawyer." She let a second go. "**And here is the part you are going to want to argue with, so you can have it now. I did not tell him what he was standing in.** He asked me on the bank, at about ten past, in about nine words, whether Mondays were a thing you did or a thing that had happened to him, and I said it was a thing that had happened to him, and he took it, and he has not asked me a second question since, and he is not going to, because he is twenty-six and I have watched a hundred and fifty of them and the ones who ask twice are the ones who cannot use the answer."

Marek Senn put his hand flat on the bench.

"What happens in a month."

"In about a month he is going to be good at the wall leg and he is going to notice that the hardest thing on that bank is not the wall leg. **It is that somebody says a word and about nineteen men wait for it.** And he is going to want to know what the word is for, and he is going to ask a woman with a bar, and I am going to have to either lie to him or explain an arrangement that has been standing here for eleven years and has never once been written down. And in about four years one of those two is going to happen to a man with a family, in a room, in front of people, and somebody in an office is going to write *unreasonable delay* on a form about it and put his name on it."

"And nobody will be able to say the arrangement was not written down, because it was not."

"**Nobody will be able to say anything at all, because there is nothing to point at, and that is what I have been telling you for three weeks and you have written it down twice and I would like it written down a third time in words I would recognise.**"

He looked at the clock again. It was a minute to nine.

"What is his name," he said, and stopped in the middle of it, and let the shop be quiet for about four seconds.

"No," said Nkechi Osei.

"No."

"You were not going to ask me for it and I have known that since about half past eight, and I want you to hear that I knew, because I have watched you do this for a term and it is the only thing about you that I have never once been able to get ahead of." A bar behind her, and a door. "**His name is on the sheet. That is where it is. It is not in this city in a form and it is not in a book of yours and it is not in a bundle anybody has published, and if somebody publishes it one day then he is a line on a piece of paper and a woman with a bar is a woman who arranged a thing, and I have not spent eleven years being the woman who arranged a thing.**"

"Right."

"And Marek." She had not said his name for eleven weeks and she said it now for a reason. "**Do not feel good about this. You did the right thing on Friday morning in about forty seconds and the price of it is a month of one man's working life and he does not know your name, and he is going to be good at it, and he is going to be good at it because I have taught him four things in three weeks and I have never taught anybody four things in three weeks in my life.** I am not being kind and I am not being cruel. I am telling you what it cost so that you cannot put it in a book and think it was free."

"I am not going to put it in the book."

"You are going to put it in the book," said Nkechi Osei, "and you are not going to tell me you have, and I am going to let you, and I am telling you now that I know you are, so that neither of us is pretending about it later."

The line went.

He wrote the entry from about half past nine and he wrote it in one go, and it is short, and the lamp stayed on again.

*84. Monday of week 22, half past nine, lamp on. This page is about me and about a rota and about a shutter, and there is not one thing in it about a person I have met, and I have not written one of those since the middle of the summer.*

*What I asked for, and what it cost me to get.* Four sentences in a corridor at ten past seven and yes in about three seconds, from a man who told me on the Thursday of week twenty-one that he had been waiting three weeks to find out whether I would ask. **I have spent a term in the belief that a door in this city is the only thing worth having, and I asked for one this morning and it took three seconds, and the reason it took three seconds is that nobody in that building has ever had to refuse me a thing, and I do not think one of them has ever thought about that.** The lanyard does not open the gate. A booking opens the gate, and a gate does not care who is on the invoice, and that sentence has been in my head since the Tuesday of week twenty-one and it is worth more to me than the lanyard ever was.

*What I did not get, and the reason, and the reason is a mechanism and not caution.* **Petar Vance will not write the box number down, and he will not write the lot onward on a company sheet either, and he will not ring the customer for a site contact, and those are one refusal and he gave me the reason without being pushed.** A box number on a company sheet stops being a gate and starts being a record, and a record gets a number that is ours, and a number that is ours is a reference, and a reference is a thing that gets kept. He is right. Nineteen years a fitter, about four hundred sites, and he could not name one of them now, and that is not a failing, that is a man who has worked out exactly what a company is for. **The sentence he wrote for me is *Meridian corridor, customer site, service*. It is true, it is on about nine hundred sheets in that building, and it contains nothing anybody can find me in, and I have copied it onto my own card in my own hand, and I am now the person who wanted that sentence. He also turned the out-of-hours rota round and showed me all of it, which he has not done in three weeks, and there are five names on it and one of them has not been answering since Wednesday afternoon and he would not say the name, and he said out loud that he is going to keep leaving that man on it, and that the leaving is the correct decision and that the only thing which is going to put it back is somebody noticing the man was still on it, and that noticing it is not his job and not the man's job and is mine, because it is on my card this morning. **So I have got a rule now that I did not have on Friday, and it is not a rule about me. It is a rule about a rota, and the reason it exists is that four men agreed to be on a list and one of them stopped answering and nobody has standing to take him off it, and that is the same sentence as a review and it came out of a fitter's mouth at about ten to eight on a Monday morning, and neither of us noticed that he had said it.****

*What I was on, at about twenty past eight, and what it actually was.* One call. A roller shutter in a lock-up in the Saltmarket, a foot off the deck, a day's stock, a plastic cap on the left-hand end of a barrel, and nine minutes on a telephone with a woman whose name I have not written down and am not going to. **I have spent a term on the fact that a slot shaped like a person is portable and cheap and free, and the whole of it has been eleven feet from my elbow on a bench for three weeks, and it had four men on it, and three of them were not answering, and one of them was right not to be.** A woman asked me who to ask for next time and I said ask for me. That is the first time anybody has ever asked me that and I have been three weeks on a number that about two thousand households in this district have written in a book. **I have been the fourth thing in a room four times since the Thursday of week sixteen and I have never once been the fourth thing in a room that rang.**

*And the thing I did not do, and the reason, and the reason is not noble.* I did not go to Fell Road. There is no version of a man who says that sentence to a woman with a bar on a Friday morning and then turns up at ten past six on that Monday with a meter bag, and I knew that on the Friday and I wrote four sentences on a docket on the Sunday knowing it. So the round happened without me and a man of about twenty-six stood on the wall leg, and the wall leg is a gap, and the gap got filled in about a second and a half, which is what a gap does.

*And the price, which she gave me at a quarter to nine tonight because I did not ring her, and which is the correct arrangement and I am not going to pretend otherwise.* **She did not tell him what he was standing in. He asked her in nine words on the bank whether Mondays were a thing you did or a thing that had happened to him, and she said it was a thing that had happened to him, and he took it and has not asked a second question and is not going to.** In about a month he will be good at the wall leg and he will work out that the hardest thing on that bank is not the wall leg, it is that somebody says a word and nineteen men wait for it. And then he will ask her what the word is for, and she will have to lie to him or explain eleven years of something nobody wrote down, and in about four years one of those happens to a man with a family in a room in front of people and a form goes in with *unreasonable delay* on it and his name on it. **And I cannot say a word about any of it to anybody, because the whole of it is a gap I made, and the mechanism is me, and the woman on the bank is not going to be a woman who arranged a thing.**

*And the other half of the price, which she would not soften.* She has taught him four things in three weeks and has never taught anybody four things in three weeks in her life. So the sentence I said in about forty seconds on a Friday morning has cost a man I have never met about a month of his working life, and he does not know my name, and he is not on my rota, and there is no column anywhere for it.

*And she told me not to put it in this book.* I am going to put it in this book. I have decided that now, at half past ten on a Monday, and I have decided that I am not going to tell her I have, and she has told me she knows I am, which means that from tonight there are two people in this city who know that this page exists and neither of us will ever mention it, and I do not know what to do with that and I have decided it is allowed.
