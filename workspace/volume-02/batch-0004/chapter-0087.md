# Chapter 87 — Up the Stairs

He went up Fell Road at about twenty past four on the Sunday afternoon of week twenty-two with a bag of shopping he had not bought, because he had walked to the corner and come back and then walked up again and that is the only reason he had anything in his hand.

The laundrette was under his own flat and shut. The woman behind the counter had asked him four times to come round on a bus and not to look at it, and on the Saturday of week twenty-one it had been the third time, and he had not done it then either.

The stairs went up from the pavement through her door.

He had not been up them since the Sunday of week twelve. That was ten weeks, exactly, and he knew the figure without doing the sum, which is what a number means when a person has been carrying it instead of writing it down.

He knocked on her door and she opened it in about nine seconds, which meant she had heard the shutter and his feet and worked out which of the two doors it was.

"You've not brought anything."

"No," said Marek Senn.

"Good." She did not step back to let him in and she did not not let him in, and after about a second and a half she turned round and walked into her front room and left the door open, which is one of the three things that means come in and he was not going to be told which one it was.

She was sixty-eight. She had a plate on her right wrist that had been on every day for three years, and she had a kettle on a stand, and there was a chair with a cushion on it that was not for visitors.

"Sit down," said Enid Sarkis. "Not there, that's the bad chair. That's the good one. I've had a plate in this arm for three years and I know which chair in a room is going to be a problem for you before you've been in it four seconds, and it is that one, because you will put your hand on the arm of it."

He moved the cushion.

"Right," she said. "Now. You've not come up those stairs for ten weeks because of me, and I'd rather you said that than not, because the other version is that you came up because of the bag and there was no bag."

"You were right the first time."

"Aye. And I want the whole of it, because I'm sixty-eight and I've had three years of people being nice at me in about nine-second parcels and I've run out." She filled the kettle. "**Say it and then I'll tell you what you actually came up here to ask, and it's not the thing you came up here to ask.**"

"I came up because I did not go up."

"That's not a reason, that's a fact about your stairs."

"I came up because you asked me, on the Friday of week thirteen, whether there was anybody whose job it was, and I said no, and I have not been up these stairs since the Sunday of week twelve, which is five days before you asked me, and I have been not going up for the whole of those ten weeks." He put his hands on his knees. "And I have been not going up because every time I have come in on a weekend I have got as far as your door and gone back out to the shop, and I have done that three times in nine weeks, and each time I decided it was because I had not got anything, and I have not got anything."

Enid Sarkis turned the tap off.

"Well, that's the first honest thing anybody's said to me about it and it's about you and not about me, so I'll allow it." She sat down on the bad chair. "**And now I'll tell you what you came up here to ask, and it isn't what you think, because I have had three years of people coming up here with a question already made up and I can tell from the doorway.** You did not come up to ask me about the plate. You came up because a document you are keeping has a date on it and you have been carrying the date, and the date is the end of the term, and you have decided that you are not going to be a man who lets a date go past without doing a thing about it, and there is no thing to do, and you have come up anyway to find out whether there is."

"Yes," said Marek Senn.

"Right," said Enid Sarkis. "**Well, there isn't, and I'll save you the fortnight, and then I'll tell you the thing that is actually the reason I am glad you came up, and it is not about the plate.**"

She did not get up for the kettle for about a minute and a half, and she used the minute and a half to look at him properly for the first time since the door, and what she said at the end of it was not what he expected.

"**You have not asked me a single question about the plate.**"

"No."

"Not one. In ten weeks." She put her hands on the arms of the bad chair. "Do you know what the last person asked me? A man from a district office, in a corridor, and he had a form, and he asked me whether the pain was worse in the morning or at night, and I told him, and he wrote it down, and that was nine weeks ago."

"That is the right question."

"It is a completely reasonable question and it is the only question anybody has asked me about my arm in three years, and I have had about nine phone calls and two of those were about a bus." She got up then and filled the kettle. "**And I want to say the other thing before the tea, because I have been not saying it for eight weeks and you are here and I have got about an hour.**"

"Go on."

"There are two women who use the laundrette under your flat. **I have written neither of them down and I have told you both and I did not tell you because I wanted to, and I want you to notice what I have just done, because it is the same thing you do.** The one on the Tuesday has got a shoulder that has not been right since before the term and she carries her shopping in a bag on the good side and she has a whole routine about it and she has never once said a word about it to anybody, and I have watched her do it about forty times. And the one on the Friday does not come in any more and she used to come in on the Friday, and I asked a woman on the Friday about a quarter past two in the spring why she had not been in and the woman said she had gone to a clinic in Crown Terrace and had been put on a list, and I do not know what the list is for, and I have not asked, and that is not restraint, that is me not wanting to be a person who asks, because I am sixty-eight and I have two bad knees and I do not have the energy for other people's answers."

The kettle switched off.

"**I have been carrying two women's shoulders in my head since the middle of the summer and I have told exactly one person about them and he is a man of twenty-two who cannot get up a flight of stairs.** That is the whole of the system. There is no system. There is a woman under your flat and a man in a shop and about two hundred thousand other people who are each carrying two and telling nobody, and the only reason you know about yours is that I asked you for it in week thirteen, and the only reason I asked you is that I had been carrying mine for about a year."

The kettle boiled and she did not get up for it for about a minute and a half.

"There's a paper on the table," she said. "Under the bit of newspaper. I wrote it in the autumn of the year the plate went on, and I've added to it since. Come and look at it and don't touch it."

He came and looked at it. It was a sheet of A4 with a lined margin and it had been folded and unfolded so many times that it had gone soft at the creases. It was in her handwriting, a small round slanted hand, and it was a list.

It said:

*1. Cannot carry a full kettle with the right hand. Left hand only. Started about the same time as the plate.*
*2. Cannot get the top off a jar. Screwdriver. Not a can opener, a screwdriver, and I want that on the list because it is the kind of thing people think is a joke.*
*3. Cannot put my coat on the hook by the door. The hook is too high and it was too high before the plate.*
*4. Cannot hold a plate and a fork at the same time so I do not eat anything I have to cut.*

Four lines, and a date at the top that said the autumn, and a date at the bottom that said the most recent time she had added to it.

And under the fourth line, in a different ink, a fifth line.

*5. I went down on the stairs ten days ago and I did not tell anybody because there is nobody to tell, and I have been sitting in this chair since working out whether it matters, and I am writing it down here because this piece of paper is the only place it exists in the world, and I am sixty-eight and I have a plate and I fell over, and if it does not matter then I have wasted a Sunday, and if it does matter then I am the only person in this city who knows it.*

Marek Senn read it twice and then he read the fifth line a third time and then he did not read it again.

"When you say you have been sitting in this chair for ten days," he said. "Have you been sitting in it, or have you been not going out."

"That is a horrible question and I would like you to know that I have been asked it before by somebody in a district office and she asked it in a nicer way than you did." She did not answer it. "**You'll tell them and they'll ring me.** That's what's going to happen, isn't it, and I want to know before it happens, and I want it from you and not from a form."

"No," said Marek Senn.

"Good," said Enid Sarkis. "**Because I have been in this chair ten days and I have not been in pain, and I am sixty-eight, and if it was my ribs I would know, and it is not my ribs, and what it is, is that I have been three years with a plate and I have not fallen over in three years, and I fell over because somebody has been coming up and down those stairs at about six in the morning for five weeks and the fourth step from the bottom is loud.**"

He sat down on the good chair.

"That is the reason," said Enid Sarkis. "And I am not going to be examined by anybody about a step, and I am not going to be examined about the plate either, because the plate has been looked at by two people whose job that is and both of them have said a thing that is in my file, and what I want, and I have wanted it since the Friday of week thirteen, is for one person in this city whose job it is to look at a wrist like mine to look at it once. **And I have not got anybody, and I have not asked you for anybody, and I want that said out loud between us tonight, because I have had a lot of people in this room who wanted me to ask them for things.**"

"You have never asked me for anything."

"I have asked you for one thing and I asked you for it badly, on a telephone, seven days late, and you told me the answer first and then you gave me the reason and then I said I would take the reason and not the apology, and I did not ask you for the apology, and that was me asking you for the only thing I have wanted for three years, which is somebody to be told." She sat forward. "**And I am not asking you again tonight, and I want you to notice that, because I can see you had something ready and I have watched you have something ready since you came in.**"

He had had it ready. It was a piece of paper with a name on it and there was no name on it.

"Right," he said. "Then here's the true thing and it is the whole of what I have got. There is no person in this city whose job it is to look at a stranger's wrist. I have spent a term proving that, on my own, out loud, to about nine people, and I have never been able to fill the gap, and I am not going to invent a post this afternoon to fill it, and if I did invent one it would be a thing I made in a shop and I would be the fourth thing in it by the Wednesday."

"I know," said Enid Sarkis. "You told me on the Friday of week thirteen and I have written the date down and I am not going to be one of the nine."

---

He asked her at about twenty past five whether he might copy it out, and he said it badly, and he said why, and she said yes.

He copied it out on the back of a docket in his own small upright hand with no date at the top and no date at the bottom, and there was about two inches of room left on the docket and he left that empty too.

"That's the fifth one," said Enid Sarkis, watching.

"Yes."

"And you're going to put it in the drawer with the page about the ninety households and the one with my name on it."

"Yes."

"**Then leave the date off it.**"

He put the pen down.

"Say that again," said Marek Senn.

"Leave the date off it." She was not being dramatic and she was not slow. "**If there is a date on it then in about four years somebody is going to use that date to say it has been going on for three years and not looked at, and a date turns a woman's patience into a company's exposure. You write the date and it stops being what I have got and it starts being what you have let happen.** I have had that plate three years and I have not once been angry about it, and I am not going to be angry about it in a room in four years on the strength of a man's handwriting."

He looked at the docket for about four seconds.

"That is the best thing anybody has said to me since the spring," he said, "and I am not going to tell you what I am going to do with it, and I want you to notice that I have said the true sentence about somebody else's document and I have said nothing about a drawer in a shop, and I have been doing that in this city for a term and I have never once noticed that the people who are right about it are all doing it in kitchens and on kitchen tables and in draughts."

"Whose kitchen table."

"Yours," said Marek Senn, "and a woman's in Nine Bridges, and a co-operative's in the Saltmarket, and a records clerk's in an office that goes to nine hundred addresses, and a twenty-three-year-old's on the top of a wall in a market. **Five. And nobody has ever met. And I have met every one of them, and I have not put them in a room, and I know exactly why I have not and the reason is that I have been treating it as a pattern and a pattern gets a reference number.**"

"Well," said Enid Sarkis. "Then don't."

She got up then, and put the kettle on properly, and made two teas, and asked him whether he was eating, and he said he was not, and she told him that was not an answer and put a plate in front of him anyway, and he ate it, and it was about the amount of food that a plate holds.

---

He got out at about half past five and stood on the pavement on Fell Road for about a minute with the docket in his pocket and did not go to the shop. Delia Frost lived two streets away with a hip unit fourteen weeks old and about four in the afternoon was the time, and he had not got a reason to go to a door, and the reason he had for a docket in his pocket and a woman of thirty-one's card in his head was not a reason you could take to a doorstep.

He went to the shop and washed his hands, which he does before he writes anything in the book.

*90. Sunday of week 22, half past six at night. I went up. I have not written a page in this book in nine weeks that has nothing in it about a case and I am going to try, and it is going to fail, and I am writing it anyway.*

*What happened upstairs, in the order it happened, and nothing else.* I knocked at about twenty past four with a bag of shopping I had not bought. She opened the door in about nine seconds. She told me I had not brought anything, and she was right, and she told me not to sit in the bad chair and she was right about that as well. She has a plate on her right wrist that has been on every day for three years. **She told me the reason I had not come up those stairs for ten weeks in the first ninety seconds, and she was right, and it was not the reason I would have given.** There is no person in this city whose job it is to look at a stranger's wrist. She asked me that question on the Friday of week thirteen and I said no and I have not been up those stairs since the Sunday of week twelve, which is three days before she asked me, and I have been not going up for the whole of those ten weeks, and I have walked past your door three times in them and gone back out to the shop, and each time I told myself it was because I had not got anything.

*And what was on the table.* A sheet of A4, folded and unfolded until it had gone soft. Four lines, in her hand, in ink, with a season at the top: cannot carry a full kettle with the right hand; cannot get the top off a jar, and she wrote *screwdriver, not a can opener, and I want that on the list because it is the kind of thing people think is a joke*; cannot put a coat on a hook that was too high before the plate; cannot hold a plate and a fork at the same time so she does not eat anything she has to cut. **Four lines, written by a woman of sixty-eight, about the four things in her life that a plate took off her, and it is the most complete record of the subject in this entire case and there is no form for it and no box for it and it has never been in a building anybody else works in.**

And a fifth line, in a different ink, added ten days ago, which says that she went down on the stairs, and did not tell anybody because there is nobody to tell, and that this piece of paper is the only place it exists in the world, and that if it does not matter she has wasted a Sunday and if it does matter she is the only person in this city who knows it.

*And what she told me before the tea, and the two sentences in it that I did not have.* **There is no system in this city.** That is the first. There is a woman under my own flat with a plate and a list, and a man in a shop with a drawer, and about two hundred thousand other people each carrying two things about somebody else and telling nobody, and the only route between any two of them is that one of them gets lucky and the other one is not proud. **And the second is that she has been carrying hers for about a year and she asked me in week thirteen because she had nobody else, and not because she trusted me, and she said so in those words and I did not understand what she had said until about eight o'clock tonight.** The two women on her counter are named to me and written down nowhere and I am not going to write them in this book either, and that is the first decision this page has had in it that was not about me.

**And the third sentence, which is the one I am going to be carrying for a fortnight and which she said without any weight at all, which was the worst way she could have said it: that a date turns a woman's patience into a company's exposure, and that is the whole of what is wrong with every form in this case and with every form in hers, and she worked it out on her own at a kitchen table with a pen.** I have been arguing about it in a book for a term and I have never once had it in a sentence I could hand to somebody. I asked her, and she gave it to me, and I am not going to be able to use it, because using it means putting a date on a piece of paper, and I have a piece of paper in a drawer with no date on it, and I am going to leave it that way.

*And the reason, and I am not going to put the funny part in here because she did not.* **She has not fallen in three years, and she went down on the fourth step from the bottom, and the fourth step from the bottom is loud, and somebody has been coming up and down those stairs at about six in the morning for four weeks, and that is me.** She is not going to be examined about a step. She has been looked at by two people whose job that is and both of them have said a thing that is in her file.

*And what she wanted, and has wanted for three years, and did not ask me for.* **Somebody whose job it is. One person, once, for a wrist like hers. She asked me for it on the Friday of week thirteen, seven days late, and I told her the answer before she had finished and then gave her the reason, and she said she would take the reason and not the apology, and she did not ask me for the apology, and that was her asking me for the only thing she has wanted for three years, which is somebody to be told.** And tonight she told me she was not going to ask me again, and she could see I had something ready, and she was right, and what I had ready was a piece of paper with a name on it and there was no name on it.

*And the thing that happened at about twenty past five, which I am going to be thinking about for a fortnight.* I asked to copy it out and I said why, badly, and she said yes. I copied five lines onto the back of a docket in my own hand. **And she told me to leave the date off it, and the reason is: if there is a date on it, then in about four years somebody will use that date to say it has been three years and not looked at, and a date turns a woman's patience into a company's exposure.** I put the pen down and I have not picked it up since. There is no date at the top and there is no date at the bottom and there is about two inches of the docket left empty on purpose.

*And the count, and it is the reason I am writing this page in a room instead of going through it.* **Five people in this city have independently invented the same protection, in kitchens, in draughts, in offices and on a market wall, and I have met every one of them: a woman of sixty-eight with a list and no date on it; a woman of twenty-seven with nine hundred words in a drawer because the second a document exists the room becomes a thing that was done; a co-operative whose quarterly carries a ruled empty box and a sentence about not having a record; a records clerk under that box who does not know the three suppliers exist; and a man of twenty-three who put a sheet through a postbox on Weaver Street on the Friday of week twenty-two at four minutes to five so that the question would be on paper before the answer could be.** Nobody has met. **And I have met all five and I have not put them in a room, and the reason I have not is that I have been calling it a pattern, and a pattern gets a reference number, and a reference number tells the people who wrote in it that they wrote in it.** I have spent a term being careful in the wrong direction and a woman of sixty-eight has just told me what the right direction looks like, and it does not look like a reference number. It looks like a docket with two inches left empty.

*And what I did not do, at half past five, on the pavement, and it is going in here because it is the other half of today.* Delia Frost is two streets away with a hip unit fourteen weeks old and about four in the afternoon is the time, and I have got a docket in my pocket and a card in my head and neither of them is a reason to go to a door. I did not go. **I have now got a piece of paper in a drawer in this shop with a woman's handwriting on it and no date on it, and I have told one person in the world that I copied it, and she is sixty-eight and she told me not to tell anybody, and I have kept it, and the keeping is the whole of the case and I am not going to be able to put that in a better sentence than that at half past seven tonight.**
