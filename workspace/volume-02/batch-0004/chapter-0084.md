# Chapter 84 — A Service Callout

The sheet said *Meridian corridor, customer site, service* in Petar Vance's handwriting, and the address at the bottom was a gate number and a road, and that was all it said, and that was the whole of the plan.

He took the van at seven in the morning on the Thursday of week twenty-two and got to the gate at about ten to eight, and the man in the cabin was about fifty-five and had a hi-vis on over a fleece and did not ask him for anything at all.

"You're the service one?"

"Service," said Marek Senn. "I've got a booking on a sheet for about four days of rigging."

"Go on then. Second sign, past the weighbridge, there's a gap in the fence."

That was it. He did not ask for a card. Nobody looked at the lanyard. **A booking is a gate, and a gate does not care who is on the invoice, and a man of fifty-five at a barrier on a Thursday morning is the cleanest thing in this business and it is going to stay that way for exactly as long as nobody puts a condition on it.**

There is a thing that happens at a gate on a site in this country and it is not a greeting. Somebody in a hi-vis comes out with a clipboard and a board and goes through a list of names, and the list has tick boxes in it, and the tick boxes are: who is coming in, which company, what is the job, and have you been on this site before.

The man in the cabin did none of that, and Marek Senn understood about nine seconds later why. **The booking had already done it.** His name, the company, the address, the day and the four days of rigging were in a system in Lattice, and the booking had been printed and put in a folder in the cabin of a van at the barrier, and the man in the cabin had a clipboard and a folder and a plastic wallet of cards, and in the folder there was a printed page with a grid on it, and on the grid there were three columns and the middle one had his company in it.

"That'll be you, then," the man said, and stamped nothing, and wrote nothing, and lifted the barrier.

**Nobody looked at the lanyard. Nobody asked for a card. Nobody asked him whether he had done a course, and nobody would have cared if they had.** The only thing in the whole of that process that was a condition was a line in a grid with a date on it, and the date was written by a fitter in a depot in Lattice, and the fitter had not thought about it for a second, and it had opened a gate. He stood on the other side of it with a meter bag at his feet and had the strongest feeling he has had since the middle of the summer, and it was not a good one, and it took him about four minutes to work out what it was.

It was that the thing he had spent a term being proud of not doing had just been done for him, correctly, on a Monday, in about eleven seconds, by a man who had never heard of it.

The site was bigger than the address suggested. It went back a long way and there was hard standing and then a building with a corrugated roof and about nine men in it who were not the company's, and a slab office with a light on in it at about half past seven in the morning, and a chain link fence around the whole of it with a sign on the fence that had a company's name on it, and a different company's name on the prefab.

The rig was a food handling conveyor run, about ninety feet, with a lift at each end and a packer in the middle, and it had been stood down since the Monday of week twenty-one with a fault that came and went.

Marek Senn spent about twenty minutes on it with a meter bag and a torch and then went and got the foreman.

"Proximity switch," he said. "Gate two, the one that stops the lift if anybody is in it. It is not failing because of a wire and it is not failing because of the switch. **Look at that.**"

The foreman was a man of about forty-four called Micky Faul and he had a radio on his belt that was not a company radio.

"That clip."

"Two of those," said Marek Senn. "They are ferrite and they are for the machine, not for the signal, and the one on gate two has a crack round the back of it where it goes over the gland, and that is about four months old, and it looks fine from the front. That is why it has been coming and going since about the spring. Every time somebody has gone over it they have adjusted the switch, and every time they have adjusted the switch they have moved the target, and that is the repair that never is."

"How do you know."

"Because I have had about nine of these and the other eight were wires." He put the meter bag down. "It is not a fault anybody can be shouted at for and it is not a fault anybody has hidden. Somebody has genuinely been trying to fix it for four months. **Do you want me to order two of them, and do you want me to write *ferrite clip, cracked* on the sheet instead of *proximity switch intermittent*, because the second one is going to have you in a meeting in about four months and the first one is going to have you in one in about four years.**"

Faul looked at him for a second.

"Write the second one," he said. "Write *ferrite clip, cracked*. I'm not having a meeting in four years either."

There were about nine men on site and not one of them worked for the company, and that took about four minutes to establish and about a quarter of an hour to stop minding. They were the packagers'. They had a bank of lockers in a cabin, a kettle, a radio on a shelf, and a foreman called Micky Faul who had been on the site nine years, and about half of what they knew about the equipment in the building they had learned from the men who installed it.

"There is a card on the post at the top end," said Faul, about twenty minutes in, when Marek Senn asked where the emergency stop was. "It came with the building."

Marek Senn walked up to the control post at the top end of the run.

There was a card in a plastic sleeve, held on with two cable ties, sun-bleached to a pale nothing, at about chest height on a post that had been painted over twice. It had four steps on it in a typeface that had been out of use in this city for a very long time, and it had a line at the bottom, and the line at the bottom said:

*RELEASE IS GIVEN BY ANOTHER VOICE. DO NOT RELEASE ON YOUR OWN JUDGEMENT.*

There was no name on it. There was no date on it and no company mark and no version number and nothing at all on it that said who had printed it, and the plastic sleeve had been on it long enough that the two cable ties had gone cloudy and one of them had a small buckle in it that somebody had put there.

"**How long's that been on there?**" Marek Senn asked.

"That?" Faul came up and looked at it the way a man looks at a thing that has been in his field of vision for nine years. "Since before I came. And the fella before me, and his fella said it was there when he came."

"Has anybody ever asked you where it came from."

"No," said Micky Faul. "**And nobody's ever asked me what's on the other cards either, and there's two on the wall by the office, and the lads don't read them and I don't read them and the bloke who put them up is not coming back.**"

"Do your lads do it."

"They do what the card says," said Faul. "Well — mostly they do what the man on the radio says, and when the man on the radio doesn't say it they wait, and about nine times out of ten that's the right call and the tenth time it isn't, and if you asked me to write down the tenth time I couldn't tell you, and I've been here nine years."

Marek Senn stood at a control post on a customer's site with his hand about four inches from a plastic sleeve and understood that he was looking at a thing that had been on that post for at least twenty years, that had cost somebody nothing, that had never been examined by a single person with a job that involved examining anything, and that had probably saved more people in that building than the whole of the case he has spent a term on.

He did not touch it. He wrote *card on control post, post 1, no date, no source — ask* on the back of a docket, and then he looked at the docket, and then he wrote *ask* out, because a docket with the word *ask* on it is a promise, and he is not going to make one in a building he cannot be found in.

Then he got on with the fault.

They ordered the parts at about nine. Marek Senn did the rest of the morning on the gates and the lift and the packer, and about half of it was not the fault, and he did it because four days had been booked and because a man who books four days and leaves after a morning has told the customer he is not needed.

The slab office has one desk, one chair, a heater, a kettle, and a filing tray with about four inches of paper in it, and at about ten past twelve Marek Senn asked the question he had been not asking since seven in the morning, which was how a rig gets ordered onto a site.

Faul got the tray out because it was sitting on the desk and it was in the way.

"That's not ours, that's the packagers', and I don't—" He stopped, because he was going through it. "Hold on. That's your lot."

"Show me."

It was a printout about nine pages stapled at one corner, and it was an assembly record, and it was about two years old, and it was not the company's own paperwork. It came off a van with about forty other lots.

Marek Senn did not ask for it. He did not ask for anything. He put the meter bag on the floor and looked at the top sheet, and in the header there was a field, and in the field there was nothing, and about two inches below the field somebody had typed a lot reference in a stores clerk's hand: *Meridian consolidation point, lot onward.*

**Meridian consolidation point, lot onward.** Six weeks ago that had been a line in an intake summary in a company system in Lattice, and a man's mouth on a Friday afternoon, and a building on a corridor that he had never been inside. Now it was a printout on a desk in a prefab office on a site where he was the only person with a lanyard on.

"Bloody hell," said Micky Faul, over his shoulder. "That's our lot, is it. That's the door gear."

"Is it."

"That's the door gear and the yard barrier and about six of the lamps, that came in on a wagon with a load of flour base in the spring." He took it out of Marek Senn's hands, which he had not expected, and read the lot line the way a man reads a line about his own shed. "**You lot are quick this year.**"

"Quick about what."

"Coming and looking at it." He put the printout down on the desk. "There's not been anybody up here about the gear since the spring. Years, since I've been on. The gear goes in and it works and nobody comes back, and the ones who come back are usually after something's broken and by then it's not the gear, it's a photocell in a housing that cost ninety pound." He looked up. "You lot are quick this year. Last lot that came in, we didn't see anybody for a year and a bit, and then a fella turned up with a clipboard in a fluorescent vest and he was from a different company altogether and he couldn't tell me what he'd come for, and I said get off my site, and he went."

There was a moment on that desk that Marek Senn did not write down and has never told anybody about and is not going to put in this book either, and it was four seconds long, and it was this: **a man of about forty-four on a site in this city was pleased, genuinely and uncomplicatedly pleased, that a man in a lanyard had come to look at his gear, and had said so out loud, and had told a story about the last one as a joke.** There is nothing wrong with that sentence. There is nothing at all wrong with it. It is the best possible outcome of a system in which a person with a lanyard comes and looks at a thing and cannot answer a question about why he is here, and it is the sentence that the whole of this case exists to make impossible, and the reason it is impossible to argue with is that the man is not lying and neither is the man in the lanyard.

"Fair enough," said Marek Senn.

"Right." Faul put the printout back on the pile. "Well, if you're that quick, have a look at the barrier while you're here, it has been slow since the summer and I've had the gate man in twice."

The goods-in sheet was in the tray as well, and he did not ask for it either.

It was a carbon copy on pale pink paper with a grid on it and about nine lines on it going back about four months, and the columns were date, carrier, reference, and received by. **Four months of deliveries off a wagon, and four of the nine of them inside a fortnight,** and the reference column had a lot number in it on every line, and four of the lots said the same thing that the printout in the office said, and one said *lot onward* again, and one said *lot onward — per driver*, which was a different hand from the stores clerk's and was a driver's.

The last column was the one he looked at for the longest.

**Every one of the nine signatures was a printed name.** Not a printed signature — a printed name, in a typeface, in capitals, in a box, which is what you get when a system writes the name of whoever is logged in, and a man on a wagon signs a sheet, and the two are then reconciled by somebody at a desk, and the desk wins, and after about a month the paper is a list of names that were in a building on a day.

Marek Senn looked at nine printed names on pale pink paper for about a minute and a half, and none of them were the name of a man, and every one of them was a fact, and a fact that is a person is a record of a person, and there were now about two hundred of those in a bundle in this city and about nine on a sheet in a tray in a prefab office forty feet from where he was standing, and the two had been made by two entirely reasonable people in two buildings for two entirely reasonable reasons.

He wrote nothing on the docket. He put the pink sheet back in the tray in the same order, which he checked twice.

He had the barrier at about twenty past two and the fault was a loop and the loop was fine, and he spent forty minutes proving that, and then he found the actual cause, which was that the safety edge on the leading face had been replaced with a different product from the one the gate was built for and it has a different dip switch, and the dip switch was set two positions out. It was a nine-minute fix and it was not his company's part and it was not his company's gate and he could have written it up as a customer's fault and gone home at three.

He fixed it, and he wrote on the sheet: *safety edge, aftermarket, dip switch set to position 3, reset to 2. Part is a customer's. Advised customer in writing.*

He stood there for a second afterwards with the pen in his hand over that line, because *advised customer in writing* is a sentence that puts a fact about a gate into a document, and a document is the thing he has spent a term being frightened of, and the fact is small and true and it fixes a gate for a man of forty-four.

He left the line in.

The lot reference was on page nine and it was under the same blank field, and about an inch and a half below the field, in a different pen, in a different hand, in biro, somebody had written a box number.

**It was not a stores clerk's hand. It was a site man's hand, and it had been written on a printout in an office because a man on a wagon had asked somebody to write down where the thing had come from, and a stores clerk in a warehouse three hundred miles away had typed a lot onward and had not known it was not an address, and this site had corrected it. In biro. On a printout. About two years ago.**

He read it four times.

Then he took his own pencil out of his pocket, and there was a docket in the van with about four things on it that he needed, and the docket would have taken six more characters without any trouble at all, and he put the pencil back.

Micky Faul came past with a clipboard.

"Got what you need?"

"Got most of it."

"You're writing the box number down, aren't you."

"No," said Marek Senn.

"Why not?"

"Because I do not put that on my paperwork, and I am not going to tell you why, and you are not going to like it." He said it flatly and it was the sixth time in a term he had said a sentence in that shape and it came out of him with a speed that frightened him slightly. "But I am going to ask you one question and it is the only one I have asked all day. Does that paperwork go anywhere?"

"Why?"

"Because if it goes in a bin at the end of the year then I would like to come back before it does."

Faul looked at him. "You want a booking."

"I want a booking. There is a lamp on the packer with a cheap ballast on it that is going to go in about a fortnight, and when it goes I am going to be the man who comes and puts it back, and I will be able to see that tray while I am up here."

"Right." He put the clipboard under his arm. "**That is not a fault that has happened yet, that is a fault that is going to happen on purpose.**"

"It is going to happen anyway. The ballast came in on the same wagon as the gate and it is a cheap one, and the cheap ones go all at once."

"Everybody says that and then nothing happens for two years."

"Then I will be wrong twice and I will have had two reasons to come," said Marek Senn, "and I would rather be wrong twice than write a box number on a docket in my own hand, and that is the entire reason and it is not a good enough reason and I have not got a better one."

Faul laughed at him, which was fair.

"Book the lamp," he said. "**I will put the tray in the office and I will keep it, and I am not going to tell you that is a favour, because I am not doing it for you, I am doing it because in about four years somebody is going to come through that gate with a different company's name on their lanyard and ask me where the gear came from and I am going to want to be able to say.**"

He got back to the gate at about twenty past three and the man in the cabin lifted the barrier and did not ask him for anything, because it was a booking and a gate does not care.

He drove about four miles and stopped at the side of a road and sat there with the engine off for a while, and then he took a piece of paper out of the middle pocket of his rucksack, which was the one he keeps for things he is not going to write down, and he wrote on it:

*Blank field, nine pages, assembly record, printout, prefab office, tray, top sheet. Lot onward typed. Box number in biro, different hand, added on site.*

No number. No site name. No road.

Then he put that sheet in the same pocket with the other three of them, which is a pocket he has had since the summer and has never shown to anybody and has never photographed, and he started the van.
