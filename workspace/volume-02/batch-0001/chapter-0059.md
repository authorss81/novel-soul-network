# Chapter 59 — The First Wednesday

The common room was on the first floor of the Ninth Street block, which was one of the four places in this city holding revision 4-19 stock, and it had eleven people in it at about a quarter to ten on the Wednesday morning of week sixteen, and nine of them were first years.

Marek Senn got to the door at about ten past nine and did not go in.

He stood in the corridor for about forty minutes. There was a fire door at the end of it with a wired-glass panel and a bench, and he sat on the bench, and he could hear a girl talking, and at about two minutes to ten she stopped, and there was a silence of about ten seconds, and then somebody said *all right* and a chair went over, and eleven people came out into the corridor and went off in two directions.

Asha Reed came out last, with her notebook, and she saw him on the bench from about six feet away and came over and sat down on the other end of it.

"You were out there for the whole of it," she said.

"Since about ten past nine."

"You were out there for the whole of it," said Asha Reed, "and you did not come in, and I am not going to do the thing where I thank you for that, and I am also not going to do the thing where I tell you it would have been better if you had. You were outside. That is a fact about you and I have got it and I am not going to use it."

"What did you say."

"I said the case is Senn's and the technique is nobody's yet, and I said it in a first-year common room on the first Wednesday of every month, and there is one voice in that room and it is mine, and eleven people heard it and about two of them wrote it down." She turned the notebook over on her knee. "And then Toby asked me the question I have not got an answer to, and I answered it, and the answer is in here and I would like to read it to you, and I am not going to, because I read the first column of a four-column book out loud in a market on the Wednesday of week fourteen to nine people by a rule an ice merchant invented in eleven seconds, and a fortnight ago I did not know that a piece of paper was going to be able to do that, and now I do, and everything I say in this room is a thing nine hundred addresses could have in a season."

"So you said it and you have not decided what to do with it."

"I said it and I cannot un-say it and I have not decided anything." She shut the notebook. "I still do not know how to fix it. I said that out loud as well, in the room, and I have said it in here and I have said it to you, and I have said it three times now and it has not stopped being true, which is a thing I expected it to do."

"How did it go."

"Nine of them asked a question," said Asha Reed. "Not a good question. A *what do I do* question. Nine of them, out of eleven, and Toby asked the true one, which was whether there is anything in this that we can be taught, and I said no, and I said that out loud in a room where I am the only voice, and about four of them looked at the floor, and that is correct, and I would do it again." She got up. "Your case has no technique in it. That is the sentence I have to say on the first Wednesday of every month, and in about a year it is going to be the sentence that people are tired of hearing, and that will be the day it is either true or a habit, and I will not be able to tell you which from inside."

"Marek."

"Don't."

He looked up.

"I have been offered a job," he said. "A real one. Four days at a company, a wage, a rota, a month's notice, and a clause that says I cannot be alone in a room with a plate on their premises, and I have not said yes, and I have asked for the Friday of week eighteen instead, and I have been thinking about the asking for the Friday of week eighteen for two days and I have not got anywhere with it, and I have just come out of a corridor having listened to you tell nine people the truth and I would like to say one sentence and I am going to say it badly."

"Say it."

"I have spent fourteen weeks refusing to make things exist, and I have been offered a job inside the company that made the thing, and I have not worked out why those two things are compatible, and if anybody in this case is in a position to notice that I am going to be the fourth thing in a different building, it is going to be the person who said the sentence to nine people on the first Wednesday of every month for a year without knowing how to fix it."

Asha Reed stood in the corridor and did not move for a second and a half.

"Right," she said. "That's two things. One. You did not come in." She held up a finger. "You did not come in, and you have sat on that bench for forty minutes, and in about a year there is going to be a version of this where people say that a third-year sat outside a first-year common room, and I am going to have to correct it every time, and it is going to be tedious, and I want you to know that I noticed and that I am not going to be the one who says it out loud for you."

"Marek—"

"Two," said Asha Reed. "You are going to say *if anybody in this case is in a position to notice*, and you are going to keep saying it, and it is going to make me the fourth thing in a building that I have never been inside, and I have watched a woman of thirty-three say exactly that sentence to you at a bench in this city on the Monday of week fifteen." She put the notebook under her arm. "Do not do that. I am nineteen and I have got a room on the first Wednesday of the month and I have said out loud that I cannot fix it, and that is the whole of what I have got, and if you make it into a post in a company then in six months somebody in a depot will be able to come and find me, and they will find me because I will have been named somewhere by somebody who was trying to help."

He said nothing, which was the right thing.

"Good," said Asha Reed. "Now go to the Exchange, because it is ten past ten, the meeting is ten to eleven, you are forty minutes late, and I am not going to tell you what I think about that either." She went off down the corridor. "And I will tell you one thing free, since you have been on that bench the whole time and have not asked me a single question. A man of twenty-three put a sheet through the box at the post office on Weaver Street on the Tuesday and got a card back, and he has not told you, and he is going to tell you in about nine minutes in front of twelve people, and you are going to be late for that as well."

---

Marek Senn got to the Saltmarket at ten past ten on the Wednesday morning, and the door of the back room was shut, and the tin was on the table behind it, and the meeting had started at ten and went to eleven, and there were about a dozen people in there, and he was outside.

He stood in the corridor by the kitchen for fifty minutes and heard all of it through a door, which is a thing he had done twice before in a term in worse rooms, and he did not go in, because the first thing Bea Nunn had said to him in the twelfth week was that anybody could put a card in the tin and be in the room, and he had not put one in, and a man who is not in a room does not open the door to it.

**One.** Petar Vance, in his own hand, dated the Friday: his district has nine crews on the road and he wants the Saturday refresher to run again on a different day, because the Saturday is a working day for most of the men who come and he cannot get them there in daylight in a term, and he does not care which company runs it.

**Two.** A card from a woman in the Cordage about a boiler. It took two minutes and it was a boiler.

**Three.** A card from Ines Sarr with *this is the fourth one I have written* on it, about a college being in a first-floor room in Nine Bridges on the Friday of week fifteen for two and a half hours with eleven people in it, and a college not being a public room, and a room not being a public room if the people in it were sent there by a college, and what happens to a person who turns up.

Nobody argued about it for two minutes, which was a new thing, and then Bea Nunn said "noted", and said, "It's noted, and I'm going to say one thing about it and then I'll shut up, because Bea Nunn gets four minutes and I've had thirty seconds."

Through the door it sounded like this:

"That room's shut, and it was shut in a car park by a woman who had the whole of the argument ready before anybody asked her a question, and I was in the lay-by. And I'll tell you the thing she asked, because it's the only question anybody's asked about a room in four years and it's the question I'd have asked. Not *who's responsible for it*. She asked **who would take the name out for them.** And every one of us in this room has a version of an answer, and Ines's is that a college is not a room, and Ines is right, and Ines's version is that a college should stop asking for rooms, and that's not an answer to the question she asked, that's an answer to a different one, and if somebody writes that down and files it, then in four years we've won and lost the same afternoon."

**Four.** Ruslan Petric. It was short and it had one question on it and he read it out himself, standing up, and he would not sit down afterwards.

> *Is it allowed for me to ask a question at this meeting about a thing where the answer is no.*

*Yes*, said Bea Nunn, without looking at her card.

> *Right. I have got two things and the first one is the smaller one. I put a sheet through a box at the post office on Weaver Street on the Tuesday, because a sheet came through the letterbox of a shop in week fourteen that said anybody who was in that room could ask for a thing of theirs to be taken out, with no form, and the only thing I had to give was where to send the answer, and I gave them a box number, and I did not tell anybody I had, including him, and I am telling you because you have got a rule and a rule is the only place a person like me can put a thing.*
>
> *And this is what came back, and I am going to read it out, and then somebody is going to tell me whether I am allowed to read it out: it is a playing card, and on this side of it in a ballpoint it says three words. **It was not there.** That is all. No name, no second side, no way of finding out who looked.*

Nobody said anything for a while, and then somebody said *that's the whole record of you then*, and Ruslan Petric said, "That is what it says. **It is not a lie and it is not a removal. It says they checked, and the only thing in the record about me is that a second candidate followed a word, and there is nothing in it to take out, and they are not going to pretend there is, and I asked.** And I want it in the book in my own words, and I want somebody to read it back to me, and I have never asked for a thing in a room in my life."

"That is two conditions and one of them is a good one and neither of them is a price," said Bea Nunn. "Ekow, have you got a pen."

> *And the second thing*, said Ruslan Petric, and he did not sit down, and the chair let him not sit down. *I have been three weeks working out whether I can say in a room that a member of the teaching staff said a word from the front of a room on the Tuesday of week fourteen and that I did what about nine other people did. And I have worked out that I can, and I want to say it now, in front of these twelve people, in the first person, with my name at the end of it, and I have already said it once this morning to a postbox and got three words back, and the difference between saying it to a postbox and saying it to a room of people is about nine seconds long and I do not know which of the two is braver and I am tired of guessing.*

> *Right. I am saying it.*

"Nobody take that card out of the book," said Bea Nunn, about nine seconds later, to a room that had heard her say it once already on the Monday.

**Five.** Reuben Daw's line, which he had written himself in block capitals on the Thursday morning at about ten past eight and signed at the end of, and which Bea Nunn had written up as line thirty-eight with his own words and his own signature and her own name in the fourth column. She read it out in full — every line of it, all four hundred words — because she does not read the short ones in full either, and because reading it out is the opposite of taking it out.

It took four minutes and eleven seconds, and the room did very well, and afterwards nobody asked a question about it, and that was the correct number of questions.

**Six.** A card from Ena Truslove of the Fold nursery, which took the full four minutes, and which was about thirty plastic chairs.

The card said that the chairs are hers, that they come from the building next door, that they are stacked in a corridor on Tuesdays and Thursdays and are otherwise in a cupboard, and that anybody in this city who has a thing to say in a room to about twenty people could have them for a Saturday, and that there is nothing to pay and there is no form, and she would bring them herself and take them back herself and she would not send a van.

"You have not asked me," said Bea Nunn.

"You have not been here on a Thursday," said Ena Truslove. "I asked Bea on the Monday and she said the room is lent to a member by arrangement and a member can lend it to whoever they like, and I have been a member for eleven years and I have never once used that sentence, and I would like it minuted that I know what I have just done."

"You have used it," said Bea Nunn, writing.

"I have used it to give away thirty chairs, which is not the same as using it to give away a room. And I know the difference, and the difference is the thirty chairs." She put her card on the table. "And I am going to say the thing nobody has said. Those chairs went into a college on the Tuesday of week fourteen because they were mine and Friday had come and gone, and I was the reason a room was public, with a hand truck, at nine o'clock, and nobody from this nursery was asked and nobody from this nursery has been thanked, and I have thought about that for a fortnight and I have not worked out whether I would do it again."

"You would," said a voice by the door, which was Marek Senn's, and everybody in the room turned round, and he did not come in.

"That is the man on the board with the list on it," said Bea Nunn, without looking up. "You are forty-five minutes late and you are standing in my corridor. Sit down or go away."

"I will go away," said Marek Senn. "I heard the last three minutes through the door and that is the answer to a thing I have been carrying about for a fortnight, and I am not going to take four minutes off a woman who has not offered them."

"That," said Bea Nunn, writing, "is noted, and it goes in the book as a caller who was in the corridor, because the rule does not have a word for what he was and I am not making one up at sixty-one. M. Senn, in the corridor, at about a quarter to eleven."

He did not argue about it, and she did not look up, and he went back down the corridor and stood by the kitchen door for another four minutes, and then went out into the market and did a job he had not booked.

He wrote the page that night at the bench with the door open and the extractor on.

*61. Wednesday of week 16. I sat on a bench in a corridor for forty minutes this morning and did not go into a room, and then I stood in a corridor outside another room for fifty minutes and did not go into that one either, and there is a rule on a wall in Lattice Ward with my name in the credit of it, and the rule is a rule for anybody who is named in a book, and I have not been in either room and I got nothing taken out and I said nothing and I could not have done either of those things in a room, and I have not decided yet what the two facts are.*

*What touched it.* A boy of twenty-three put a sheet through a postbox on the Tuesday and got three words back, and read it out in a market in front of twelve people at about a quarter to eleven this morning, and somebody wrote it in a book with his name at the end of it, and I heard it through a door. And a woman of about sixty came into that room and said a thing about thirty chairs and a hand truck that she has not been thanked for, and I was the one who was asked about it and I answered it from a corridor because it was the true answer.*

*How a person stops it.* A woman of sixty-one with a tin and a rule that takes eleven seconds to write and four weeks to stop believing in. That is the whole of it and I have got nothing to add and I have not been able to add anything to it in fourteen weeks.*

*How many people have been put through it.* Five. Four of them I can name and one of them is a man of twenty-three who has now put two things into two different buildings on two different days and cannot work out whether he has been safer or more findable, and I think the answer is both, and I think that is what being in a book is.*

*Do not know.* Whether I will file the request. The third entry running and the first one where the answer is not *no on principle*. **And today is the Wednesday and there is no letter.** I have been at this bench since half past four and there is no letter, and the conditions went at midnight the way a condition goes when nobody has written the mechanism down, and I have not rung the woman in Cutter Street, and she told me on the Tuesday that she would put the lapse in writing, and she has not, and I have decided that I would rather she never does. **I wrote the word *letter* in this book four times this term and every single time I meant punishment, and today a condition expired and nobody wrote me a word, and that is the correct outcome and I have got no way at all of making it feel like one. I have written that here because nobody else is going to and because a book nobody reads is now the only place in this city where the true sentence is going to be, and that is a sentence about a man and not about a rule, and I am aware of it.*

He put the pen down at about half past ten and left it where it was, and got out the form he had written out in ninety seconds on a kitchen table on the Sunday morning of week twelve — a carrier bag with his name on it, a reference number, a ground, and four headings — and read that too, and put it back, and it was still folded, and it was not in a drawer, and it was not in a postbox, and it was going to be in a drawer under a battery and a roll of solder by the Sunday, next to a page with a woman's name and her wrist on it that has not been looked at since she asked.

At about ten past four in the afternoon of the Wednesday of week sixteen, at the top of a set of stairs above a shop in Nine Bridges, Delia Frost stopped.

She was sixty-one and the hip unit was thirteen weeks old and it had been ten weeks old on the Wednesday of week thirteen, when she had telephoned a repair shop at six in the morning and spoken for nineteen minutes and had been seen by a clinician at eleven.

She got short of breath about four in the afternoon. Twice a day. It goes in ten minutes or a quarter of an hour. She has told one person, on a telephone, on a Wednesday morning in the middle of a term, and she has not told the woman she rang the number about, and she has not told the shop, because the shop is a shop and the man in it is a stranger, and she is perfectly clear about the difference and she said so on the telephone and meant it and would say it again.

She stood on the landing for about forty seconds.

Marek Senn was four miles away at a bench in Lattice Ward, writing a page about a deadline that had passed in silence, and he was not told about the landing, and there is nobody in this city whose job it is to be told, and there was not going to be one in September either.
