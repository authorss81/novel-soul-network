# Chapter 52 — The Same Four Words

Petar Vance rang the shop at about twenty past eight on the Wednesday morning of week fifteen and Marek Senn told him, before he had said anything, that he was not going to ask about the van book.

"I wasn't going to give you the van book," said Vance. "The van book is in a locked office in a building I do not have a key to and I have not asked for a key to twice. I rang because you asked me for the four words on the Tuesday and I have got them written down and I have done a thing I should not have done, and I want somebody to know I have done it before somebody finds out I have done it."

"Say the thing first and I will promise you nothing."

"Visitor column," said Vance. "It is a sheet on a spike by the gate at each depot. It is a carbon, it is a copy, and it does four things: date, name, who they came for, and one line about why. It has had the same four words on it twice in six weeks and I have never seen those four words on any Threadline document in eleven years, and I have been a service engineer on this district for eleven of the last eleven years, which means I have read every piece of literature this company has ever printed."

"Four words."

"Deliveries — north — awaiting — signature."

Marek Senn put the receiver down on the bench and picked it up again.

"That is a normal line for a van that has come back for a signature."

"It is an excellent line for a van that has come back for a signature. It is a line I have written myself four hundred times. It is also, and I have been putting these two facts next to each other in my head for a fortnight and I do not like it, the only four-word line in that book that nobody has ever been able to tell is false."

"You cannot tell it is false."

"No. That is the whole thing and I have not been able to say it out loud to anybody in this company without sounding like a man who has been reading a book." He shifted. "So I did the thing I should not have done. I rang the depot at Rotherham, which is forty minutes up the corridor, and I have known the assistant there for nine years and I have never once asked him a question that was not about a part. I asked him what his visitor column says."

"And?"

"And nothing. He said he would look and ring me back, and he rang me back on the Sunday evening at about half past seven at home, and he read it out to me over a mobile telephone in a car park, which I did not enjoy." A pause. "It says the same four words, fourteen times in a fortnight, which is nothing, that depot gets forty vans a day, and if it was the filler for a line that says nothing then it would be on every sheet in that building since it was put up."

"Right."

"But here is the part I actually rang about, and I have written it down and I am reading it off the sheet." Paper. "The fifth copy of your bulletin went off the second shelf on the Tuesday of week fourteen. The stock docket for it is D/2214/6, and the second shelf is where we put things that are going to a registered office that does not want them on the manifest, and the manifest was recycled on the Monday, which is normal, we recycle on the Mondays, and D/2214/6 is still on the docket spike because dockets do not get recycled, they get destroyed, and nobody destroyed it, because destroying them is on a Friday and I have been off since the Tuesday."

"So the docket is still there."

"The docket is still there. The number is on the spike. The sheet is on the spike with the number and a line saying one item, one unit, and a destination, and the destination is a box number and a post town, and I know the box number and I know the post town and it is not Nacre." He stopped. "Marek, I am not going to tell you what the box number is on a telephone, and I have not written it down, and I want that on whatever you are about to write. I have not written it down anywhere and I have not put it in the load book and I am not going to. It is the one thing I have got and it is not mine."

Marek Senn wrote two lines on the back of a job card and did not write on them yet.

"What did you take out of that depot on the Tuesday."

"Three things. The bulletin, a carton of booklets, and a replacement instructor card set, four of them, sealed, revision 4-19, in a brown sleeve. The card sets are not stock. We stopped selling the 4-19 card in October. They are on the shelf because the last service round in the district had three classrooms that asked for a spare, and that was in week eight, and I signed for them myself and I have thought about that in the last fortnight more than I would like."

"You signed for them."

"I signed for them because I am the engineer and that is the signature line and nobody has ever been disciplined for signing for a thing that was on the shelf. That is the whole of my crime and I would like it noted that I have not slept properly since the Tuesday." He let a second go. "And nobody came for the carton. It is still in the bay. I have been down there twice and it is still in the bay and I have not moved it and I have not put a label on it, and if I put a label on it that is me deciding something, and I am not paid to decide something this week."

"Then leave it."

"I will leave it," said Vance. "And I want to say the last thing and then I have got a valve to do at eleven. The four words are on two spikes in two buildings. I have not got a name and I have not got a man and I have not got a reason. What I have got is that a company that has a problem does not write the same four words twice and hope. So either somebody is very lucky, or somebody is putting something into that depot in a shape that comes out looking like nothing, and the second one is not a thing a man can say out loud in a room, which is why I rang a repair shop instead of my depot manager."

"You should have rung your depot manager."

"I have known that for four days," said Petar Vance, "and I have decided I would rather be wrong in the wrong direction than be right in a way that puts me in a form."

Rafi Pell came into the shop at about half past eleven on the Wednesday morning between two runs, with a carrier bag and a piece of paper folded in four, and put the carrier bag down and did not say what was in it, which is a courier's habit and not a lie.

"You've got a Saturday somewhere," said Marek Senn.

"I've got a Saturday on a yard off the Meridian and it is not the one I lost, and I want to be extremely clear about the order of those two sentences, because I have been sitting on a bus thinking about it for an hour and the order matters." He put the folded paper on the bench. "The one I lost, I lost. Six of them. It's gone. This is a new one, at twelve pounds, and it is a Saturday, and I am not going to pretend to somebody I got it back."

"Nobody's pretending anything."

"I know. I want it said anyway, because you are the man people say things to and I would rather I said it first." He turned the folded paper round. "They've given us a pack. District's mark on the front, and a date on the front, and the date is the twelfth, and the twelfth was the week before the thing happened. That's what I want you to notice. Not that it's a bad pack. It isn't a bad pack. It's a good pack. It's got photographs."

Marek Senn did not touch the paper.

"Who gave it to you?"

"Supervisor. Woman. Forty-six-ish, glasses on a chain, name badge, and she was good, and I want that written down as well because nobody has said that part and I have been thinking about it all morning." He took his hands out of his pockets. "She did the whole yard in about an hour and a half and she did it in the sun and she was talking the whole time, and she did not once tell anybody to stop working, which is what I expected, because I have been to two of these and both of them were somebody standing at the front telling you to stop working. She told us we could keep going, and then she said the new thing, which is that if there's a load you are not sure about you do not do it on your own, you say it out loud and you wait, and every supervisor on that yard has got a radio and there is always one of them on."

He put both hands flat on the bench.

"And that's the whole of what I got. That is a Saturday, and that is what a Saturday is now."

Marek Senn looked at the paper on the bench and did not move.

"What's the word she uses."

"I don't know. I didn't write it down. I've been trying to remember whether it was a word or just the shape she made with her hand, and I remember the hand." He looked slightly annoyed with himself. "That's the bit I can't give you. I know. I know that's the bit. I sat on a bus for an hour and I can give you the pack and the date and the woman and the shape of her hand and not the word."

"That's a proper answer, though."

"Is it? Because if you'd asked me on the Friday I'd have said the word. I'd have said it in about a second, and I'd have said it wrongly, because I always say the second thing." He pushed the paper across. "Look at page nine. Don't take it, look at it. There's a bit at the bottom of page nine and it's got a box round it, and I'll tell you what's in the box because I can't read the writing in it and I don't want it in my head the way I want the rest of this not in my head."

He went and stood by the sink with his back to the room, which Rafi Pell has done in a kitchen and in a market and in this shop three times now, and Marek Senn let him.

It was a box, and under the box in a small print, four words.

**Releases confirmed by supervisor.**

Marek Senn read it twice and then put the paper down on the bench, squared, and then took his hand off it, which he had started doing a fortnight ago and had not told anybody about.

"Right," he said.

---

He went out to the depot on the Wednesday afternoon because there was a valve on a Saturday job and the depot is where valves are, and because a man who has been told a thing on a telephone is entitled to look at the thing.

The visitor column was a carbon sheet on a spike by the gate, a bit of string through the corner, and it had about forty lines on it going back four months in a hard pencil, and Marek Senn stood at the gate and read forty lines in about three minutes and the words *deliveries north awaiting signature* were not on one of them.

There is about a sheet a week on that spike going back four months, and it was on eleven lines out of about four hundred, and two of the eleven are inside the last six weeks and the other nine are older than anybody can date from a spike. He counted them twice. The second of the two is on the Tuesday of week fourteen, about four minutes after a stock docket for one item and one unit had been signed in a hand that was not a driver's.

He wrote nothing down. He stood at the gate and looked at the eleven lines, and then at the red brick, and then at the yard behind it where somebody's van was up on a ramp with its doors open, and he understood, standing there, exactly what Petar Vance had understood in a car park on the Sunday, and what it was worth.

Because the four words are on eleven lines out of about four hundred. That means they are not a filler. A filler is on every line. A filler is a thing you can see and read and check and say *that is nothing, that is a line that says nothing.* **These are eleven lines out of four hundred, which means somebody wrote them, and then somebody else did not write them, eleven times, on purpose, and there is no way at all of finding out which is which from a spike by a gate.**

He went inside and asked the man on the counter, who was about twenty-three and called Okonkwo, whether the visitor column was ever copied.
"Copa?" He said it the way you say a word for a thing that is a daft question. "No."

"Ever photographed?"

"No."

"Does Dunleavy take a photograph of the gate for anything?"

"He took one of the door in the summer for the depot thing," said Okonkwo. "There's a fire thing. Door, roller shutter, bollards, exit. That's it. It's a list."

And it was a list, and the list did not have a gate on it, and Marek Senn stood in a depot corridor at about half past two in the afternoon of the Wednesday of week fifteen and had in his hands the fact that a photograph of eleven words exists in a phone in a van in about nine places, and that the reason he could not ask for one of them was the same reason he had not filed the September register, and that a service engineer had already worked all of that out in a car park and had told him on the telephone before he got here.

He went out to the gate on the way out and put his hand flat on the top of the spike without touching the sheets, the way you would steady a fence post, and then took his hand away, because there was nothing to steady.

*58. Wednesday of week 15, later, and not the same evening as the rest of it: I could have had it in about nine telephone calls. A photograph of a gate with a carbon sheet on it. I stood at the counter of a company depot on the Wednesday afternoon and worked out in ninety seconds exactly how a photograph is taken and by whom and of what, and there are about nine vans in this district, and I did not make one of the calls, and I went back to a shop in Lattice Ward with nothing. The reason is the same sentence I have written six times since the fourth week of the term and I have never once got any further down it.*
