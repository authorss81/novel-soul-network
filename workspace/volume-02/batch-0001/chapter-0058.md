# Chapter 58 — The Reason on the Card

Oren Vey came into the shop at about twenty past four on the Tuesday of week sixteen with a document folder under his arm and a paper cup of coffee he did not drink, and stood in the middle of the floor for about four seconds looking at the wall, and then said, "You have got a list up."

"The trust put it up."

"I know. I read it. It is on page nine of our quarterly, which is where I found out that the condition I have been managing a file about for three weeks has already been read about by a co-operative." He put the folder down on the bench. "Do you mind if I sit down? I have been in a depot since seven and I am going to be in one again on Thursday."

"Sit down."

He did, and put the cup down without drinking from it, which told Marek Senn more about Oren Vey's week than forty minutes of talking would have.

"Three things," he said, "in this order, and the third one is not money and I would like to get to it and then stop."

"Go on."

"One. Your intake field. I have a written, logged, numbered request in my own system — the reference is on the top of it and I will give you the reference if you want it and I am not going to leave it here, because if I leave it here then somebody will find it in about four years and say I supplied it to an outside party." He turned a page round so it faced Marek Senn. "I asked every supplier in the district to complete the upstream technique bundle and the upstream licensor fields on every practice pack at intake, or refuse the pack, and four of them refused to send anything at all. That is in the file, in writing, with dates, and I would like you to understand that refusing a pack is a legitimate answer under my instruction and I told them so, because in this business an instruction that says *complete it or go away* gets you a company that fills in a field with a word rather than a name, and then we are the ones who printed a guess."

"Four of them out of how many."

"Eleven on the list. Seven came back within a fortnight and four of the seven have the field completed and three of the seven are blank, and all three of the three are genuinely blank, and I have gone back and looked at all three twice." He squared the page and did not leave it. "So. Here is the part I want and I am going to be straight about wanting it, because you have spent a term watching men in rooms not be straight and I would like to be the exception on a Tuesday afternoon. Eleven suppliers in this district and three of them will not put anything in a box that somebody has ruled, and a company of thirty people put its own lanyard on a Saturday and got thirty-eight people through a door, and the difference between those two facts is the whole of my working life and I do not know what to do about it."

"No. I do know what to do about it, but I want to say the true thing first, which is that the three blanks are the first good news I have had since the week of week eight. A blank with a name in the field next to it is a man who has guessed. A blank is a man who has gone to look. Every one of the three has written something in a box underneath, and I have read the three, and I am going to say what they say, and then you are going to do whatever you want with it and I am not going to write it in my own file, because if I do then you have given a supplier's answer to a customer and that is a supply chain problem I would like to keep for about a week."

"Go on."

"They are the same sentence. Word for word, on all three, and it is not the same man and it is not the same company and two of them have never spoken in their lives. It says: **the originating practice holds no record of it.**"

Marek Senn did not say anything for a moment.

"Say that again."

"The originating practice holds no record of it. Three suppliers, three packs, three buildings, one sentence. And the sentence is not a lawyer's sentence and it is not a stock phrase, because it is oddly specific — it is not *we no longer hold* and it is not *not applicable* and it is not *see attached*. It is *the originating practice holds no record of it*, which is a thing a person writes when they have telephoned somebody and been told something, and then written it down, and then sent the pack." He put his hand flat on the folder. "Marek, a records clerk of a co-operative in a market printed a ruled empty box under the heading UPSTREAM LICENSOR in a quarterly that went to nine hundred addresses on the Friday of week fourteen, and under it she wrote four lines saying that no member had supplied it and the originating practice holds no record of it, and she printed a request that readers treat it as a hole in the document. **I have got that sentence from three suppliers who cannot possibly have read that quarterly, and it is in my hand on a piece of paper I am about to put back in a folder, and I have been sitting in a depot since seven this morning with a headache and a piece of paper and I do not know what it means.**"

Marek Senn looked at the page for a while and then at the wall, which had a list on it in a plastic sleeve.

"Do not read the field," he said. "That is the first thing and it is counter-intuitive. **Do not ask the three for anything.** The field stays blank and goes on the label and goes out into nine hundred, a thousand, five thousand addresses, exactly as it has been doing for years, and you do not fill it and you do not annotate it and you do not put a note on the pack. If you fill it, the field becomes a record of a conversation you had in a corridor, and the sentence goes to file, and in four years a company that no longer exists gets cited for a sentence a girl of nineteen read out in a market."

"Then what."

"The sentence. It is the only thing in this case that has ever been in two places at once and it is in two places and neither of them knows about the other, and that means it came from a place. Not from a person — people do not invent sentences, they repeat them. It came from a telephone call, or a letter, or a conversation at somebody's kitchen table, or a man in a corridor saying *ask them, they will tell you, there is no record.*" He turned the paper back round. "Your intake field gets a date on it. Not just the instruction — the *day* it was ruled and who ruled it. One date and one name of a person in your own building. You will not find the licensor with that and you will not be able to tell anybody you did. What you will find out is whether anybody in your company has ever been told to leave a field blank on purpose, and if the answer is yes, then somebody was told, and somebody has a memory, and a memory is the only thing in this trade that has never once been falsified, because a company can alter a field and cannot alter the fact that it thought about it."

"How long."

"Three months," said Marek Senn. "Possibly two. And it will be the worst-placed person in your building who remembers it, and you will find them by asking the question wrong. Do not ask who ruled the field. Ask them to tell you about a day they were told a document would go out and something could not be filled in, and then be quiet."

"And when the three suppliers have been in a depot for eight months, do I go back to them and ask who they asked."

"No. Ask all eleven. Ask the four that refused to send anything as well, and ask the three that put a word in it as well, and ask the four that put a name in it. Eleven identical questions with no target on them, on a Tuesday, in a form, and nobody singled out and nobody able to tell who was asked first. And then in three months you will have eleven answers and four of them will be the same sentence again and the seven that are not will be a list of words, and a word is a road and a sentence is a wall, and you want the wall."

Oren Vey wrote two lines on the back of his own hand and read them back and put the folder down on the bench on top of them.

"I have not reported any of this to anybody," he said. "I have not put it in a form. I have had it in a file on my own machine since the week you asked me for it outside a department office, and I have thought about putting it in a form about four times, and every time I have stopped, and I would like to know what to do, because I am the operations director of a company that sells the thing and I am not sure I am allowed to be the person who finds out who wrote it."

"You are not allowed to be the person who finds out. You are allowed to be the person who asks eleven people the same question and then carries the answers to a room where they are printed. That is a different job and it is the one you are actually suited to, and I have known that since the week of week eight and I have not said it out loud until now, and I am saying it now because you have given me a sentence in a corridor and I have had two days to do nothing with it and this is what I have."

"Two," he said, and did not put the pen down, and Marek Senn noticed that and understood that the folder was not the reason he had come. "The job. It is in here and it is real and it is a real wage and I am not going to dress it up, because you would find the dressing in about a week and then nothing else I have said this afternoon would be worth anything."

He turned a sheet round. It was four sides.

**TECHNICIAN — DISTRICT SERVICE, LATTICE. Four days, Monday to Thursday. Grade 2, spine rate plus an on-call allowance. Reporting to W. Dunleavy, district service manager. Start: Monday of week 19. The appointment runs to the end of the department's term unless terminated earlier, and the department's term ends on the last Friday of week 30.**

"Review," said Marek Senn. "What review."

"The one in the sixth of six conditions on a sheet in a file in that building," said Oren Vey. "In person, at the end of the term, before a board that is not the panel, and you attend and you are examined, and I have read it because supply contractors can read that file, and I want you to hear what I think about it before you do the arithmetic, because I have done the arithmetic and it took me about ninety seconds and it is the reason I did not put a start date on this that was nearer."

"The last Friday of week thirty. The coming term starts on the Monday of week nineteen and I have been sent the end date on a form, so I am not working it out, I am reading it." He was looking at the sheet. "So I start on the Monday of week nineteen and I finish on the Friday of week thirty, and the working share expires at five o'clock on the Friday of week thirty, and the trust's conditions come to an end at the same moment, unless they come to an end on Wednesday of week sixteen, which they will, and a man told me that on the Tuesday. Eleven weeks. A company job for eleven weeks, on a rota, with a supervisor at the depot who can put a hand on a practice of mine, and a fourth condition about company premises and company insurance that is not limited by hours, and no share at all on the Friday it stops."

"Yes," said Oren Vey.

"That is not a job. That is an eleven-week window in which I am extremely useful to you and have nothing."

"That is exactly what it is," said Oren Vey, "and I put the end date on the front page in the third line where you would see it before you signed, and I would like you to notice that I could have left it off and that I did not, and that I have not offered you anything to make it longer, because there is not one, because in the new year the whole of this company goes into its budget and I do not know in the eleventh week what the new year looks like, and a man who tells you otherwise is selling you a second page of the same four sides."

He turned the sheet a quarter of an inch.

"And before the money clause, one thing I am going to say and then never refer to again, because you have spent a term watching men in rooms not say things. You minuted a price in that room on the Thursday of week fourteen, in public, and the price was that in about six weeks a department in this city is going to be able to telephone you about a boundary release and you will not be able to tell whether they already know about you. If that telephone comes, and I have read the minute, so I know the words you used, then you will be able to ring this depot and say *my share has lapsed*, and I will say it back to you in a form, and it will be true, and the department will not be able to have you. That is not a favour. It is what a contractor is for, and nobody has ever offered it to you because you are a third-year and until Thursday we did not know what you were."

"That is a reason to take a job."

"It is the only reason I would give you for the money," said Oren Vey. "I am not going to pretend to know the other reasons, and one of them is probably that you have not had a wage since the start of the term, and I am not going to say that one out loud twice."

"And the fifth clause, which I have put in myself and which is not negotiable, is that you take the company's own 4-19 course, both modules, in your first month. It is two days, a Monday and a Tuesday, and it is the course the district has run for eleven years and it is the one on page nine of that pack your courier brought in on Wednesday. **To an employee it is on the clock and there is no fee, because it is a condition of the job. To anybody else it is four hundred and forty pounds for the two days, which is what the district charges and what I have no power to change.** And at the end of it you get a card with your name typed on it, and the card does not expire."

Marek Senn said nothing at all for about eleven seconds, which in a repair shop is a very long time.

"Whose name types it," he said.

"Yours."

"Read the card back to me."

Vey turned another sheet round without a word and pushed it across, and Marek Senn read it, and it was a different card from the Saturday, and it was a better printed one, and it said, in a box, in a small print:

> *This card confirms that the named holder has completed both modules of the 4-19 supervised release course, and that the holder is able to perform and confirm a release where the conditions for release are not within the holder's own judgement. The holder is competent to confirm scope to a learner.*

Marek Senn read the last sentence twice.

"That is a certificate in a lanyard," he said. "No. It is worse than a certificate in a lanyard, because a lanyard is obviously a lanyard. That says the holder is competent to confirm scope to a learner. On the Thursday of week fourteen Davan Rusk was heard as a witness in a room and said, on the record, in his own words, that his own certificate is *the holder is competent to confirm scope to a learner*, and that that is what taught him the completion he supplied to a room full of people on the Tuesday of week fourteen, and that he would have said the word again."

"Yes," said Oren Vey.

"You have put it in your own induction course."

"I have put the existing course in our induction pack. I did not write it. It is eleven years old and it is the sentence that every person in this trade in this city has been taught, and I have never once had anybody point that out to me, and I have been looking at it for about ninety seconds and I would like to go outside."

They both sat there for a minute.

"Marek Senn turned the four sides round and gave them back, and did not say anything at all for long enough that the shop's extractor came on by itself.

"I am not going to say no," he said. "I want you to hear the shape of that, because I have said no nine times in a fortnight and I have got very good at it and a man who is good at saying no is a man you should be careful with. I am not going to say yes either, and the reason I am not going to say yes is on the front of that sheet in the third line, and it is that a thing which stops on the last Friday of week thirty is not a job, it is a gap with a wage in it, and the wage is the reason and the gap is the shape, and the only person in this city who has ever handed me a gap with a wage in it is a woman on a rail who came off three about a year ago."

He put the four sides down on the bench and squared them, which he does.

"Tell me the Friday of week eighteen. Bring it back then and I will read it in the same chair, and if the answer is the same on the Friday of week eighteen I will say yes on the Friday of week eighteen, and you will have had a fortnight to think about whether you want me, and I will have had a fortnight to find out whether I want it, and neither of us will have found out in a room with a man who came from a depot without drinking his coffee."

"That is a fortnight of you not saying yes to a wage."

"That is a fortnight of me finding out what I am agreeing to, and I would rather do it that way, and I am aware it is a habit and I am aware it is the one habit I have that you cannot use, because you have already offered me the thing I have been refusing for a term and I would have taken it in September without asking you a single question, and I do not want to find out what I am like when I do not have a question."

Vey picked the four sides up and squared them, which Marek Senn noticed, and did not comment on.

"The Friday of week eighteen," said Oren Vey. "I will put it in the file as an offer held open and not as a refusal, because it is not one, and because the week after next I have to give the grade-two vacancy to somebody else or lose it, and I am telling you that so that you know the cost of your fortnight is mine and not yours, and I would like it minuted in the file that the holder of the offer chose to delay it."

"Minuted."

"And on the Friday of week eighteen I will bring it back, and there will be nothing different in it."

"Then it will be the same answer," said Marek Senn, "and you will have wasted a fortnight, and I am going to be sorry about that in a way I cannot presently explain to you."

"Don't tell them I said no to the pages."

"There are no pages. I have never seen a page. You have a book and a wall and a woman with a book, and I read about all of it on Friday night in a quarterly with nine hundred addresses on it, and I have not got anything in my folder about any of you, and that is deliberate, and I am aware it is the sort of thing that later looks like evidence."

That was the moment he got the third thing out, and he did it badly, and he knew he was doing it badly.

"Third," he said. "It is not for me to give. I am giving it to you because I am about to be in a position where somebody will ask me about it, and I would rather be able to say where I got it from than make it up in a boardroom in a fortnight."

Marek Senn did not move.

"That is not mine to give. I want that said first. There is a field service engineer in this district who has known me for eleven years, and on the Wednesday morning of this week I told him four words off a stock docket on a telephone and he would not write them down anywhere, and he said *the one thing I have got is not mine*, and he is right and I have not asked him for it again and I am not going to tell you what it is. So I am going to give you the building, and not the box, because a box number is a person's mail and a building is an address, and an address is not a secret, it is on a van."

"Yes."

"The lot came off a van out of the Meridian consolidation point," said Oren Vey. "Those are our own words. It is in our intake summary, which is the document our stores clerk fills in when a lot lands, and it says *Meridian consolidation point, lot onward*, and it is a place and not a supplier, and that is because whoever sold us that lot did not name itself, so the clerk wrote down the first three things she could read off the manifest. I have never been inside that building and neither has anybody who works for me, it is a customer's, we bought a lot from it, and that is the whole of our relationship with it, which is a purchase."

"Marek has got a street."

"Marek has got a street and not a person, and there are about forty other lots that came off the same van." Vey put the folder under his arm. "I have given you a building on a corridor. I would like it understood that that is all it is, and that I have given it to you for free, and that a man who is owed a reply about a blank field has just been given one, and that I would like those two things to be written down separately, because in about a year somebody is going to say I sold him a building, and I did not, I sold him four sides of a job and a sentence off the back of a course card, and the building came out of a piece of paper that was never in my folder."

He stood up and picked up the coffee and drank about a third of it cold.

"Read it," he said. "There is a clause about giving notice and it is a real clause and it is a month, and I would like you to notice that I have put the month in, so that in about four years, when you finally want to leave, you will find that you can."

"One of them is a Monday at ten past six and there are about seven left before the year's out."

"Then keep the Monday," said Oren Vey, "and I will put you on the rota on the Tuesdays and Wednesdays, which is what the depot actually needs, because there is nobody in at seven on a Monday after the twenty-third of the month for a reason I am not going to explain in a shop. And before you thank me for that, I am going to tell you the cost of it, because I have been the man who is pleasant in a corridor for a term and I have watched what it does. **You will be on a yard on a Tuesday morning in a fortnight with a lad called Reuben Daw, and that yard is off the Meridian, and I have had a service engineer look at a card on that yard this week and come back and tell me the card was there when he started in 1998.**"

"How does he know a man in a repair shop sent you to a yard."

"He does not, and I did not tell him, and I did not need to, because a card on a post is a card on a post." Oren Vey put the folder under his arm. "I have read that yard's sheet. I have not met the foreman and I have not met the boy. I have read the sheet and it has a line on it about a release signal and a field for a name that nobody fills in, and I have known about that line for eleven years, and I have never once asked what fills it, and I would like you to notice that I have just said that in a repair shop on a Tuesday afternoon to the only man in this city who could use it, and that the district that writes those cards is a customer of mine."

He got to the door and stopped.

"One more, and then I am going to go and be in a depot. The job is not the interesting part. The job is how I get to be in a building four days a week with a card, a plate and forty men who have all been taught the same thing by people like the woman in your market. The job is a lanyard with my name on it and it will get me through a door that a great many people in this city cannot get through, and I have been thinking about that for about nine seconds and I have decided I am not going to be embarrassed about it, because a door is a door and I am not the worst of the people who want one."

"Read that back to me."

"Take that back to her and tell her a man with a lanyard is not neutral, and tell her a lanyard is not neutral, and tell her to write it on a card in a market in plain language with no logo and no name on it, because that is what she is good at, and I have paid for four hundred courses of it." He opened the door. "Goodbye. It is the Friday of week eighteen and I will be in this shop at about four, and I will bring it with me, and you will say the same thing, and I will take it, and I will not be cross."

He wrote four lines at the bench at about ten past ten that night with the extractor off for once, on the back of a day sheet, and then thought about that, and then wrote them in the book.

*60. Tuesday of week 16. He brought me four sides of paper and a sentence three suppliers wrote down without knowing about each other, and I gave him a way of asking that was the opposite of the way I would have asked it, and he wrote both of them on the back of his own hand and he is going to walk out of that building with it in his pocket and not in a file.*

*How a person stops it.* I said no to a job and he took it as a fortnight rather than a refusal, and I said the reason out loud, and the reason was true, and it was also the best answer available to give a man whose cost is my delay, and I would like somebody to write down that I gave that answer knowing what it was.*

*How many people have been put through it.* Three. Two of them are men who come into this shop and a third of them is a man of forty-one with four hundred courses behind him who has never once asked what fills in a box on a post.*

*Do not know.* Whether I am going to say yes on the Friday of week eighteen. And I have worked out something about that and I am going to put it here because I have nobody to say it to. **If I say yes in week eighteen it will be because a man came into this shop on the Tuesday and did three things well, and not because I have worked out what the job is. And the two of those are not the same, and the second one is the only one of them I could defend to a board at the end of the term, and I have not done the second one, and I have three weeks.*

*And the thing underneath it, which has nothing to do with the job.* A woman of thirty-three came into this shop on the Monday of week fifteen and read one page and said *there is nobody in it who can say no*, and a man read a card on a Saturday of week fifteen and said the words on it out loud, and I have been the fourth thing in four working buildings in six weeks, and I have been handed a lanyard with somebody's name on it that gets through a door a great many people in this city cannot get through, and she is right, and he is right, and they are talking about the same room, and I have been in it, and I was not the one asked to say it.
