# Chapter 57 — The Second Reading

Nobody came for the second one either, and then somebody did.

Marek Senn had the ledger out on the bench at ten to nine on the Monday morning of week sixteen with a pencil on a string, and the shop was shut and the shutters were half up and the light was not properly in, and he did the thing he had done the week before, which was to write the date on the inside cover and read it back about nine times.

Then he did a job for two hours, and came back at about eleven, and Dessa Kwan was standing at the bench.

She had come in her own time, which meant she had come off a shift, and she had a paper bag with a box in it and she put the bag on the bench without taking her hands out of her coat.

"I want a row out."

"All right."

"You haven't asked me which one."

"I did ask you which one about four seconds ago and you didn't say and I didn't ask again, and I want to be careful here, because if I say the wrong one and take it out for the wrong reason that is a worse thing than not taking it out at all." He put the pencil down. "It's yours, isn't it."

"It's mine and it says *D. Kwan, 9 sec, hot pass, tested, no name for the practice*." She said it in a flat voice looking at the middle distance. "And a records clerk of a co-operative has printed it in nine hundred addresses with a ruled empty box over the other half of it, and my name is on it, and I have been in this building four times since the term ended and I did not know that until the Friday when the proof came in the post, and I want it out of your book, and I want the whole row, and I want whatever rule it is that lets me do that without asking a man for anything."

Marek Senn wrote a date at the top of the second page, and a time, which was a new thing and he did not decide to do it.

"Right," he said. "Before I do it, three things, and then I do it and you don't get a vote.

"One. The rule that lets you do it is not mine, it is Bea Nunn's, off the board at the Exchange, and I am going to copy the credit on so that anybody reading this in four years knows where it came from and that the person who took the call stays in. That means my name goes in the fourth column, and I am the person who took the call, and my name is in it, and it stays in when you go.

"Two. The row comes out, not the name. So there will be a date on this page and a time and the fact that a row was removed at the request of a person named in it, and a rule, and my name at the bottom of it, and nothing else. Not your name, not the kitchen, not the nine seconds, not the fact that you were good at it.

"Three. It is not a photocopy and I am not going to keep a copy, and the page is not going anywhere else, and the line underneath it is going to say so in plain words, because otherwise there is going to be a rumour in about a year that a copy of it went into the co-operative's file, and you would be right to believe it."

"Fine."

"And it costs you something and I am not going to pretend it does not." He looked up. "It is the only page in this book that has got three names on it, and the three are you and a boy and a man, and the four of you were named by yourselves and not by me, and one of you in his own hand on a sheet he wrote himself. And Reuben Daw is not on the page. He asked to be a named stopper in the week of week thirteen and there is a name for him in the department’s file, and what he said at the time was *I have got no form*, and I wrote that down, and that is the whole of why he is not on this page, and it is a better reason than yours." He turned the ledger a quarter round. "And when your row comes off there is a page in a book in a shop with two names on it, and the two are a boy and a man, and the third one is the person who could cook a hot pass without talking to anybody, and that person made the coffee in a back room on Saturday morning while a company gave out certificates on the other side of a wall."

Dessa Kwan took her hands out of her pockets and put them flat on the bench, which is a thing people do in repair shops and almost nowhere else.

"I know what it costs," she said. "I've known what it cost on the Saturday when I made about forty cups and watched it. I want it out anyway, and I want you to write down that I knew, so that when somebody comes and asks me why a co-operative's quarterly has got my name in it and a repair shop's book has not, there is a page in the world that says I chose it."

"That's fair."

"It's not fair, it's a fact. Fair is for the other thing." She looked at the page. "Can I read the rule before you do it."

He turned the ledger round and read the rule out, and she read it over his shoulder and did not touch the page, which is not a thing everybody does in a book.

"Right," she said. "Then do it."

He read the whole row out loud in an empty shop — *D. Kwan, 9 sec, hot pass, tested, no name for the practice* — and then put his hand flat over it, which is a child's gesture and was not lost on either of them, and wrote across it in the small upright hand, one stroke, in a pen, not a pencil.

Then he wrote under it, in the same hand: *Row removed at the request of the person named in it, Monday of week 16, 11:20. Rule above. Taker: M. Senn. No copy made and none kept. The row is not written down anywhere else in this city. It was written once, in this book, off what she said in about nine seconds in a market in the week of week thirteen, and it is not on a card, and it is not in a form, and there is no second place to go to and ask for it to be taken out of.*

"You have just written that there is nowhere else to ask," said Dessa Kwan.

"Yes."

"Then you have just made my row permanent, and I would like you to notice that, because I have been in a repair shop for eleven years and nobody has ever made one of my rows permanent by refusing me a copy of it."'''

"That's a good line."

"It is a line," Marek Senn said. "I have got four or five of them and they are all about not making things exist."

She went to the door and stopped with her bag.

"Two things and I am not staying."

"Go on."

"One. There were forty-one people on that list and there were forty in my employer's back room on Saturday morning and one of the forty-one never came, and I have counted both numbers twice and I have made forty cups of coffee and I did it in a kitchen and not in a room, and there is not one thing I can put in the Exchange book about it, because what I would be putting down is that thirty-one people in this city took a Saturday off work to be told by a woman with a lanyard that they are allowed to ask, and I have written that sentence out about nine times since Saturday and every version of it is an accusation, and I have got about two days of not saying anything left."

"Three days, maybe."

"Two." She shifted the bag. "Two. That woman's number is real. Sixty per cent, eleven months, and she said it out loud in a room and did not put the second number away, and I have been furious about that all weekend because she was decent about it and it did not help, and if I had been you I would have gone up to her and I have been waiting since Saturday to find out whether you did."

"No," said Marek Senn.

"Right," said Dessa Kwan. "I will write the thirty-one down in a notebook, which is not a book and does not go anywhere, and if I ever do hand it over I will ring you first and you can say no before it is a thing."

He wrote that down too, with a date, and she watched him do it and did not object, and then she went out into Lattice Ward in her own coat at about twenty past eleven in the morning of the Monday of week sixteen.

---

He rang Bea Nunn at about ten past twelve and told her, and did not ring her to ask her permission, which was a decision he made at about eleven forty and did not write down.

She let it ring twice.

"Abe."

"It's Marek Senn. I want to tell you something and I'm not ringing to ask you for anything, and if you hang up at any point in the next ninety seconds I'll say the rest of it to a engaged tone and that will be fine."

"You've had that one before."

"There's a rule on your board and it is in a book in my shop now with your name on the credit of it, and somebody used it on Monday morning, and the rule says the taker's name stays in, and the taker is me, and your name is on that page as well, because you took the first call in this city into that book in the twelfth week and it is a different building and the rule does not care."

There was a pause of about two seconds, and then Bea Nunn said, "That's not how I wrote it."

"No. It's how it works. And you are on it, and you have not asked to be, and I have not asked you, and I am telling you on a Monday at ten past twelve because you are a person who finds out about things and I would rather you heard it from me than from a woman in a kitchen."

"I see." Another pause. "Then it's on the page and I'm not taking it off, because if I take mine off then in about two years a person will use the book to work out that the rule has a hole in it at the top, and there'll be a person who finds the hole. Is that the only one?"

"That's the only one."

"Right." He heard her put something down. "Then you can tell whoever it is that Bea Nunn's name is in a repair shop in Lattice Ward, and that there is a line in it in his own hand about a man of fifty-six ringing the Exchange in the twelfth week about a number on a wall, and that it says *R. Ancrum*, and that's all it says, and no, I have not read it, and no, I would not take it out if I had."

"It's in her own words. It's the rule. She signed the end of it herself."

"Course she did," said Bea Nunn. "That's her. Listen — the girl from the kitchen. The one that's a member. Has she asked for it, or has somebody asked her?"

"She asked for it. On Monday morning. Standing at the bench."

"Then I withdraw my objection to the whole idea," said the ice merchant, "because I've had that argument with her about three times and I have lost it three times, and she does it for a reason every time and the reason is never the one I'd have used. Right. I've a till. Don't ring me about a book again for about a fortnight, because I will be thinking about it instead of working, and I've two deliveries on the Friday and a man coming about ice."

"I won't."

"You will," said Bea Nunn. "About the eleventh. You always do."

---

The book stayed on the bench, and the Monday reading finished at about ten past twelve, and there were four names in it: the boy, Rafi Pell, and Marek Senn twice, once for taking a row out and once for writing down where a card is in a kitchen.

---

He made the three calls at about half past two on the Monday afternoon of week sixteen, from the bench, with the shutters up and the door shut, and it took eleven minutes in total and it was the worst eleven minutes of his fortnight.

He did it because Bea Nunn's rule is that the name of the person who took the call stays in, and the whole of the point of a rule that works is that it tells you when you have to telephone somebody, and he had spent fourteen weeks refusing to make things exist and had not once worked out that a rule you put in a book obliges *him* to do something.

**Rafi Pell** was on a run and picked up on the second ring and Marek Senn said about four sentences and got to *the rule says the taker's name stays and it is me and it is going to be in the fourth column* and Rafi Pell said, "Stop."

He counted to two out loud, visibly, in a doorway, on a street, in daylight.

"Two," he said. "Go on. Say the rest of it and don't leave me hanging on the bit where you've taken something out of a book." He listened for about half a minute. "Right. So the rule's not just for them, is it. That's what you're actually ringing me about." A door, and a street, and about nine seconds. "You tell Dessa I said she was right and I don't care that she didn't ask me first, because a rule you only use when somebody else isn't in it isn't a rule, it's a favour. And don't you dare ring her and say I said that. You've had a week where nobody's rung anybody, me included, and I'm not going to break it at about twenty to three on a Monday."

He rang Reuben Daw on the yard, in the wind, with the horn cage in the background.

"I'm not asking you about anything," he said, before Reuben had got a word in. "I'm telling you something I did, because there's a rule in a book and I'm one of the four names in it, and the rule says the person who took the call stays in, and a woman came in on Monday morning and asked for her row out and I've taken it out, and there's a date and a time and a stroke across it and my name at the bottom, and no copy anywhere."

A long pause on a windy yard.

"Which row," said Reuben Daw.

"Yours is not the row. Yours is line thirty-eight in a book in a market with Bea Nunn's name in the fourth column, and I had nothing to do with that, and I would have told you I'd had nothing to do with it if there'd been anything to tell you." He put his hand over the receiver for a second, which Reuben could not see and could not have objected to. "It's the cook's. The one from the hot pass. She asked for it herself, on Monday morning, at about eleven, and she made me read her the rule out before I did it."

"And she's not in it now."

"She's not in it now, and she is not in it anywhere else either, because there was never anywhere else, and I have just written that down in a book which is the first time in fourteen weeks that a sentence I have written has made something less findable rather than more, and I would like somebody to notice that it is the first time."

"You could have done that without the date and the time," said Reuben Daw.

"I know. I did it anyway."

"Right," said Reuben Daw, and the wind went quiet for a second, which is what the wind does on that yard at about half past two. "Then that's a Monday-afternoon-in-a-shop thing and not a complaint, and I'd say that to anybody who asked me. I'm not going to say it to anybody who's asked."

He rang the last one last, and it took two of the eleven minutes, and the boy was at home and answered it and Marek Senn had three sentences ready and got through none of them.

"What."

"Wednesday of week fifteen. I took a row out of the ledger in the shop this morning. Somebody's row, not yours. Their own request. There's a date and a time and a stroke and my name at the bottom, and there's no copy and nobody is named in it, and I am ringing you because you are one of the four names in the book and I did not think it was mine to do that without telling you."

There was a long silence, and then:

"Don't ring me about the book," said Tam Arrick. "I don't mind. Ring me about the thing on Monday and Tuesday. That's the only one I've got and it's on a Wednesday of week twenty, so you've got about eleven days." The line went.

He wrote none of the three calls in the load book, and wrote the date of them on the inside cover of the ledger under the line about the first reading, in the small upright hand, with a time, and put the book back on the shelf.

The Monday of week sixteen was the first Monday the world had a date on it. The first reading had nothing in it and he had written that down and Darla Vint had asked for a photograph of the page. The second had a woman in it at eleven o'clock, and a stroke of pen, and a page that was emptier than the one before, and the department did not come, and the trust did not come, and the trust's five conditions had about a day and a half left in them, and there was nobody in this city whose job it was to be here, and there never had been, and there was not going to be one in September either.
