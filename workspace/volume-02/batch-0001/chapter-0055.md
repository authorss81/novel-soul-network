# Chapter 55 — A Place With a Door

The back room of the Saltmarket Exchange had forty people in it at half past ten on the Saturday morning of week fifteen, and about nine of them had come on foot, and there was a woman at the door with a clipboard and a list that had a tick on it, and the tick was not a name.

The card in the tin had gone in on the Wednesday from a woman who ran a depot for somebody else and had the bit of a card on the front of a clipboard that comes off when somebody handles it, and it had said:

> *SAFE RELEASE REFRESHER — TWO HOURS — FREE. Saturday of week 15, back room, Exchange, 10:30. Coffee. Booklet. Certificate at the end. No cost, no catch, no employer asked to sign anything. Bring nothing. All welcome.*

**No employer asked to sign anything** was on the card, in the handwriting of the woman who wrote it, and that is the line that got forty people through a door on a Saturday morning, and Marek Senn stood at the back against the wall and read it four times.

Dessa Kwan came through the back of the room from the kitchen with a tray, because the back room is behind the kitchen and there is no other way into it, and she put the tray down on the table by the door where nobody could get at it, and then stood with her arms folded facing the front for about a second and a half, which is the longest she has ever stood still.

"Forty," she said, out of the side of her mouth.

"Forty."

"There are forty people in a back room on a Saturday morning being given a certificate, and the coffee in there is in this building because I made it, and there is not one man in here who has been told by a woman that he is not allowed to do something on his own." She did not turn round. "I want you to have noticed that."

"I have noticed that."

"Good. I'm going to go back to my kitchen."

---

The woman doing it was called Yvette Kohl and she was forty-six and had a lanyard on and glasses on a chain and she did it, Marek Senn decided afterwards, the way a person does a thing they have done two hundred times, and the reason he decided it afterwards is that from where he was standing he could not tell for the first twenty minutes whether she was good or dangerous, and it turned out that she was both, in that order, and the order was the whole problem.

She did not start with a technique. She started with a photograph on a card, of a man's shoulder in a warehouse, and she asked who had loaded something heavy above their own head height in the last year, and about twenty hands went up, and she said *right, so we are all in the same room*, and she let that sit, and it was not a trick and it was not cheap and it worked.

"Every one of you who put a hand up has made a decision in the last four seconds on a load you did not have all the information about, at a height, alone, with nobody behind you who could see your face. That is not a criticism. That is the job. I am not here to tell you that you are doing it wrong." She put the card down. "I am here because we have spent longer putting somebody else in that four seconds than I would have believed, and it has been the best of my working life, and there is a number behind it, and the number is the reason I got the last two budgets, and I am not going to read it out in a room like this because I have watched what a number does to a room and it is not what people think it does."

There was a noise in the room that was not quite applause.

"Nobody has ever read us a number before," somebody said at the back.

"I know," said Yvette Kohl. "That is not a criticism of you either. That is a criticism of a whole industry and I am in it."

Marek Senn was against the wall at the back with a booklet he had picked up off the table on the way in, and he had it open at page nine before she got to the box.

---

The technique took forty minutes and it was good. That was the part he had not been ready for. She was quick, she did not over-explain, she used the word *load* for the thing and *hand* for the part of it that knows, and she made nine people do it in pairs and then made them do it alone, and when a man of about fifty got it wrong twice she did not correct him; she said *you looked at him before you decided. Do it again and do not*, and he did it again and got it right and looked at nobody.

That was it. That was the hinge, and it took forty minutes, and she was not doing it on purpose any more than Davan Rusk had been on the Tuesday of week fourteen, and Marek Senn stood at the back of a room full of people and watched a competent woman in a lanyard teach eight years of somebody else's design to forty people on a Saturday morning, and could not get a single word out of his mouth, and knew exactly what he was looking at, and had no way of saying it that would not sound like a man who had failed an examination in a room with forty people in it.

The certificate was the thing that undid him.

They came at the end of the second hour, most of them, on card, A6, printed, with a name typed on each one by a woman with a typewriter, and the woman's name was called out to the room as well, which is the part he could not stop thinking about afterwards. It said:

> **SAFE RELEASE — COMPLETED. Two hours. Delivered by Y. Kohl, district safety and training, Threadline. Issued to: ____________**
>
> *This card confirms that the named holder has completed a supervised refresher in the safe release of a load at height, and that the holder is able to perform and confirm a release where the conditions for release are not within the holder's own judgement.*
>
> *No expiry. No assessment. No fee.*

He read it four times standing up.

"Ask her," he said.

Rafi Pell was beside him. He had been in the room the whole time and had not said eleven words to Marek Senn, and had a certificate in his hand in a plastic sleeve, because he had asked the woman for one and had been given one and had put it in a sleeve.

"Ask her what."

"The card." Marek Senn pointed at the bottom of it. "That bit. *Where the conditions for release are not within the holder's own judgement.*"

"I know what it says, I've read it, I've been reading it since about ten past twelve."

"That's the third beat," said Marek Senn. "That's not a safety instruction. In a card, the third beat is you hand the decision back to the thing that is carrying the load. That sentence is saying: hand the decision back to a person. And that is the design, and it is the design in about nine hundred schools in this country, and this is the first time I have seen it printed on something a man can put in his coat pocket."

Rafi Pell looked at the card in his sleeve for the length of a breath.

"Right," he said. "How do I say that to her."

"You do not say it to her."

"Why not."

"Because she has got forty people in a room who took a Saturday to be told they are not stupid, and I have no licence, and four people who can end anything I say, and if I stand up in this room and say the word *hinge* she will ask me what a hinge is and I will have to explain it, and I will do it badly, and she is a better teacher than I will be in a car park, and she will go home tonight and think a twenty-two-year-old ruined her Saturday." He put the booklet back on the table. "And because I could be wrong. I have been wrong in a room before and there was a rail involved."

"You think you're wrong."

"I think I am right and I think I have got about four per cent of it, and the four per cent is not a reason to stand up in a room with forty people in it," said Marek Senn. "That's the honest position and I have never had it before and I did not expect to have it at a quarter to one on a Saturday."

"Four per cent," said Rafi Pell. "That's a figure."

"It's a figure about me, not about her."

"Yeah, well." Rafi put the sleeve in his coat. "I'm keeping the card."

"I know you are. I would keep it too. I am not going to tell you not to and if anybody else tells you not to you will do the opposite and that has been established."

---

Kohl came past him on the way out with a box of booklets under her arm and stopped, because he was standing at the back and a woman with a box has to get past a man with a booklet.

"Sorry. Are you waiting for a certificate? There's another six on the desk, they're the ones I didn't get to."

"No. Thank you."

"You've been standing at the back for two hours." She shifted the box. "You don't have to say anything about that, I get it, it's somebody's mum."

"It's a man who failed an exam in this building in September."

"Then you're in the right room," said Yvette Kohl, and put the box down on the table, which she did not have to do, and looked at him properly for the first time. "Do you want the number?"

"Yes."

"I'm not going to give you the number."

"You did an hour and a half of teaching and you won't give me the number."

"The number is not the point and that is exactly the thing about the number." She put her hands on the table, on the box she had just put down. "Every figure I have ever been given on that is real. Every one. And I have watched four different rooms full of competent people stop asking a question because a number came out of my mouth, and the number was true, and the question did not go away, it just went quiet and sat in the corner and waited for the next number. So I give it out in rooms where somebody is writing it down, and the woman from the clipboard out there is not writing it down, and you have not asked me a single question, and you have been standing at that wall for two hours."

"I've asked you nothing at all."

"I know. It's the other thing that is unusual." She picked the box up. "I'll tell you one thing for nothing, because you'll work it out in about a month and I'd rather you heard it in a Saturday. If you do this, you do it for the number, and the number will be for a room somewhere with a floor and a projector, and it will be the best two hours of my month and I will be pleased about it. And the thing that will happen is not in a room you will be in either. It is that somebody who has been through a Saturday like this will stand at the top of some steps one day and wait for a voice, and the reason he is waiting is that this worked, and nobody will ever connect it back to a Saturday with coffee in it."

"Then why are you here."

"Because forty men took a Saturday off work to be told they were not stupid," said Yvette Kohl, "and the alternative is that they find out on a Monday, in a yard, on their own, and I have been on both sides of that and I know which one of them costs."

She went out through the kitchen and Dessa Kwan was standing in the doorway of it with a tea towel over her shoulder, and they did not speak to each other, and the woman from the clipboard outside said *forty-one, one left* to nobody in particular, and put the tick on the list, and the tick was not a name.

---

Dessa Kwan was behind the counter of a shop on a corner in Lattice Ward at about half past six on the Saturday evening with a box of washers in a carrier bag, and she made him say what it was for before she answered him, which is a thing she has done since the week of the fourteenth, and which is not a rudeness.

"You gave forty people a Saturday."

"I gave forty people a Saturday," said Marek Senn, "and I stood at the back of it and said nothing, and you made the coffee."

"I made the coffee and I let her use the room, and I want the second one on your book and not the first one, because the first one is the one that will be in people's heads in about four years and it is the wrong one." She put the bag down and did not take her hands out of her coat. "Do you know what the room is."

"It's a back room behind a kitchen with a door and about forty chairs in it and no windows."

"It's a back room behind a kitchen that four hundred people a week walk past to get to a kettle," said Dessa Kwan. "It is not neutral, it is not a room somebody lent us, and nobody voted, because there is nothing here to vote about — I have a key to it, Bea has a key to it, and the truth is that about nine people in this building could let a company put a table in it on a Saturday and none of them would call a meeting about it. That is not corruption. That is what a room is when the people who can open it are all busy and honest."

"And the certificate."

"That is the part I cannot get past and I have been getting past it since about half past twelve." She turned round and put both hands on the counter, which she does when she is working out a sentence. "Thirty-eight of them walked out of that room with a card with their name typed on it, and about nine of them do not have a job, and four of the nine are on this side of the river. And the card is free, and the Saturday is free, and there is no cost, and that is the whole design of it and there is not one dishonest person in the room. **And it is for them, and not for the nine.** In about six months there will be a woman on a yard who is going to get hurt, and somebody will say to her employer *did you do the release course*, and the answer will be yes, and the woman will believe it, and the thing that got her hurt will be the thing that got her card, and no company in this city will ever be asked about it by anybody, because the card is evidence that she was safe."

She stopped, and then she said the true thing, which is the thing she had come to say.

"You asked her about the word."

"How do you know I asked her about the word."

"Because I was in the doorway at the end and I heard you ask and I heard her not answer, and I have been trying since half past twelve to work out whether I should have said something in that room, and I have decided that I would not have, and that is the part I came here to say." She picked the bag up. "I would not have, because I have got no fourth beat either, and I have got eleven words and none of them is the word, and if I stand up in a room of forty people and tell them that the whole of it is a word, then in about a year somebody writes it down and it is *the cook says it is a word*, and it is a story about a cook, and a story about a cook is the easiest thing in the world to file."

"Are you saying you want me to say it."

"I am saying you would not be believed and you would be the man who said it, and a man who said it and was not believed is worth about a third of what I am worth, and I have got a good room." She got to the door. "And I am not being kind and I am not asking you for anything, and if you write any of this down anywhere, write that I told you the kettle was the room, because that is the true bit and the rest of it is just me being right."

---

He did not go up the hill that evening. He sat on the wall at the top of the Weir road at about ten past eight with the load book shut on his knee, and he worked out what he had seen, and then he made himself write it down, in a way that could not be repeated in a room, and then he sat there for another forty minutes.

*60. Saturday of week 15, and this one I wrote on a wall off the Weir road because it would not sit down anywhere else.*

*It is the same design.*

*It is in a card in a school with an instructor in it and in a card on a Saturday with a supervisor in it, and the third beat is the same third beat, and the fourth beat is the same fourth beat, and the eleven-page booklet's page four is the same sentence as page nine of a Saturday refresher in a room the Exchange's kitchen made the coffee for.*

*I cannot name it in a room. There is a word for it and it is not a word anybody outside this case uses, and it is the wrong word for it anyway, because it is not a person. It is a shape. The shape is: somebody stands at the front of a hard thing and completes it with their voice, and the hard thing gets easier each time, and the getting-easier is the whole product, and the product is sold in a laminated card to a boy of sixteen and in a printed one to a man of forty.*

*And the woman is good at it. That is the part I have been not writing for two hours. She was not doing it on purpose, any more than a man in a practical department said a word out of habit on the Tuesday of week fourteen, and the men in that room would have gone anywhere she asked them to go, and every one of them has a load he does not have all the information about and somebody else's hand on the decision, and they came out of that room lighter than they went in. I have been sitting on a wall for an hour trying to find the sentence where those two things stop being possible and I cannot find it, and I have got a feeling the sentence is going to be about a yard off the Meridian and not about this room, and I have got a worse feeling that the sentence is going to turn out to be about me.*

He shut the book and did not go up the hill and did not telephone anybody.

In the laundrette under his own bedroom, a woman of sixty-eight with a plate on her right wrist for three years was sitting at her till at about twenty past ten with a kettle going, and nobody came up those stairs, and he had not been up them since the Sunday of week twelve.
