# Chapter 54 — Withheld Pending Notice

Talia Venn's answer was on the Friday morning of week fifteen and she gave it in a room on the first floor of a building in Bower Street to a man whose title was *continuity and returns*, and Marek Senn was not in that room and has never been in that building and has never seen the inside of it, and there is a reason for that which she put in a car park eight days ago in four sentences and one of them was *not to be helpful*.

She telephoned him at about ten past one from the corridor outside it and she said four things in the order she had said them in the room, which is a thing she does.

"Two hundred and six. Sixty-one of them are mine. And I have used a word they did not want, and they are going to make me use the other one, and I have got about four weeks before the second letter."

"Which word."

"*Refusal.* That is the word on the form. If I write *refusal* then the file says a nurse declined to produce records on request, and that is a thing a file can do, and the file can then be sent to somebody, and somebody can then say *she declined*, and declined is a word a person is. Withheld pending notice is a word a file cannot do anything with, because it has a date in it and an intention, and it says I will come back with something and not that I have decided to keep it." She was walking; he could hear the echo. "I said the true thing, which is that about two thirds of what he has asked for is other people's, and that producing them is how they become his, and that a page in a drawer is mine and a page in a file is theirs and the difference is about nine seconds long. And he wrote it down. He wrote all of it down, which I did not expect, and then he said, and I am giving you this word for word because it is the whole of the problem in one sentence — he said: *Miss Venn, I am not asking you to break a confidence. I am asking you to build a machine that can do it without you.*"

"And you said no."

"I said no to the machine." A door, somewhere, and then a street. "I want to be exact, because you will get this wrong in about a week and then you will not let go of it. I said no to a mechanism for extracting pages from people who do not know they are in them. I said yes to a count. I gave him a number per month, on a piece of paper, and I said I would give him the number for as long as the number is the question, and I said the day he asks me for anything else I will give him the same sentence in writing. So there is now a document in Bower Street with a number on it and no name on it, and he is going to keep it, and in about two years it is going to be the reason somebody thinks he has my practice, because a number that was never wrong is very difficult to disbelieve."

"He is not a bad man."

"He is not a bad man and that is why it is going to be difficult," said Talia Venn. "Marek, listen. In four weeks he is going to come back with a request for the count to be evidenced, and I am going to have to say no to that as well, and I am telling you now so that when it happens you are not surprised and you do not turn up. If you turn up I will not be able to do it, and I have given you that sentence for nothing if I give it to you for a thing you were going to do anyway."

"Understood."

"Say it back."

"You will be asked for the count to be evidenced and you will say no again, and I am not going to turn up."

"Good. Now the other thing." Her voice changed about a quarter of an inch, which after thirteen weeks he could hear and could not explain to anybody. "I want a line out of the ledger."

---

He did not say anything for about four seconds, and the shop was empty, and it was one o'clock in the afternoon on a Friday in the fourth week out of term.

"Which line."

"The R. Ancrum line. The one in the twelfth week, on the Tuesday, when a man of fifty-six rang the Exchange about a number on a wall." She was not walking now. "It is in a book on a nail in a market and it is in his own words and it ends with his name and it is the only place in this city where a man who works Mondays has written down in his own hand that he asked a question about a college drill and was told no. And on the Wednesday of week seventeen a woman from the district is going to ring me and ask me for a statement about the practice on the round, and I am going to have to say that I have not seen a round, and that is not true, and I was on Fell Road on the Friday of week twelve and I asked three people and one of them is fifty-six and works Mondays."

"You want the row out of Bea's book."

"I want the row out and the fourth column kept," said Talia Venn. "I read her rule in a lay-by on a Saturday and I have not been in that building. The whole row comes off and the taker's name stays in, and then the book says that somebody came in on the Friday of week fifteen and asked for that row to come off, and it does not say whose row it was and it does not say what was on it, and I can then say no to the district honestly, and I can say it without lying, and I can say it because there is nothing in the book to give them."

He looked at the shelf, where the book was, with the shop's name on the front and her name on the inside of the front cover and the page drying under a paperback that he had taken off it on the Monday.

"No," he said.

"Marek."

"No. And I want to give you the reason, because you have been decent about reasons all term." He put the phone against his forehead for a second, which she could not see and could probably hear. "You are asking the wrong man, and that is the half of it that is your fault and not mine. It is not my book. It is Bea Nunn's, and she wrote the rule, and she has never once asked me about a line in it, and if I walked in there on Friday and asked her to take out a row she wrote in her own hand, then in about four years there is a book in a market with a hole in it and a column of names of people who asked for holes, and that is exactly the machine you have been telling me about in a lay-by for a fortnight."

"That is not an argument against the rule."

"It is an argument against me asking," he said. "So ask her. You can go in on any morning she is in there and you can say the sentence out loud in four minutes and she will say noted in the tone she says it, and if she says no then it is a no from the person who owns it and not a no from a repairer in Lattice Ward, and I will not be one inch disappointed in you, and I will say that to somebody one day."

"That is the second time this afternoon you have told me to do it myself."

"It is."

"Then here is the other half, and this is the part you have been waiting fourteen weeks for and you are not going to like it, and I am not going to be gentle about it because you have never once been able to do anything with a gentle version of me." He turned the book over on the bench, which she could not see. "In about three weeks' time, Corvin Ashby is going to write a report about the Weir, because he has to write one every season and he has written one every season for nine years. And the first line of it is going to be a decision about whether this round is *informal* or *established*, and he will write whatever keeps the pump money coming, and I have heard him say so to me twice in a lane in nineteen seconds each time, and he was right both times. **And if a man of fifty-six has been removed out of a book in a market because somebody on a telephone was frightened of a district office, then Ashby's report is the reason it happened, and he will never know, and I will.** That is what a removal is. Nobody puts a name on it and everybody is affected by it, and it is not a rule any more, it is a lever, and the whole reason Bea Nunn's is the only rule in this city a person can use without first finding out whose job it is, is that she built it to be one-handed and not a lever. So no, and ask her yourself, and if she says yes I will drive you."

There was a pause on a telephone between a shop in Lattice Ward and a first-floor corridor in Bower Street.

"Right," said Talia Venn. "Right. And I have asked three people in thirteen weeks to take a thing out of a record and all three of them have said no to me in a way I could argue with, and this is the first one where I have been told that the question is badly put rather than that the answer is no."

"It is badly put."

"It is badly put," she said. "And I have been asking it badly since about the seventh week and I did not know, and you have now told me in a way I am going to have to think about for about a fortnight, and I am going to think about it on a Thursday and not before, and I am not going to tell you that, and you are not going to ask."

"I was not going to."

"It is already a thing between us," she said. "That is what I have learned in fourteen weeks and I have not said it out loud to anybody, including you, until this minute."

---

There was about a minute of line noise on the connection from a first floor in Bower Street to a shop in Lattice Ward.

"Now the other half of it, and then I am going to be somewhere else." Her tone came back up, which was the professional one and the one she used when the sentence coming was the hard one. "I need a page."

"No."

"That was fast."

"You did not tell me what it was for, and you have never once asked me for a thing in thirteen weeks, and I would have said yes to a page." He was aware of how that sounded and left it. "So. What is it for."

"Rhoda Ilesanmi. Not Rowan. Nineteen. First year, maintenance track, the forearm." She did it in one breath, the way you do a thing you have done a hundred times. "She had a review yesterday, in a clinic, four stitches out on the ninth day, and there is a district return to complete, and Roisin Keogh has read the return and telephoned the woman who will read it back, in advance, which she told us she would do in a corridor on the Wednesday. And the woman who has to sign her off has asked, in writing, in a form, whether the candidate completed a practical under the supervision of a licensed holder during the assessment on the Tuesday of week fourteen. And there was no licensed holder. There was a door, a key, a determination, a panel and a repair crew, and Rusk said a word out of habit on the Tuesday morning and a rail went across her arm, and there is no name in that a form can take."

"You want the ledger page for the Tuesday."

"I want the page of the ledger for the Monday of week fifteen," said Talia Venn, "which has three names on it and a boy's handwriting gummed in at the top, and I want it because a form is asking a nineteen-year-old whether a man was standing near her and whether she would let him, and she is going to have to answer that about a room she was hurt in, and I would like her to be able to say that there was a book, and that a page came out of it, and that a woman of twenty-four asked for it on the Friday, and a man in a shop read it to her over a telephone and did not keep a copy."

"You want it to be a document."

"I want it to be a thing that exists," said Talia Venn, "and you have spent a term refusing to make things exist that do not exist, and you are going to tell me that is the same principle, and it is not, and I would like you to notice that I know it is not and that I am asking anyway, because you have been the man who notices things since the sixth week and I am tired."

He stood at the bench for about a minute.

"No," he said. "Same principle. Exactly the same principle, and you know it is the same principle, and you asked me anyway, and I am not going to be angry about it and I am not going to be one of the people who says *for you I would*."

"Fine."

"For the record, if the form gets to the point where nobody can answer it, tell me and I will come and stand in the room and say the true sentence in it, and I will be a bad witness, and I will do that. That is not a page and it is not a copy and there will not be one afterwards. That is the offer and it is the only one I have got."

There was a pause.

"That is the best thing anybody has offered me in thirteen weeks," said Talia Venn. "Including you, on the Saturday, in a lay-by, with the light gone, and I am not going to say the rest of it and you are not going to write it down."

He did not write it down.

---

That night he wrote the load book, and it was a full page with four headings on it, and it is the longest page he has written since the Wednesday of week thirteen at two in the morning.

*59. Friday of week 15. What happened today that I am not going to forget.*

*What touched it.* Talia Venn asked me for a row out of another woman's book and I said no, and she asked me for a page and I said no, and both times she was right to ask and I was right to say no, and I have written both halves down because I have noticed in fourteen weeks that when I only write the half I like, the page lies by Wednesday.

*How a person stops it.* A man stops me by writing a page. That is the whole mechanism and it has been the whole mechanism since the Thursday of week one and I did not have the words for it until this afternoon at twenty past one.

*How many people have been put through it.* Two. One of them is a woman of twenty-four who has just been told by a man in a repair shop that she has been asking the wrong question since the seventh week. One of them is a man of forty-five who has twenty-two years in a room and has never been asked for anything.

*Do not know.* Whether telling somebody no for the right reason is the same as telling them no because I am afraid of what happens if I do not. I have written in this book since week four that the answer is always the first one. I have not been able to test it once. She asked me for a page and I said no in under two seconds, and two seconds is not long enough to be a principle. Two seconds is long enough to be a door I do not want opened.*

*And the thing under that, which is the reason I am writing a page at ten o'clock on a Friday instead of doing two jobs.* In fourteen weeks I have built one institution, which is a book on a bench with three names in it, and it is on the wall because a woman from a trust put it there, and on the Wednesday of week sixteen that woman has told me it comes off the wall. So the book is about to lose the only reason anybody could give for reading it, in order for me to keep the one thing I have refused nine times, and the two of those are on opposite sides of the same door, and I have known that since about ten past one this afternoon, and I have not written to anybody about it, and I have written it here, which is a book nobody reads, which is the fourth thing in a row this term that I have done.
