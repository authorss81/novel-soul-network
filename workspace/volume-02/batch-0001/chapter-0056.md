# Chapter 56 — Deliveries, North, Awaiting, Signature

Reuben Daw telephoned the shop at about ten past ten on the Sunday morning of week fifteen and Marek Senn said, before Reuben had got to the second word, "Are you coming to the shop or do you want me to come to a yard."

"How do you know that."

"Because you have never once telephoned me on a Sunday and because on Friday you said the word *college* came up in a doorway and that the thing about a doorway is that you cannot get anybody to write it down. So either you've found somewhere it can be written down, or you want to show me something." He put the extractor down. "And I'm not being clever. I'm asking because I don't want to be the man who turns up."

"Pull-off off the Meridian," said Reuben Daw. "The big one, past the grain terminal where the road bends. Yard office with a kettle in it and a door that opens at six, and about forty of us on there Monday to Thursday."

"And a Sunday?"

"There's four of us who went on the Sunday," said Reuben Daw, "because that's the only day a man is not doing something else, and I did not want to take a working day off to stand in a wind looking at a card, and I'd like that on the record before you ask me why it's four and not forty."

"Thursday?"

"There's a man coming Thursday for the horn."

"Aurel Kosta's coming?"

"How do you know that."

"He stood in the doorway of this shop in the ninth week for twenty minutes and would not come in, and he has been on that yard for thirty-one years, and he knows what is on that post better than anybody in this city, and I have been sitting here since Wednesday trying to think of a second person who would." Marek Senn put the extractor down. "Is he on today."

"He's on today. He's the one with the hose."

---

The yard was two hundred yards long with four sets of steps up to a wagon and a control post at the top of the middle one with a plastic horn in a cage on the wall, and a blue card bent at the corner on the post, and Marek Senn read it standing up, in the wind, with Aurel Kosta working a hose four feet behind him.

The card had the district's mark on it and it said **LOAD MOVEMENT — SIGNALLED RELEASE — SUPERVISED**, and underneath, in a small print he had to put his glasses on for, it said *the named holder will not proceed until a supervisor has given the release signal*.

The corner was bent about an inch and a half from the bottom, and it had been bent a very long time, because the crease had gone white on both sides and the printing had cracked across it.

"That is my card," said Aurel Kosta, over the hose, without turning round. "That is not a new card. Nobody in this building is going to tell you how old that is, because I have never once asked and I am not going to start on a Sunday. It was on that post when I started. It was bent when I started. I have driven a shunter for twenty-six years and I have been on that yard thirty-one and nobody has ever once told me how to read a card, because you do not need to be told, you are a grown man on a wagon."

"Somebody would have had to write it."

"Somebody did, once, a long time ago, in an office, with a typewriter." Kosta shut the hose off. "And there is a copy of it in the office on the wall next to the kettle and it is older than the one on the post, and it is not a district document and it is not a school document, and I have never asked where it came from, and that is the last sentence I am going to say about it in my life."

"You do not have to say that."

"No," said Aurel Kosta. "I know I do not. That is why I said it."

Reuben Daw had been standing on the top step with his hands in his pockets the whole time and had not interrupted, which is a thing about a man who has been on a yard for thirty-one years: he can hear four sentences in and know whether they are going to be trouble.

Marek Senn looked at the card for a while.

"It says the holder waits for a supervisor's signal. Is that a district rule, or a yard rule."

"It is *that's the card*," said Aurel Kosta. "That is the phrase. Thirty-one years and there is about forty men on that yard and I could not tell you one that does not do it that way, and the new ones ask about it, and the answer is *that's the card*." He wound the hose onto the standpipe. "And there is no school in that. I want that said. That is a yard thing and a district thing, and it is not one of your college things, and if you go in there and say it came out of a college you will be wrong, and every man on that yard will know you are wrong inside a week, because we all know the difference."

"I am not going to say that."

"Good," said Aurel Kosta, "because my mate Vic went to that college. Twenty-two, thirty years ago, technical side, a fitter. And Vic does not have a horn, because Vic did the old way, and Vic's way was he said it out loud to himself and he took his hands off, and Vic has never once in his life stood on a wagon waiting for anybody in a control post, and Vic is a stationmaster now."

They stood in the wind for a minute.

"Vic said it out loud in front of anybody, or on his own."

"Every time. On the wagon. Out loud, to himself, and I used to think he was talking to me, and he was not — he was telling the load what had happened to it, and I have never had to wait for a horn in my life, because I have a Vic in my ear and I do what he did." Kosta picked the hose up. "And I am fifty-eight, and the thing I am telling you is that a man of fifty-eight who has been on a yard since before most of your fourth beats were printed can tell you that the old way is not as safe. It is not. And the new way has a very good record for a reason, and both of those are true, and you are going to have to write something that holds both of them, or you will be doing what everybody else does, which is pick one and shout it."

"You have thought about that before today."

"I have thought about it since about half past ten this morning," said Aurel Kosta, "which is when two men from a college turned up on a yard on a Sunday and one of them asked me about a card, and that is not a thing that is going to happen to me twice."

They stood in the wind at the top of the middle set of steps for a while.

There were about forty men on that yard and every one of them was doing something. Two were lashing a load on a wagon. Four were on the top set, working a shutter. A man of about sixty was washing his hands at a standpipe and had been, by the state of the puddle, for some time. And a boy of about nineteen with a hi-vis a size too big in him was standing at the control post with a clipboard he was not writing on, and had been standing there since Marek Senn arrived, and did not move, and looked at the top of the horn and then at his feet.

"Which one's the boy," said Marek Senn.

"Him," said Reuben Daw. "Nine weeks. He does not ask anybody anything, he's not allowed, and on the Tuesday he went up on the top set when a load came over and he's the one who held the shutter while the other bloke got round, and he held it and the whole time he was looking up at the post to see if anybody said anything, and nobody said anything, and the other bloke came round and they got it done and went home." He was not looking at Marek. "And then on the Wednesday they put a card up on that post and told us that's the new one, and the boy's been different since. He's not stood at that post the same way. He stands there like he's waiting to be told he's allowed to have been on that set of steps."

Marek Senn looked at the boy for about a minute and did not go over.

"Somebody in 2019 went over your shoulder up there."

Kosta stopped with the hose in his hand.

"Aye," he said. "Third wagon. Shunt went in crooked and I had a bad one for two years and I have never been right since and I never went to anybody, because you don't, because you're on the Saturday list and if you go to anybody about a shoulder you come off the list." He wound the hose slower. "And there is no form for it anywhere. I've been on that yard thirty-one years and I have never seen one, and there is nobody in this city that has ever asked me, and I have had four drinks' worth of thinking time about why that is, and here is the answer and it is not clever: **if it is not written down then it did not happen, and that is fine, right up until the day somebody wants it to have happened, and then it did not, and there is nothing to argue with.**"

"That is not a rule anybody agreed to."

"No," said Aurel Kosta. "It is a rule that agreed to itself, and it is in a shed, and it has been in a shed for longer than your college has had a fourth beat."

"But the office has got a report."

"Now that," said Kosta, "is where I've been for eleven weeks and that is what I said to the young man here, and it is the only time in my life I have said a thing like it out loud to a stranger. A foreman's report went in that year. It is a form, it got a number off the district, it says *manual handling*, and it is in a drawer in an office in this city with a number on it." He looked at the ground. "So a shoulder went in the front of the office and a shoulder went in the front of a box, and the one in the office is a piece of paper about nothing, and the one in the box is a piece of A4 with a man's name on it, and neither of them says a fella of fifty-eight got hurt on a wagon, because the first one does not and the second one never got a number and it is in a box in the office with the tea bags in it. That is the whole of it. And I have had eleven weeks to work that out and I have not told anybody, because the second I ask about two things I am a man with a notebook, and then I am a man with a complaint, and then I am the man that comes and asks you things." He picked the hose up. "So I have asked about one thing, and it was that, and every man on that yard knows I asked about one thing and what it was."

Reuben Daw had not said anything at all through any of it, which was the correct thing for a man of twenty-two to do standing next to a man of fifty-eight who has been keeping a sentence in a shed for eleven weeks.

A lorry came through the gate and the man at the control post moved out of its way and then came back and stood in exactly the same place.

"Can I ask you one thing," Marek Senn said. "And it's about a card, and I am asking because it is the only card in this city I would like to see and I have no right to see it."

"If you've got a way of getting it, no."

"I have a way of getting it that involves a man with a clipboard on a Saturday and a book at a market, and I am not going to use it, and I would like that on the record." He put his hands in his pockets. "Is the name on the new one."

"Which new one."

"Page nine. In the pack in your office. Is there a space for a name on the thing the supervisor gives you, or is it just a signal."

Reuben Daw thought about it for a bit.

"Signal," he said. "You get your hand up, and the fella on the radio says it, and that's it. That's why I want you to stand up there, because the boy's been getting told he's waiting for a voice and he's been waiting for a voice that isn't there, and if a man from a college stood up there and said *there's nobody coming, you're the one who's there, go back to work* — "

"No," said Marek Senn.

"You keep doing that."

"I know what I'm doing. Listen to me for a minute, because this is the only useful thing I've got and then I'll go and get the bus." He was looking at the boy at the post, and not at Reuben Daw, which was the correct way round and he knew it. "You said the thing that gets anybody hurt is that the record goes in front. It doesn't. In 2019 a lad wrote a piece of A4 with a pen and put it in a box and it is still in a box in an office with the tea bags in it, and nobody hid it, and nobody read it, and that is the best possible outcome for that lad and it is still a waste."

"Then what do you do with a man in a box with a piece of paper in it."

"Nothing," said Marek Senn. "That's the answer and I have hated it for four days. I want you to write your own line in the book at the Exchange and I want you to sign the end of it, and that is all I am asking anybody to do in this city and I have been asking for it since the Wednesday of week thirteen. Then there's a row in a book in a market that says a man stood at the top of those steps on the Sunday of week fifteen and that a boy of nine weeks was stood at a post waiting for a voice that isn't his. That's it. No form number. No claim. No person named who's not got a name in it already."

"He'll have to write it himself."

"I know. That's why it has to be in his words and I have to be nowhere near it. If I put a hand on it, it's a document with a man's initials on it and it is a document about a college, and the second that happens the yard reads it as a college being on their round. It took me four days to work that out and Corvin Ashby worked it out in nineteen seconds and he has still said no twice."

Reuben Daw took a small notebook out of his coat and looked at it and shut it again.

"He'll want to know what it's for."

"He won't ask. He's a boy of nine weeks and nobody has ever given him a reason for anything."

"He'll ask," said Reuben Daw. "They always ask."

They put it in on the Thursday morning of week fifteen at about ten past eight, in the back room of the Exchange, with Bea Nunn in the chair and no meeting, because the tin is not a meeting and the chair takes cards in the back room on any morning she happens to be in there, which is why that book has thirty-eight lines and there have only ever been about twenty people in that room.

It is line thirty-eight.

Reuben Daw wrote it himself, in block capitals, and signed the end of it in capitals, and Bea Nunn wrote the taker's name in the fourth column and her own at the end of it, and the rule is the rule: *the caller writes their own words, in the first person, and signs the end of it, and the taker's name is recorded separately as the taker.*

Bea Nunn read it twice, all the way through, in front of eleven people, which she does not do with the short ones, and then said "noted" in the tone of a woman noting a change in the weather, and then said, "Nobody take that card out of the book."

Which is not a rule Bea Nunn has ever stated, and about four of the eleven people noticed that, and Reuben Daw did not, and Marek Senn was not in the room and did not hear it until the Wednesday.
