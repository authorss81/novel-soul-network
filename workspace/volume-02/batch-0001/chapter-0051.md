# Chapter 51 — What a Failed Condition Is

He had written it down in the load book, in the small upright hand he wrote tags in, on the Thursday of week fourteen, at about twenty to one in the afternoon, in a corridor on the second floor, about twenty minutes after a woman had told him in plain words exactly what was going to happen about the drawer. *Have not asked one person what a failed condition does.* And then he had written it down again every morning since, which was five of them, the way you touch a tooth.

The trust's office was on the first floor of a building on Cutter Street with a brass plate and a lift that worked, and the woman at the desk on the Tuesday morning of week fifteen was about fifty and offered him a chair before he had got his coat off, which meant somebody had telephoned her.

Anders Kolt came out in a shirt with no jacket and read the page of his own conditions that Marek Senn had not brought and did not need to bring, because he had it memorised, which was its own kind of stupid.

"You have finally asked," he said.

"You told a woman in a corridor what was going to happen about it on the Thursday and I have not asked one person. That is not the same as finally."

"It is not the same at all. Sit down. Do you want tea? I am not going to pretend I did not know you were coming, because I did know, and because I would have said no to this appointment if you had not asked for it, and the reason I would have said no is that I have thought about this and I have decided that the worst thing I could do to you in week sixteen is not know the answer." He sat down on the other side of the desk, which is not what a man in his position does. "So. What do you think a failed condition does."

"I think you stop paying for something."

"You think I pay for something." Kolt said it without any inflection at all. "Marek. The trust does not pay your living. I do not know who pays your living. I have never known who pays your living and I have never asked, because it is not the trust's business and because a person who has told a grant-making body what he earns is a person who has told a grant-making body what he earns." He turned a sheet round. "The trust gave you a bench slot, two days a week, paid at the shop rate, for one term. That is four hundred and eighty pounds across twelve weeks and it is the only money the trust has ever given you and it stops at the end of the term whether you do condition three or not. It was never the point."

"Then what was the point."

"The point is the other five."

He did not read them off a sheet. He held up a hand instead, and he said them the way a man says six things he has said to himself a great deal.

"One is the ledger. Monday at nine, open to this office and to the department, for the term. Two is the rota and the list, and the list includes the work you do not do, and it is posted where any member of the public may read it, which is the part of it that frightens me and is the reason I like it. Three is the request, in writing, within ten working days of the date of my letter, **and a copy of the filing reference to the district practice standards office** — which I have never seen mentioned by you and which I have read out to about six people since the Thursday, and which is the sentence in that list that has the longest tail on it in this city, and I would like you to think about it for about four seconds longer than you are going to want to.

"Four is not a reward and it is not a punishment. It says the holder does not demonstrate, teach, assess or assist at any practice in this department, in this term, on any premises of this department. Five is the list of the four people who may stop you, given to the department in writing, and the department may put that list in front of any of them at any time it chooses without telling you. Six is a review in person at the end of the term, before a board that is not the panel, and you attend and you are examined."

He put his hand down.

"Those are the five other conditions and they are conditional on this trust still being in the room on the Wednesday of week sixteen. If condition three is not met, the trust is not in the room, and a thing that is not in the room cannot require anything of anybody."

"So the ledger closes."

"The ledger is a book on a bench in a repair shop with a shop's name on the front. What closes is the reason for it being open. From the Wednesday of week sixteen, if the request is not filed, the department may not come and read it, because the arrangement that let them come has ended, and you would have to invite them yourself, one at a time, on your own say-so, forever, and I would not be one of the ones you invite."

"And the list."

"The list comes off the wall. The plastic sleeve goes back in the drawer. The five conditions lapse together because they were never five, they were one condition with a piece of work attached, and the piece of work is not done."

"And the page with the three names on it."

"Now that," said Anders Kolt, "is a different question and you asked it in the right order." He had a folder open and there was nothing in it but a single sheet, and it was not the sheet with the six conditions on it. "This is a shop's book. The shop's name is on the front. Inside the front cover there is a line in another person's handwriting, *S. Quill, lead repairer*, and under the words *maintenance ledger* there is a typeface from about 1978, and the first page has a boy's handwriting gummed into the top of it on a strip of paper about two inches long. I was given this book on the Friday of week fourteen at about half past four in a corridor by a woman of forty-five, who put it in my hands and did not give me anything written, and I have carried it in a folder for four days and I have not got a condition in the world that lets me put a page of it in a report."

"Nobody loses a page."

"Nobody loses a page," said Anders Kolt. "There is no mechanism by which a condition that has expired reaches into a book on a bench in Lattice Ward and takes a boy's handwriting off a strip of paper. **But here is the part I have been in this office for four days about, and it is nothing to do with your page. You lose four of the six.** One, two, three and six go, because they are mine and I am not going to be in the room, and three is not done. **And you keep two. Four and five stay, and they stay for reasons that have nothing to do with me: a prohibition does not need a sponsor, and a document a department received on Thursday of week fourteen is a document a department does not un-receive.** So from Wednesday of week sixteen you may not demonstrate, teach, assess or assist at any practice in that department on any premises of that department, and that is yours now, and I did not invent it; and the four names went to the department in writing, and the department may put that list in front of any of them whenever it likes. **You lose the reasons and you keep the rules, and I have not got a sentence for how that feels, because I have not got an experience of it, and I have done this for nineteen years and I have never once had to watch it happen to somebody I had decided to help.**"

He let that sit, and then he said the thing, and he said it in the same voice.

"Now. I have made this easy on purpose, and I would like you to notice that I have made it easy on purpose, because a man who has spent fourteen weeks refusing to do a piece of work is not a man who should be left to find his own way out of it in the last week of a term."

Marek Senn sat in a chair on the first floor of a building on Cutter Street and did not move for about four seconds, which is longer than it sounds.

"Say the rest of it."

"The rest of it is this. Condition three is a records request for the September open-day issue register and the counterfoil stubs. It has a reference number, it has grounds, and it names an office that holds it. **If you file it, this trust will lift the other five conditions on the same day.** Not in ten working days. The same day. Because condition three is the only piece of work in the list and the other five are not conditional on it at all — they are conditional on me, and I stay engaged with a file that has work in it, and there is no work in it if you do not file the thing. That is not a favour and it is not me being kind. That is a trust with a budget line that cannot justify six conditions and a grant to a man who did not do anything."

"That is a reason."

"Yes," said Anders Kolt. "It is the best reason anybody will ever hand you for anything, and I have just handed it to you in a plain sentence and I have not dressed it up, and I would like you to think about how much easier that has made your year and not about whether I am a good man."

"You could have said no."

"I could have said no and you would have gone away and thought about it for a week and not filed it, and in four years there would be a woman of sixty-eight with a plate on her wrist and no document and it would not be the first one, and I would have been a man who protected a third-year from a piece of work." He picked the sheet up. "So. You know. It is the Tuesday of week fifteen. The condition runs out on the Wednesday of week sixteen. **You have eight days.**"

"Will you put that in writing?"

"No," said Anders Kolt. "I will put the lapse in writing. I will not put the alternative in writing, because a thing in writing is a thing you can be given, and a thing you can be given is the fourth thing this case has tried to make you, and I have read the department's record of its own room and I would like to be the first person in this city who refuses to do it."

---

He wrote three lines in the load book that night at the bench with the door open, and three lines and no headings, because he had three things and three things do not need four headings.

*56. Tuesday of week 15. I asked what a failed condition does, after fourteen weeks of writing down that I had not asked. It is not a punishment. Four of the six go on the Wednesday of week sixteen and two stay. The ledger stops being open to a department and a trust, the list comes off the wall, and the four hundred and eighty pounds stops at the end of the term either way.*

*57. AND HERE IS THE THING. He will lift the other conditions the same day I file it. He said it in a plain sentence and he said he had made it easy on purpose, and I have been sitting in this shop since half past four trying to work out why, and the answer is that a man who has refused nine times for the right reason can be got by a reason instead, and the reason is better than the refusal, and that is the danger, and he knew it and he handed it over anyway.*

*Do not know whether I am keeping a rule by not filing it or keeping a wound by not filing it. There is a difference and I have not found it and I have now got eight days to look for it, and the eight days are the first thing anybody in this city has ever given me to decide anything.*

*And the thing I did not see until I had shut the book, which is that six went with three. Six was the review. In person, at the end of the term, before a board that is not the panel, and I attend and I am examined. That is the only thing in this city anybody ever put in writing that was going to happen to me, and it was on a sheet in a folder in Cutter Street on a Thursday, and it has just lost the man who wrote it, and nobody in this city is going to convene that room now. I have got about fourteen weeks to find out whether a man walks into a room nobody has called. I am not going to write down what I hope the answer is.*

He read the three back and shut the book and put it on the shelf, and left it, and did not add anything to it that week.

---

The post office on Weaver Street was a small one with a green floor and a queue of two, and the box in the corner had a number on it and nothing else on it, and Marek Senn had stood in that queue once, on the Friday of week fourteen, with the three folded copies of somebody else's account of the Tuesday in his hand, and had not put them in.

Ruslan Petric put one sheet in on the Tuesday afternoon of week fifteen, on his way back from the practical building, and told nobody, and the whole of it took about four seconds.

There was no form and there was nothing to fill in and it had to be written by hand, and he had thought about what to say for about four days, which was more thought than Marek Senn had given a piece of paper in a term.

> *To whoever reads these. I was in room one of the practical building on the Tuesday of week fourteen. I was the second candidate. I was sitting at the side. At about twenty-one minutes past nine a member of the teaching staff said a word from the front of the room in the way he says words, and I did what about nine other people in that room did, which was I followed it. I did not know I was doing anything. There was nothing to decide.*
>
> *There is no record of that anywhere that I have been able to find and I have looked, and I would like there not to be one, and I am aware that asking for that is itself a thing that somebody could write down.*
>
> *I am not asking for anything to be given to me. I am asking for that one thing to be not there.*
>
> *R. Petric, third year, Group C.*

He did not tell Marek Senn. He told Asha Reed, in a common room, on the Saturday, because she asked him a direct question and he answered it, and she wrote it down in a notebook that is not a book and is not in anybody's system and which she read the first column of to nine people in a market a fortnight later.
