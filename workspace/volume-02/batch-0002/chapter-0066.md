# Chapter 66 — Nothing to Suspend

The district's practice standards office is on the third floor of a building in Bower Street with a stair that turns, and it has a fire door and a waiting area with four chairs and a water cooler that works, and Marek Senn had never been inside that building and had been told, on the Saturday of week fourteen, in four sentences, not to be helpful. The one thing he knows about the floor below is that a room on it belongs to a man whose title is *continuity and returns*, and that a woman has twice refused that man a machine that could do without her, and that both refusals were given on a telephone while he sat in a shop and said nothing. It is the same building. It is a different office on a different floor, the two of them have never shared a corridor, and he has never met a soul who works on the first floor.

Wyn Dunleavy had sent him because Wyn Dunleavy had four technicians and one of them was the twenty-two-year-old who had filled in the form.

That is the whole of the reason and it was given to him in a corridor at the depot on the Wednesday afternoon in about nine words — *you did the paperwork, you go* — and Marek Senn had said that he would go, and had meant it as a refusal and had heard himself say it as an acceptance, and had then spent four minutes in the van outside working out that it did not matter which one it had been, because the company was not going to send a man of twenty-two in front of a district office on the same morning as a form with his own handwriting on the bottom of it.

Sondra Mbeki was already there when he came up the stair. She was in a wheelchair with the sling and she had a paper bag on her knees with a leaflet in it, and she had got there on her own, and she had got there on a bus, and it had taken her fifty minutes from a flat in Ashfields.

The woman whose job it was was called Dilys Fenwick, and she was fifty-one, and she had been in the district thirty-one years doing standards and complaints, and she had a printed sheet in front of her and she had a pen and she put the pen down when Marek Senn came in, which he noticed.

"You are the technician," she said. "You filled the form."

"Yes. On the Thursday, in about four minutes, on a tailgate, in the rain. I have not said that to anybody yet and I would like it in whatever you write down."

"Then I will write that down." She wrote it down. "I am going to start by telling both of you what this room is and what it is not, because I have had this conversation about a dozen times in thirty-one years and it goes wrong in the same place every time, and the place is that two people arrive expecting either a person to be stopped or a thing to be fixed, and this office does neither and does both, and the difference is a piece of paper with three things on it."

She turned the sheet round.

"A complaint about a certificate holder needs three things. A named holder. A specific act — not a pattern, not a concern, an act, on a day, described in words anybody could check. And harm, to a person who was relying on that holder's certificate, or in that holder's care. Three things. You do not get all three, there is nothing. You get two, it goes into a file and gets a reference and nothing happens for about a year. You get one, you get a letter telling you what would be needed."

Marek Senn looked at Sondra Mbeki's hands.

"There is no holder," said Sondra Mbeki.

"No," said Dilys Fenwick. "There is no holder. I want to say that clearly, because I know exactly what has happened in your head since Thursday and I want to catch it before it gets away from you. Somebody has told you that a company did this. A company is not a certificate holder. A company cannot be suspended and it does not hold a card and nobody in this district can put a condition on it, and if I were to write *Threadline* on a complaint it would come back from the regional office in about six weeks asking me which named individual and which card number, and there is not one, and I would have wasted six weeks of yours and some of mine."

"What about the card," said Marek Senn. "The one she has not got. If the woman had a current 4-19 card, then there is a holder and a specific act and the harm is the harm."

"Yes. That is exactly right. If she had a card, this would be a straightforward matter and I would have it in about nine weeks and I would be asking you very hard questions about a supervisor's confirmation and a radio, and you would be tempted to say something you should not, and I would want it in writing." She put the pen down again. "So. Has she done the course."

Sondra Mbeki said, "No."

"Are you certain."

"A woman rang my firm on Thursday at about eleven and asked me whether I had done the release course and I said no and she asked me to confirm that I had not done the course and I said yes, and she said thank you, and there is a note on my file now that says I have not got a card. So I am certain, and I know it is certain because it is written down, which I did not want."

"Right," said Dilys Fenwick, and wrote *no certificate held — confirmed by subject, and by employer record* on her sheet, and Marek Senn watched her write the second half of it and understood that she had done that on purpose, to give the answer a second source, because the answer was going to be the whole of the case and she was not going to let a woman be told she was wrong about it twice.

"Then," said Dilys Fenwick, "I can tell you what happens, and I am going to tell you the true thing and not the kind thing, because the kind thing is what will make you cross a road for nothing."

She turned the sheet round again.

"Three things. You have got the act — a release that was not confirmed, on a Thursday, at 10:41, with a word given for a different load. You have got the harm — a forearm and an operation and a woman who is thirty-nine and has a sling. You do not have a holder, and the holder is not an optional part, because the entire point of the route is that a person can lose a card. **If there is no record that she was ever taught anything, then there is nothing for me to take off anybody.** That is not a sentence I have come up with. That is the design. A certificate is a thing a person has and can lose, and the whole of the protection in this city for a person who is standing under a load is that somebody who is competent to confirm a release is accountable and can be stopped. Take away the certificate and you have taken away the thing that was being protected. I know how that reads. I have written it in a report once and a woman on a working group cried, and I did not soften it, and I have thought about it every year since."

The room was very quiet and the water cooler made a noise.

"What do I do," said Sondra Mbeki.

"You can make a health-and-safety report. It is not this office, it is a different process with a different threshold, and the threshold is about systems and not about one person, and a single forearm is not a system, unless the same thing has happened more than once at the same place, and I can tell you honestly that I do not know whether it has, and that the only person in this city who would know is the company, and I cannot make a company tell me, and neither can you, and neither, before you ask, can he." She looked at Marek Senn. "Can he."

"No," said Marek Senn.

"Can you ask."

"Yes," said Marek Senn. "I could ask a company's standards woman in about four lines whether there has been a second release in a compound off the Meridian. I could not ask her for the answer and keep my job if the answer was the answer, and I would not be able to tell you what she said, because I would be the company man in this room, and there is no version of that in which what I said is mine. You are right to look at me and I would have said the same about anybody else in this chair."

"Then that is the whole of it," said Dilys Fenwick, "and I have to be honest with you both, because I would rather be honest than be liked, and it is about eleven o'clock and I have got two of these after this. **There is no complaint to bring. Not because nobody will read it. Because there is nothing on the page for a person to be accused of.** The thing that hurt you is a procedure and a card and a radio, and a procedure is not a person and a radio is not a person and a card is not a person, and the one person in the room who is nearest to being the reason has done nine of them correctly that morning and six years before that and there is nothing in this system that lets me put my hand on her for that."

Sondra Mbeki sat in the wheelchair with the paper bag on her knees and looked at the fire door for a while.

"Could I get the card," she said. "Would that help."

Dilys Fenwick looked at her.

"You could get the card," she said, "and I am going to tell you what getting it costs, because I have watched two people do this and both of them found out at the wrong end. It is a two-day course. It is the district's own course, it has been running eleven years, and it is on a Monday and a Tuesday, and it is four hundred and forty pounds, and to an employee of a subscribing company it is on the clock and there is no fee. To anybody else it is four hundred and forty pounds and two days and the pay you do not get for them, and if you have had an operation on a forearm this month then the two days are not available to you and the four hundred and forty pounds is not available to you, and I have said those two sentences out loud in this room before and both times the person heard the second one and not the first one.

"If you had the card, then yes: on Thursday there would have been a holder to complain about, because a person with a card who is told to hold and is not confirmed has been failed by a procedure that their own certificate is evidence they were trained to read. And that is a real complaint and I would take it and I would win most of it. **And the route to the only thing that would let you complain about this is four hundred and forty pounds and two days of a body you do not currently have.** I have known that since before I came to this chair and I have never been able to do one single thing about it, and there is no office in this city whose job it is."

She wrote something on the sheet and turned it round and pushed it across to Marek Senn.

It said, in her handwriting: *Employer rep present. Stated to me that he could obtain from the operator a statement as to prior incidents at the same site and would not, and why. Recorded at his request.*

"You asked me to write that down," she said. "So that is what I have written, and it is in your employer's file as well as in mine, and you have just put a sentence of your own into a district record, which nobody asked you to do and which I imagine was not popular with the man who sent you up a stair with a turn in it."

"No," said Marek Senn. "It was not."

"Good." She looked at the clock. "There is one more thing and then you can both go, and I am going to say it because I have been in this chair for thirty-one years and I have about nine of these left in me and I am not going to be the person who says nothing at the end of one."

She put both hands flat on the table.

"You have not given me a name. I asked you twice this morning and you have not given me a name, and you did not do it unkindly, and you gave me a reason and the reason was the truth, and it is the first time in this chair that the person sitting in it has turned down the thing I actually asked for and told me why in the same breath. **And you are not it.** I do not know where you got the phrase from and I have not asked. But I want to tell you what I have seen in the time you have been in this room, because it is the only observation I am qualified to make and I am going to make it. **The person who caused this is not the problem. If the person who caused this were the problem, then this would have been fixed in about nine minutes on Thursday afternoon, and instead a woman is in a wheelchair in Bower Street because the system worked exactly as it is designed and the design has a hole in it that is shaped like *there is a person who decides*.** You do not fix a hole by removing the woman. You have removed a hundred women from a hundred rooms in this city over the years by doing that, and every one of them was good at her job, and I have watched a company put a nice frame round the hole twice in my career and call it a safety improvement both times.

"So when you go out of here, one of you is going to want to write a sentence down that says what the hole is. I am telling you that if you write it down on a piece of paper it will be the sentence that the piece of paper was for. I am not saying do not write it. I am saying that a sentence which is written down is a thing that gets asked about, and the first person it gets asked about is you, and the second is her, and neither of you can afford it this month."

Marek Senn sat very still, and then he did not stay still.

"There is a thing you have not asked me for," he said, "and I am going to say it and it is the only thing I have, and I want it in front of the person it is about."

"Say it."

"On the Friday of week fifteen, at twenty past one, on a telephone, with her in a first-floor room in Bower Street and me in a repair shop, she told me she would not build a machine to do it without her, and I said to her that if a form ever got to a point where nobody could answer it I would come into a room and stand in it and say the true sentence, and be a bad witness, and there would not be a copy afterwards. And she said that was the best thing anybody had ever offered her." He turned the pen over on the table. "This is a form that has got to a point where nobody can answer it, and I have got the sentence, and I have had it since the Tuesday of week fourteen, and the offer I made out of it was on the Friday of week fifteen, and I have told you I cannot use it, and I am telling you the reason out loud so that it is in your file and not in mine.

"I am going to use one word once and I am going to tell you I am about to, because somebody is going to have it used back at me inside ten minutes and I would like there to be a moment in this room where you knew it was coming. **I know what the fourth beat is.** Not by name. By shape, off a plate, off a card in a drawer under a bench, and off a page in a book that somebody in this city is going to have to read one day. I have had the sentence for about a month and I have not said it in a room and I am not going to today, and I am not going to be coy about why, and it is not a principle, so here it is.

"I signed four sides on a Friday. I am an employee of the company that wrote the instruction on that wall and sells the card in her purse and I have been an employee for four days. If I say a sentence in this room, the sentence is the company's sentence, in a district office, in a file, and in about two years somebody in a regional office writes *the technician said* on a piece of paper, and that is the sentence that did the first damage in this city, and I have worked that out and I have been carrying it since the Sunday. So I am not going to say it, and I want it minuted that I am not saying it, and that I am not saying it because of what I am paid, and that on the Thursday of week fourteen I would have said it in this chair before I had finished the sentence."

There was a pause of about three seconds.

"That," said Dilys Fenwick, and did not look at her sheet, and looked at him instead, "is the first time anybody has told me the reason in thirty-one years."

"Is it the right reason."

"I have no idea," said Dilys Fenwick. "I have been about nine rooms like this one and in about six of them somebody has stood up and made a speech, and in about two of them it has been a speech, and in one it was the true sentence and it was a woman of about thirty and she ruined about nine months of somebody's life and she was right. I am going to write down what you said and I am going to write down that you are correct that there is nothing else I can do with it, and then I am going to do the small thing, which is to give her a reference and a date, and it is not going to help her."

"The phrase," said Dilys Fenwick. "I am not going to write that one down. I have had this office for thirty-one years and I have never had a word like that in a file and I am not starting with a supplier's technician. **But I have been sitting here for four minutes with a woman who has a sling on and a piece of card in her bag that says a thing she cannot prove, and I have been trying all afternoon to work out what the sentence is that makes a person put a card in a bag instead of a complaint in my postbox, and I have not got it, and I have been doing this for nine years.**"

She took a card and wrote a reference on it and a date on it and pushed it across to Sondra Mbeki.

"That is the reference for the health-and-safety report, which is not this office and which you may make, and which will be answered, and the answer may be no. The date is eleven weeks, because that is the honest service standard and I am not going to put a different one on a card to make a morning better. And I am going to give you a sentence and I would like you to keep it, because it is the only weapon I have and it is nearly nothing.

"**If anybody in any depot, on any site, in any office, ever tells you that you have done a release course, or that you have been trained on 4-19, or that there is a record of you anywhere — ask them for the number, and ask them for the date, and ask them who typed it. There is no such thing as a record of a Saturday morning. I have asked a handful of times in nine years. If somebody gives you three of those three things, then come back up this stair and do not wait eleven weeks.**"

"Is that worth anything," said Sondra Mbeki.

"It is worth a great deal more than it sounds," said Dilys Fenwick, "and it is worth nothing at all, and both of those are true and I am not going to pretend it is the first one because you would then use it and it would not work."

Marek Senn was in the corridor outside the office with the fire door open for about two minutes at about ten to twelve, and Sondra Mbeki came out of the stair with the chair and the paper bag and stopped.

"Chairs," she said.

"What?"

"While we were waiting. The woman at the front desk, when I was on the landing, she asked me whether we had any chairs." She shifted the bag. "We have not got any chairs. I have not had chairs in my life. She said it like a joke and then she said — and I am telling you this because I have been in this building for fifty minutes on a bus and I have not been bored, and this is the second thing this morning that has been so stupid it has circled round.

"She said: *we have got a room on this floor with nothing in it for eight weeks a year and there are about twenty women on our books who come in on a Saturday, and we have not got a chair for one of them, and somebody has told me there are thirty plastic chairs going spare in a market.*"

Marek Senn stood in a corridor in Bower Street.

Ena Truslove had put a card in the tin at the Saltmarket Exchange on the Wednesday morning of week sixteen at the sixth item of the monthly meeting, and had said that the chairs were hers, that they came from the building next door, that they were stacked in a corridor on Tuesdays and Thursdays and otherwise in a cupboard, and that anybody in this city who had a thing to say in a room to about twenty people could have them for a Saturday, and that there was nothing to pay and no form, and that she would bring them herself and take them back herself and would not send a van.

And she had said, in that room, in front of twelve people, that those chairs had gone into a college on the Tuesday of week fourteen because they were hers and Friday had come and gone, and that she was the reason a room was public, with a hand truck, at nine o'clock, and that nobody from her nursery had been asked and nobody from her nursery had been thanked, and that she had thought about it for a fortnight and had not worked out whether she would do it again.

Marek Senn had answered that question from a corridor. She would.

"Right," he said.

"You know about the chairs."

"I know whose they are."

"Then you know that whoever collects them is not the Exchange deciding and it is not the market deciding, and it is somebody with a form, or a woman on a landing, or a van." Sondra Mbeki looked at him. "Are you going to do anything about it."

"No," said Marek Senn.

"That is the right answer," said Sondra Mbeki, "and I asked it anyway, and I have got a lift at half past twelve, and I am going to be a technician's customer in about four months if that operation goes the way the man on the television said it goes. Turned round, then. Please. These corridors are a nightmare."

He turned the chair round and took it down the stair with her and put her at the bottom door, and the sun was out, and the street in Bower Street was doing about nine o'clock's worth of noise for a person four months out of a sling, and she went off up the hill towards a bus stop without him, and he watched her go and then stood in the street for a minute and a half.

Then he went back up the stair and knocked on the open door of the office.

"Miss Fenwick."

She was packing the sheet into a folder.

"Can I ask you one thing that is not about the complaint."

"You can ask me one thing that is not about the complaint and if it is a bad question I am going to tell you it is a bad question."

"Four weeks ago there was a Saturday in a back room of a co-operative in Saltmarket," and a company gave a two-hour refresher away, and at the end of it a woman with a typewriter typed thirty-eight cards with people's names on them." He said it carefully. "Is there a record of that Saturday anywhere that you could get hold of, if you asked."

Dilys Fenwick stopped with the folder open.

"Was it district-funded."

"No."

"Then there is not," she said. "There is a card. There is always a card. A card is a piece of card with a name on it and it is not a record, it is the thing a record is remembered by, and the difference between a record and a thing that is remembered by one is the whole of this office." She put the folder down. "I have been asked that exact question eleven times in nine years. In nine years. And I have never once had a name, and I have never once been able to say *I have looked*, and I have said *no* and it has turned out every time that nobody had looked either."

"That is what I thought," said Marek Senn. "It is not a complaint. It is a thing I needed to know for a different reason and I am not going to tell you the reason, and I have put the answer in your head on purpose and I would like you to be annoyed about it."

"I am," said Dilys Fenwick. "Go to work."

He was at the bench in Lattice Ward at about half past four on the Friday afternoon of week nineteen with a load book open in front of him and a pen in his hand, and he had written the date at the top of a page and had not written anything under it for about twenty minutes.

Reuben Daw had lost six weeks of Saturdays on the Wednesday of week fourteen. Not a page, not a form, not a letter. A foreman said the word *college* in a doorway, on a Wednesday, and Reuben Daw was the newest man on a fish office yard and worked Saturdays, and the Saturdays stopped, and one Saturday is one week, and the sixth of them was going to be the next day.

He did not telephone Reuben Daw. He wanted to, at about four o'clock, for about nine seconds, and the reason he did not is that the sixth Saturday is not his, and a man who has been the fourth thing in five working buildings in six weeks does not get to be in a doorway on a Saturday, and he wrote that down instead, in the place where the sentence about the box on the yard sheet is, and the sentence he wrote was this:

*I know what tomorrow is. He has never told me the four words and I am not going to ask him for them, because he has already said it out loud in a market in front of twelve people and in my own hearing, and if I ask him for them again on the eve of the sixth one then I am a man collecting a thing off him, and I have been that man in four buildings in six weeks and I am trying to stop.*

He put the pen down and picked it up again and wrote one more line and it is the only line on that page that is about him.

*I have signed four sides and I am on the clock on a Monday and a Thursday with nobody in the building, and I know what tomorrow is for a man who lost six of them, and there is nothing whatsoever in this city I can do about it, and I have written that down in a book in a shop, which is the fourth document in four days that has cost somebody more than it has cost me.*

*69. Friday of week 19. A room in Bower Street with a woman in it whose job it is, and she was kind, and she was correct, and she was unable, and I have been able to find nothing wrong with any of the three.*

*What the complaint needs and what there is.* Three things: a named holder, a specific act, harm to a person relying on the holder's certificate. We had the act. We had the harm. **We did not have a holder, and a holder is not the optional part.** A certificate is the thing a person has and can lose, and the whole of the protection in this city for a woman standing under a stack of pallets is that somebody competent to confirm a release can be stopped, and the way you remove the protection is to make sure there is nobody to remove. She said that out loud and she said a woman on a working group cried once and she did not soften it, and she has thought about it every year since. I have written it down and I am not going to pretend I have a better sentence.

*How a person stops it.* Not at all, and I have now been in a room where a person with thirty-one years behind her told me the route is shut, and she was not being kind to me and she was not being cruel to the woman, and the only thing in the room she did for herself was put my sentence in her own file because I asked her to, which is the first time in fourteen weeks that anybody has written down something on my behalf that was against my interest. And the thing I have taken out of it, and it is not hers, it is mine: **the nearest person to the reason has done nine of them correctly that morning, and if the nearest person to the reason is the reason, then this was fixed on Thursday afternoon, and it is not fixed, and a woman is in a wheelchair in Bower Street because the system did precisely what it is designed to do.** Every time the hole in a design gets named it gets a person put in front of it and the person goes and the hole is still there. I have been in five buildings in six weeks and I have been the person somebody put in front of a hole five times and I do not know one single thing that would have been worse.

*What the route to the door costs.* Four hundred and forty pounds and two days. She said the second sentence and both times the person heard the second one and not the first one, and that has been true for as long as she has been in that chair, and there is no office in this city whose job it is. I have written that in my own hand and I would like somebody in Bower Street to be angry about it and I am not going to be the one who is, because the woman who should be angry about it is in a wheelchair at home on a Friday.

*What I refused in that room and what it cost me.* I had a sentence and I did not say it, and I have told the whole of a district office the reason, out loud, so that it is in their file and not mine, and the reason is that I signed four sides on a Friday of week eighteen and I have been an employee for four days, and if I say it in that room then in two years a regional office writes *the technician said* on a piece of paper. I was right. I would have said it on the Thursday of week fourteen. **I have now offered one true sentence in a room in this city and refused the same sentence four weeks later, and the difference between the two is a lanyard and four days on a company's rota, and I have written that down, and I have not been able to make it feel like anything except a wage.**

*And whether a sentence does anything at all.* Whether the sentence would have done anything. She told me the truth version: the first person it gets asked about is me, the second is a supervisor of thirty-eight who has done nine of them right, and neither of us can afford it this month. I do not believe her, because I do not think a sentence is a thing that gets asked about, I think that is what she has had to believe for thirty-one years in a chair that nobody sat in before her. And I do not have one piece of evidence against her. That is the first time in fourteen weeks I have been on the other side of a sentence and had nothing at all, and it is the worst place I have stood in this book and I have written four hundred pages about it.
