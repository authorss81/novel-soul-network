# Chapter 64 — The Box on the Sheet

The gate at the near end of that yard is a sliding gate on a track and it is worked by a man in a cabin, and Marek Senn got out of the van at about a quarter past eight in the morning with a work order in his hand and a green cord round his neck, and he did not use it, and the man in the cabin looked at the van and at the cord and at the badge on the cord and pulled the gate back about eleven feet without saying anything at all, and Marek Senn got the van in and shut the door and thought, for about a second and a half, about a sentence he had told himself for three weeks.

Then he got on with the job, and the sentence went where it goes, which is not nowhere, but about four inches under the surface of whatever he was doing, and he noticed it going and did not stop it, and that is what the lanyard does: it does not make you a different person, it moves the sentence about four inches under, and everything you do afterwards is done from four inches lower, which is enough and is not enough and there is no way of telling which until about four months in.

The yard was two hundred yards long with four sets of steps up to a wagon and a control post at the top of the middle one, and about thirty of the forty men who work it on it on a Wednesday morning at the end of the autumn, doing four different things badly in the cold.

Petar Vance was in the cabin at the gate talking to the man in it about the beacon, in the way of two men who have done this before, and Marek Senn went up to the office.

The prefab door was propped open and there was a kettle on a ring and a chair and a window, and a man of about sixty was washing his hands at a standpipe, and inside the office a man of fifty-eight was doing something to a clipboard with a biro.

The blue card was on the wall by the window, exactly as it had been on the Sunday of week fifteen, with the top corner bent about an inch and a half where somebody had sat on it, and the crease gone white on both sides and the printing cracked across it.

**LOAD SECURED — RELEASED BY YARD CONTROL**

And under that, where there would have been a line for a name and a time, nothing at all, and under the nothing a rule, and the rule said **RELEASE ON HORN.**

It did not look like anything. It had looked at Marek Senn for six weeks like a card on a wall, and he had been in a shop doorway and a Saturday room and a woman's back room and a hundred pages of his own book, and he had a sentence in him about it that he had not said to anybody, and it turned out on the Wednesday morning of week nineteen that he had been carrying a sentence about a piece of printed card in a prefab office for a working man to solve a problem he does not have, and he put the sentence where he puts them.

Next to the kettle, on the same wall, at about the height of a man's shoulder, there was a sheet. It was a printed sheet with a grid on it and a printed heading, and where the heading was there was a box with a line in it for a name and a line for a time, and the box was empty, and the paper had gone the colour that office paper goes.

It had been empty in the thirty-one years Aurel Kosta had been in that room. He had told a stranger in a doorway in Lattice Ward on the Tuesday of week twelve that it was empty, and had said that it was not a district document and not a school document, and that he had never asked where it came from, and that it was the last sentence he was going to say about it in his life.

And on the Wednesday morning of week nineteen there was a second piece of paper on that wall, which had not been there on the Sunday of week fifteen, and which was pinned over the corner of the first one, and it was a single printed side with a heading on it in a different typeface and four lines under the heading, and Marek Senn read it standing up because it took eleven seconds.

It said that for a difficult release the person holding the line was to raise a hand and hold it, and was not to enter the path of the load, and was to wait for the company representative's word; and that if no word was received within a stated interval the person was to withdraw clear and report it to the supervisor; and that a certificate holder's name was to be entered in the release register on the yard.

Somebody in this building had put a company instruction on a district yard wall, over a district sheet, eleven days ago, in a typeface that had four weights in it, and the district card was still on the wall underneath it, and the two documents were saying almost the same thing in two different vocabularies about two different things, and nobody had taken the old one down, and nobody had to.

Aurel Kosta had watched him read it.

"Third one this month," said Aurel Kosta. "Not that one. The other one." He did not point.

"The card."

"The card has been on that wall since before I started. I told a lad in a shop in your ward that and I have not told anybody anything since and I am not going to start." He put the clipboard down. "You are not the college one."

"I sat at the back of a hall on Monday morning and I did not do anything."

"You are the one who came on a Sunday with him." Kosta looked at the van through the window and then at the green cord, and Marek Senn watched him work out in about a second and a half that a man with a cord round his neck on a Wednesday morning on his yard is somebody, and watched what happened in his face, and it was the same as the man in the corridor in Ashfields the day before, and it was not the same at all. "That is a company cord."

"Yes."

"That is a supplier's cord. I have had men off that cord in this office for eleven years. You are not selling me a lamp."

"No."

"Then what are you doing on my yard."

"I am a technician. There is a work order for two units on the wagon-side steps and a beacon on that post that has been reported four times since the summer and I have got the same sheet your beacon has had four times on it, and I am here to find out why four men have reported the same thing and been told to close the ticket."

"Right," said Aurel Kosta. "Then the kettle is on the ring if you want one, and the tap is outside and the water is cold, and I am not going to help you and I am not going to stop you, and there is a form on the spike for the work and I do not have to sign it, and if you want me to sign it I will, and I have signed about four hundred of them in thirty-one years for men off that cord and I have never once read one after I have signed it."

---

Petar Vance came in about ten minutes later with the cabin man's name and a bench, and he had a clipboard of his own, and he put it on the table under the window, and Marek Senn watched him stand about two feet from the sheet with the box on it for about four minutes while he asked the man at the control post about a beacon and got an answer about a reed and a bird, and did not look at the sheet, once, in four minutes, in eleven years' worth of habit.

He did not look at it, and Marek Senn knew exactly what that cost, because he had said the same sentence about Kosta in a market and had been thanked for it and had told the man not to thank him.

Vance looked at the second sheet, the company one, and said nothing about it either, and then he went out to the steps with a meter.

---

The beacon was a reed switch in a weatherproof housing at the top of the middle set of steps, and it was a nine-minute job, and it took thirty-five, because the housing is at head height on a post and you are on the top step and the post is at the top of the middle set and there are three other sets and about nine men on them.

The boy was at the post.

He was nineteen and in a hi-vis a size too big and he had been on that yard for twelve weeks and he had stood at that post since the Wednesday a card went up on a wall, and every part of him dated from the card, and he had a clipboard he was not writing on, and when two men with a lanyard came round the corner of the wagon he stood up straighter, immediately, before either of them had said anything, the way a person stands up in a corridor when a door opens.

Marek Senn noticed the boy's eyes go to the cord, and then to the badge on the cord, and then to the badge on Vance's cord, and then back to the post, in about a second and a half, and he noticed that the boy was not looking at a stranger, he was looking at a thing he had been told about.

"Two," said the boy. "We get two now."

"What's two."

"Them." The boy did not point and did not look at them again. "We had a fella come round. Not a fella off the cord. A fella with a book of cards, and he did the whole yard in about an hour, and he was here the Thursday before last, and he was here and he was talking."

A man on the far step, who was about forty, said, "That was the one with the certificates."

"That was the one with the certificates," the boy agreed, and then stopped, and looked at the post, and said, "And he asked whether we'd had the new sheet, and I said we had, and he said the sheet's the rule now, and I said the card's the rule, and he said the card's the release and the sheet's the release, and I didn't—" The boy stopped again. "I didn't want to say it wrong in front of you."

"You have not said it wrong," said Marek Senn. "Say it however you would say it to the man at the post."

The boy looked at the post for about four seconds.

"Both," he said. "He said both. And I've been doing both. I've been waiting for the horn for three weeks because there's a horn, and I've been doing the sheet for three weeks because there's a sheet, and I didn't know till you came that it was two different things."

The man on the far step said, "There's only one horn, son."

"I know there's only one horn," said the boy.

---

They were in the office for about eleven minutes after that, and Marek Senn wrote the fault on the work order, and got the reed switch part number off the card on the housing, and put the housing back on, and while he was doing that the man from the near set came in — Reuben Daw, twenty-two, on the yard Mondays to Thursdays, who had written four hundred words in block capitals into a book in a market about a card on this wall, and who had had a telephone conversation with Marek Senn in a wind in week sixteen about a row that was not his.

Reuben Daw looked at Marek Senn in the office doorway and did not say one word about any of it, and neither did Marek Senn, and they worked in the same eight feet of prefab for about eleven minutes while a man of fifty-eight drank a cup of tea and a man of nineteen stood at a post outside, and it was the most careful eleven minutes of Marek Senn's working life.

Reuben Daw did not say: *you work for them.* He did not say: *you are on a yard off the Meridian with a cord on and he is a district man and you are in his office with his kettle.* He did not say anything at all, and he did not look at the sheet, and when he left he put his hand on the frame of the door on the way past, which is a thing you do to a door frame, and which is not a sentence.

---

At about twenty to ten, with the work order done and the reed switch ordered, Marek Senn went back into the office to sign the sheet on the spike, and the boy came in behind him with a clipboard and then went out again and came back in, which is a thing a boy of nineteen does four times.

"Can I ask you something."

"You can ask me something."

"Is that sheet the one that goes in the book." He said it carefully. "The office book. The one with the boxes on it and the one where you write the time as well."

"There is no office book," said Marek Senn. "There is a sheet on a wall and a box on it and it has not been filled in in thirty-one years, and there is no book, and nothing on it goes anywhere. I am telling you that because you have asked me a question and you are entitled to the true answer before you decide what to do."

The boy took a pen out of his pocket. It was a biro with a chewed cap and it had a company name on the barrel that Marek Senn recognised as his own employer's, and the boy saw him see it and said:

"I did the induction. About a fortnight ago. Half a day, in the office, with a woman with a lanyard. It was good, actually. She was good. She did the whole yard in about an hour and she didn't tell anybody to stop work." He turned the biro round in his hand. "And she said the sheet's the rule now. And I've been on this post three weeks and I've never filled that box in, and I asked her on the Thursday whether I was supposed to fill it in and she said it wasn't hers, it was the district's, and I said fine, and then I went back to the post and I thought about it and it isn't the district's, because I've never seen anybody from the district on this yard, and if it was the district's then somebody from the district would be here sometimes, and nobody's here sometimes."

He put the pen down on the table next to the work order.

"So can I fill it in," said the boy. "Not now. Now there's nobody been on the middle set. But when there's been a fella on the middle set and he's come off and the horn's gone, can I put the time and his name in that box. In the pen. That's it. That's all it is."

Marek Senn looked at the box on the sheet on the wall.

It was a printed box with a line in it for a name and a line for a time, on a sheet that was not a district document and not a school document, in an office on a customer's yard where about forty men worked and where the man who had been in the room the longest had told a stranger in a shop in week twelve that nobody had ever told him how to read a card, and had said the box had not been filled in in thirty-one years, and had said that it was not a district thing and there was no school in it, and had said that it was the last sentence he was going to say about it in his life.

And the boy had a pen with his employer's name on it.

And Marek Senn was the fourth thing in four working buildings in six weeks, and he had spent fourteen weeks refusing to make things exist, and on the Monday of week sixteen a woman of twenty-seven had said *then you have just made my row permanent* and he had written that down and had been told that refusing a copy is how a row becomes permanent, and he had a man of fifty-eight's sentence in him about a shoulder that went into a foreman's report in 2019 and a piece of A4 in a box with the tea bags in it, and neither of them says a man of fifty-eight got hurt on a wagon.

He picked the pen up.

He wrote the date. He wrote the time. He wrote his own name, in the small upright hand he had taught himself for tags, in the box for a name on a sheet on a wall in a prefab office on a yard off the Meridian Corridor, and he did not put anything in the time line about a horn because there had not been a horn and there had not been a release, and he left the rest of the box empty.

Then he gave the pen back to the boy.

"Take it," he said. "And I am going to say three things and then I am going to go and finish my sheet, and I am saying them out loud in this office because there is a man in this office who has been here thirty-one years and a man at that door who wrote four hundred words in a market about a card in here, and if I say them quietly to a boy of nineteen then in two years somebody will find out that a company man said something in here, and I would rather the office heard it."

"Go on."

"One. That is now written down. It is in a building on a customer's yard and it is on a sheet that belongs to a company that is not the district, and there is a company's cord round my neck and I have just written my own name on a wall in a yard I have been in for two hours. **I have spent fourteen weeks not putting a name in a building, and I have done it, and I did it because a boy of nineteen handed me a pen, and I would like somebody to write down that the reason was a pen and not a principle.**

"Two. That does not make anybody safer. It makes one release in one yard have a name and a time on it, and it is the first one in thirty-one years, and there are a great many of these places in this city and a card on every wall, and I have been in one of them twice in six weeks and I have no idea what is on the other eight hundred and ninety-nine and I am not going to go and find out, because I have a job that stops on the last Friday of week thirty and I am not going to spend the wage finding out.

"Three, and this is the one that is actually for you. That box is a record and a record can be used. Not by the district and not by me and not by anybody in this yard, and I am telling you that as a man who works for the company that put the other sheet on that wall, and I have put nothing on that wall, and the reason I have put nothing on that wall is that the second I do, a man off a cord comes round and says a thing about how a load gets released, and then this yard is never the same yard again, and it will not matter what I say, it will matter that I came.

"So the box is yours. You can fill it in. And every time you fill it in, somebody in an office can read it, and one day somebody is going to want to know about a release on this yard and the sheet is going to be the first thing anybody goes to, and the boy who filled it in is going to be the first name anybody reads. That is not a warning. That is just what a box is for. And I have written my name in it once, so that the first name on it is a man who works for a company and not a boy of nineteen, and I would like you to have that for whatever it is worth, which is not very much, and I am aware of that while I am saying it."

The boy looked at the sheet on the wall for a while.

"It is my name on the *rest* of it," he said. "That is what I am saying. If I do them, it is my name on all of them and not just one."

"Yes."

"Right," said the boy, and put the pen in his pocket, and went out to the post.

---

Aurel Kosta had not moved from the chair, and he had a cup of tea he had not drunk, and he said one thing and it was the last thing he said, and it was said to Marek Senn and not to the room, and it was not about the box.

"You have been on this yard two hours and you have not asked me one question."

"No."

"You have not asked me one question," said Aurel Kosta, "and you have read that card, and you have read that sheet, and you have read the new one and you have not said one word about the new one, and you have been in here about half an hour and you have not asked me about my shoulder, and I told a stranger about my shoulder in a shop in your ward six weeks ago, and a man who worked for them came and I said it and I have thought about it every day since and I have not said it to anybody else, and you have not asked me and I have been waiting all morning to see whether you were going to ask me.

"You are the fourth person. I do not mean a bad one. I mean you are the fourth person who has come through that gate and worked out that I am the man in here, and I have been the man in here for thirty-one years and I am fifty-eight and my mate Vic has never had a horn and is a stationmaster now, and I have one question and it is the same one and I have never asked it of anybody in this building because every one of you is a stranger with a reason.

"So I am going to ask it of you, and you can do what you like with it." He put the cup down. "**Why is the box on the sheet and not on the card.**"

Marek Senn stood in a prefab office on a yard off the Meridian Corridor with a green cord round his neck and a work order under his arm and a date and a time and his own name in a printed box on a wall.

"I do not know," he said.

"Right," said Aurel Kosta. "Well. That is the first true thing anybody has said to me about that wall in about eleven years and I am not going to pretend it was worth the two hours." He picked the cup up. "You are not going to come back, are you."

"I do not know."

"No," said Aurel Kosta. "You are not. Off you go, then. And lad—"

Marek Senn stopped in the doorway.

"You did not have to write your name in that box," said Aurel Kosta. "And I have thought about why you did while you were doing it, and I have got one, and it is not a good one, and I am not going to say it in here because the kettle is on and anybody in this building can hear a kettle. But I will say this much and then I am going to get on. That box has been empty for thirty-one years and it has cost nobody anything for thirty-one years, and it is empty because nobody wanted anything off that yard badly enough to make a box. And you have put a name in it, and it is a good thing, and I would like you to know that the reason it is a good thing is that it is now going to cost somebody something, and it will not be you, and it will not be me, and I have a very good idea who it is going to be and I am not going to say her name because she is nineteen and she is at the post."

He turned the light on over the kettle.

"Out you go," said Aurel Kosta, "before the next one comes on and I have to be two men."

---

Reuben Daw was at the near set of steps and Marek Senn stopped at the bottom of them, and neither of them looked at the office.

"He is going to fill it in," said Reuben Daw.

"He is going to fill it in."

"Every one."

"Every one," said Marek Senn.

Reuben Daw looked at the tarmac for about three seconds, the way a man of twenty-two looks at tarmac in a yard when he is putting something in order in a book he cannot get to.

"He did the Tuesday," he said. "Before the card went up. He held a shutter on the top set for a man and looked up at that post the whole time and nobody said anything. That was before." He shifted his weight. "He will not be doing that with a box on the wall and a name in it. He will be doing it slower and he will be checking, and checking is what he has not been doing, and it is the only thing on that yard that was actually wrong with him." He looked up. "That is not a good thing. I want that written down somewhere. It is not a good thing and I have known it since the Sunday of week fifteen and I wrote four hundred words and none of them were about that."

"Then write the other four hundred," said Marek Senn.

"I am going to," said Reuben Daw. "I have not told anybody that either. That is twice in a fortnight and you are the only person I have told and it is because you are stood on my steps with a cord on, which is the only reason there is."

The van was in the yard at a quarter to eleven and the gate went back about eleven feet without a word, and Marek Senn drove out of it and sat at the road for about a minute with his hands on the wheel.

He had a date and a time and his own name on a wall in a yard off the Meridian Corridor and about twenty minutes' work still to do that afternoon, and he had not asked Aurel Kosta one question in two hours and had been asked one instead, and he was carrying a sentence in him about a card on a wall that he had taken out of his mouth in a corridor in week twelve on the grounds that it was a yard thing and a district thing and had no school in it, and it had no school in it, and a man of fifty-eight had said *you will be wrong and every man on this yard will know you are wrong inside a week*, and every man on that yard would have known it inside a week.

And the last thing was the only thing that was really his, and it was not a thing he had found out. It was that a man of fifty-eight had decided in about four seconds that Marek Senn was somebody, and had told him so by not helping him, and had asked him the same question he had refused to ask, and had then told him what the answer would cost, in a prefab office, out loud, with the kettle on, in terms.

And Marek Senn had done nothing at all on that yard except a reed switch, a form, and his own handwriting in a box, and it had been the worst two hours of his working life, and he was going to be back, and everybody in that yard knew it.

---

He wrote it at about ten past ten that night at the bench with the door shut, and it came out in about half an hour, and it is the only page in that book in nineteen days that he has not had to stop in the middle of.

*67. Wednesday of week 19. A yard off the Meridian, a card on a wall, a reed switch in a weatherproof housing, and about nine minutes in a prefab office with the kettle on.*

*What the card is.* LOAD SECURED — RELEASED BY YARD CONTROL. Under that, where there would be a line for a name and a time, nothing at all. Under the nothing a rule, and the rule says RELEASE ON HORN. The top corner is bent about an inch and a half and the crease has gone white on both sides and the printing is cracked across it, and it is older than me and it is not a school thing and it is not a college thing, and a man of fifty-eight told me in a doorway in the twelfth week that if I go into a yard and say it came out of a college then every man on that yard will know I am wrong inside a week, and he was right, and I did not say it, and today I read it standing up in an office with a reed switch part number in my pocket and I had a sentence ready and I put it where I put them.

And about a foot away from that card, pinned over the corner of a sheet, there is a second piece of paper now, and it was not there on the Sunday of week fifteen. It is a company instruction, four lines, one typeface with four weights in it, put up on a district's yard wall eleven days ago by somebody whose job I do not know, and it says that for a difficult release the person holding the line raises a hand and holds it, does not enter the path of the load, and waits for the company representative's word, and that a certificate holder's name is to be entered in the release register. **Two documents on one wall, in two vocabularies, about two different releases, both about loads, and the district's is still there underneath and nobody has taken it down and nobody had to.** I stood and looked at the two of them for about a minute and a half and I have not written a sentence about the pair of them and I am not going to today, because today I did the thing below, and the thing below is worse, and if I write the sentence about the two of them today I will use it to make the other thing look smaller.

*What I put in the box, and the reason it was a pen.* The sheet next to the kettle has a printed box with a line for a name and a line for a time and it has been empty in thirty-one years. It is not a district document and it is not a school document. A boy of nineteen handed me a biro with my employer's name on the barrel and said *can I fill it in — not now, there's nobody been on the middle set, but when there's been a fella on the middle set and he's come off and the horn's gone, can I put the time and his name in that box.*

I said yes. I did not say *ask somebody else first* and I did not say *wait until there is a system*. I said yes, and I picked the pen up, and I wrote the date and the time and **M. Senn** in a box on a wall in a building on a customer's yard, in a company-customer relationship, with a green cord round my neck, and I gave the pen back to the boy, and he put it in his pocket and went out to the post.

**I have spent fourteen weeks not putting a name in a building so that nobody could be found afterwards, and I have done it four times in three weeks, and the reason on this occasion was that a boy of nineteen handed me a pen.** I want that written down as the reason, because if it is written down as a principle then in four years somebody will read it and think a man thought about it for a term, and he did not, he stood in an office and a boy asked him a question and he said yes.

*What it costs the boy, and I told him in the office and he is not to forget it.* I said three things out loud because there was a man who has been in that room for thirty-one years and a man at the door who has written four hundred words about a card in it, and the third one was this: that box is a record, and a record can be used, and the boy who fills it in is the first name anybody reads. Not by the district and not by me and not by anybody on that yard — by whoever wants to know about a release in about two years. **He said, back to me, that the name on it would be his on all the rest of them and not just the one, and he was right, and that is the arrangement now, and I am the first line of it, and the price of my being the first line is that it is now a company's yard, a district's card and a company's sheet, and a boy's handwriting, and there is nobody whose job it is to ask the boy whether he wanted any of that.**

*What the boy said, which took four seconds.* There was a woman with a lanyard round the yard about a fortnight ago and she did an induction and a half a day and she was good, and she told him the sheet was the rule now. And a boy of nineteen, three weeks at that post, said to me in about nine words on a set of steps: *we get two now* — and he meant me and the engineer I was with, and he recognised a cord before either of us had said anything. **I have been the fourth thing in a company's product since the Saturday of week fifteen and I did not know it had an industry.**

And then, after the box, he said the other one and I want it in the book in his words: *I have been waiting for the horn for three weeks because there is a horn, and I have been doing the sheet for three weeks because there is a sheet, and I did not know till you came that it was two different things.* A yard has a horn and a yard has a sheet and nobody has ever told a boy they are two different releases, and the boy worked it out in three weeks standing at a post, and a man of fifty-eight standing ten feet away with a clipboard who has been in that room for thirty-one years did not work it out and I have known him for six weeks and I have not earned the right to bring it up.

*Do not know, and it is not a small one.* He asked me a question and I answered it and the answer was true. **A man of fifty-eight asked me why the box is on the sheet and not on the card, and I said I do not know, and it was the first true thing anybody has said to him about that wall in about eleven years, and he told me afterwards, in that office, with the kettle on, that the box has been empty for thirty-one years because nobody wanted anything off that yard badly enough to make a box, and now somebody does, and it will not be me and it will not be him and it will be a boy of nineteen.** I have now made a mechanism. It is a box and a pen and a boy who is good at waiting, and it is running, and I cannot stop it, and I could not have stopped it even if I had said no, because the sheet is on the wall and the woman with the lanyard is real and the boy was going to fill it in with somebody's name on it by the end of the month. **I have been the fourth thing in four working buildings in six weeks and I have now been it a fifth time, in a fifth building, on company time, in a customer's yard, in about forty seconds, out loud, with a reason, and I did not even have to be asked, which is the part I am going to have to sit with for a while.** I have never once been in a room where the fourth thing was the right answer, and the fourth thing is never the right answer.
