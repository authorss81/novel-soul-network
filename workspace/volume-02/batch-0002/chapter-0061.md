# Chapter 61 — Nothing Different In It

The shop had been open since eight and the extractor had been on since a quarter past, and Marek Senn had done four jobs and had charged for three of them, and the one he had not charged for was a woman in Ashfields with a lamp that had been out for a fortnight and who had gone out on the bus to the shop twice to tell somebody about it.

He had no wages. That was the thing he had been living inside since the Friday of week twelve without ever putting the sentence anywhere. He had a trade and about half the work in Lattice Ward went to a shop off Cutter Street that had a man of fifty in it, and a man of fifty is better at a lamp than he is. Everything that had come in since the week before the term had come in one lump sums: twenty-eight pounds here, sixty there, forty off a hospital on Fell Road that had taken eleven hours and paid for nine of them, and the trust's money, which had been twenty pounds a week for twelve weeks and had stopped on the Friday of week twelve along with everything else the trust had been paying for.

Nothing had arrived on a date since the week before the term. He had written that down once, in week eleven, and had not looked at it since, and he was aware on this particular Friday afternoon that he was aware of it.

At about ten past four the bell went and it was Oren Vey, with no folder, in a plain shirt, carrying four sides of paper in a clear plastic wallet, who came the whole length of the shop and put the wallet on the bench and then sat down in the chair by the extractor, which was the same chair he had sat in on the Tuesday of week sixteen.

"There is coffee in that tin if you want it," said Marek Senn.

"No. I have a car park to get back to and a Tuesday that has gone wrong." He squared the four sides on his knee. "Same four pages. Nothing has been retyped. Nothing has been added. I have read it twice on the way here looking for something I could improve without changing it, and I have found one full stop, and I have left the full stop alone, because if you have signed a page because of a full stop then I have not been talking to the right man."

"Read it out."

"I am not reading it out. You have read it. I am putting it on the bench." He put the wallet down and took his hand off it. "That is the whole of the delivery, and I want to be accurate about what I have and have not done with it. I have written to three other people about the vacancy this week. I have told one of them the start date. I have not told anybody the name of the person it is going to, and the reason I have not told them is not discretion, it is that I do not know yet, and I am not going to be a man who says a name in a corridor and then telephones somebody and says the corridor was wrong."

Marek Senn took the four sides out of the wallet and put them flat on the bench and read them, and it took him about ninety seconds, and he read them the way a man reads a document he has read before in order to find out whether he has remembered it correctly.

**TECHNICIAN — DISTRICT SERVICE, LATTICE. Four days, Monday to Thursday. Grade 2, spine rate plus an on-call allowance. Reporting to W. Dunleavy, district service manager. Start: Monday of week 19. The appointment runs to the end of the service term unless terminated earlier, and the service term ends on the last Friday of week 30. One month's notice, on the front page.**

Below that, the fifth clause, which he read twice, and the card language, which he has read so often in the last fortnight that he could have said it from the back of a room.

The spine rate for a grade two technician was thirteen pounds and forty an hour, and thirty-eight hours, which came to five hundred and nine pounds and twenty a week before anything came off it, and the on-call allowance was forty-six pounds for a week on the rota, and the rota ran one weekend in four, which over twelve weeks was three, and a man on call in his first month did not get the choice of which one.

"Nine seconds," said Oren Vey.

"What?"

"You counted. I watched you count. You have been looking at the hours and the allowance and the rota and you counted it in about nine seconds, the way a man counts the thing he is actually asking himself, and I am not going to pretend the money is not in it, because it is, and I put it on the page at the top where you would find it before you found the end date, and the end date is in the third line, and I have been waiting seventeen days to see whether you read the hours or the date first."

"I read the date first."

"I know," said Oren Vey. "That is the entire problem with you and I have not worked out yet whether it is a virtue or a habit."

He put his hands flat on the table on either side of the wallet.

"So here is what I am going to do, and I am doing it out loud because you have watched me do it on the Tuesday and you worked out that I do it on purpose, and you were right, and I am not going to insult you by pretending today is a different kind of conversation. **I am not buying a man who agrees with me.** I do not want one. If I wanted one I would have got a cheaper one on the Tuesday afternoon inside about nine minutes and I would not have brought it back on a Friday, and you would have signed it on the Tuesday and gone off to a yard on the Monday and never once thought about it again, and my training budget would have been forty thousand cheaper this year. **I am buying a man who will write down what he finds.** And those are not the same job, and the second one is the only one of them that is any use to me, and it is the one that is going to cost you money, because the man who writes down what he finds is going to write down things about my own company, and about my own pack, and about the sentence at the bottom of the card, and I have thought about that for seventeen days and I have decided that I would rather have it in a technician than not have it at all, and I have also decided that I am not going to be one of those men who says that out loud to somebody's face in a repair shop on a Friday afternoon and expects to be thanked for it."

Marek Senn had his hand flat on the four sides and had not moved it.

"Then why now," he said.

"Because on the Tuesday I was a man in a corridor being helpful and this week I have had a district buyer ask me for a figure I do not have, and I have had a supervisor in Ashfields fill in a form in about four minutes and get it exactly right, and I have had somebody ask me a question in a corridor that I could not answer, and I did not like the answer I gave her, and I went away and worked out that the reason I could not answer her is that there is nobody in my company whose job it is to have the answer. That is not a scandal. That is a department of one. And I have been an operations director for six years and I have never once had a technician whose job it is to tell me when a form is right." He stopped. "I am not telling you this so that you will think well of me. I am telling you because it is the reason there is a job on a piece of paper, and if I do not say that then you are being given a job for a sentimental reason and you will be able to work that out inside a fortnight and then you will be on a different sort of rota."

Marek Senn read the four sides one more time, all the way down, and then he took the pen off the string, and he did not sign it, and he put the pen down again.

"I am not signing it yet," he said. "I want to ask four questions and I want them answered in this room, and if the answer to any one of them is a shrug then I will not sign it, and I will not be angry, and you will have lost seventeen days of waiting and seventeen days of me not saying yes, and that is the correct outcome and I would rather have it than a signature I do not understand."

"Ask."

"What happens on the last Friday of week thirty."

"Nothing is arranged," said Oren Vey. "I have told you there is not one. The service term ends and the establishment is reviewed and in the new year either the vacancy exists or it does not, and if it does not, this is a fixed-term appointment and you have thirty-one days on it and no more. I am not going to soften that and I am not going to put anything under it. If I had a clause that said *renewal expected* I would not be able to keep it, and you would be able to, and I would rather you did not have something I cannot honour."

"Who is my supervisor."

"Wyn Dunleavy. Fifty-two. He has been in post nine years and he will be at the depot at seven on Monday and he will say good morning to you and ask whether you have got your own tools, and he will not ask you anything else for a fortnight. He is not warm and he is not cold. He signs things he does not choose, he is not a coward about it, and he is going to be the man who countersigns a recall in about four months if it comes to that, and if it comes to that he will do it inside a day and he will not enjoy any part of it."

"And the fifth clause. Not the card — the course. Two days, on the clock. What happens if I am the fourth one in on the Monday of week nineteen and the intake is full."

"Move you to the next intake, and I will not pretend that is costless to me, because the next intake is the Thursday and the Thursday is my only day on a yard without a depot to be in." He turned the fourth side a quarter of an inch. "And there is no way round the fourth clause and there is no clause anywhere in this company that lets you stand in a room alone with one of our plates and run a practice. Not on a customer site, not in a yard, not in a training room, not for four minutes. I have looked, because I would have liked to give you that, and I asked a woman in our own standards side this week whether an employee could hold a supervised practice with a customer present and be the named holder for the purpose of it, and the answer came back in writing in about four lines and it was no. It is no everywhere in this trade. It is no because of what happened in this city before any of us were working, and the reason it is no is not a company being unkind to a man, it is the only clause anybody in this business got right."

He put the pen back on its string.

"That is four," said Marek Senn. "I asked four questions and you answered all four and none of them was the question I have been carrying for seventeen days, and I am going to tell you what that question is, and then I am going to sign it, and I want you to hear the reason before the signature rather than after it, because after it you would be able to say I signed first and thought second, and that is not what happened."

Oren Vey sat back.

"The question is not on the page," said Marek Senn. "I have not worked out what the job is. I have spent seventeen days on it and I have got four things and none of them is an answer. I do not know what your four days are for. I do not know what a technician in Lattice does with a plate. I do not know whether the thing that is actually wrong in this city is a page or a street or a sentence, and I have known since the twelfth week of the term that I would not know, and I have had three weeks of not knowing and I have not come out of it knowing anything.

"I am signing it because on the Tuesday of week sixteen a man of forty-one came into this shop and did three things well, and I want to be exact about them, because they are the whole of my reason and I would like them written down. One. He refused, out loud, a thing he could have done to a boy of twenty in about nine minutes, and he told me what he would have done instead, and that is not a thing a man does to be fair to me, it is a thing he did so that it would be on me. Two. He wrote the blank field's next question down on the back of his own hand and then wrote it out again on a sheet so that he would not lose it, which means he intended to do it and he knew that people who intend to do things lose them. Three. He put the end date in the third line in bold, where I would see it before anything else on the page, and he could have left it off, and he told me he could have left it off, and I have not been able to think of anything else in fourteen weeks that was given to me for free by a person who did not owe me anything.

"Those are three things and they are all the same thing, which is that a man was straight with me in three different rooms in one afternoon, and it took him no time, and it cost him something, and he did not do it for me. And I am signing this piece of paper because of that, and not because I have worked out what the job is, and I know perfectly well that the second of those is the only one of the two I could defend to a board at the end of the term, and I have not done the second one, and there is a fortnight of a man's life in that and it is mine and not his and he offered me seventeen days of his own time to find out and I did not use them for that."

Marek Senn picked the pen up off the string and signed four sides in a small upright hand that he had taught himself for tags, and dated it, and put the pen down again.

"Right," said Oren Vey, and did not say anything else for about four seconds, and then said, "Thank you," in a completely flat voice, and put his hand flat on the four sides and did not take them away, which was the first time in three visits he had not picked a document up off that bench within a second of finishing with it.

"You are not getting a thank-you," said Marek Senn. "I have told you the reason and the reason is that I have been got by a reason instead, and I have written that down in my own book on the Tuesday and it is the most frightening sentence in it. A man who has refused nine times for the right reason can be got by a reason, and the reason is better than the refusal, and the reason is the danger, and he knew it and handed it over anyway. That is me, on the Tuesday of week sixteen, about you. I would like you to notice that I have just said out loud that a wage was the thing that did it, and I have not got a single better word for it."

"I have got a word for it," said Oren Vey. "It is called a job. You are twenty-two and you have not had one."

"Do not do that."

"Then I will not." He got the four sides into the wallet. "I have got to tell you two things and then I am going, because I have a car park to get back to and it is Friday. One. The rota. You are on the depot floor Tuesdays and Wednesdays, which is what the depot actually needs and which is a genuine statement about staffing. Mondays and Thursdays you are on the road with a work order, and on a Monday you sign on at seven, take the van, and there is no shift on because there is nobody in at seven on a Monday after the twenty-third of the month. Which means that two days a week you are the only person in a company building, and you are alone on company premises on company equipment under company insurance, and there is nobody there to tell you no."

"Four days a week. And on a Monday there is nobody in the building."

"On a Monday after the twenty-third there is nobody in the building and there is a light on over the office and a spike and a key board and no person. I have told you before and I am telling you again with the date on it, because I would like it written down that I told you." He stood up. "I put you on the Tuesdays and the Wednesdays for a reason and you have worked out that the reason is your Mondays, and the reason your Mondays are clear is that I have never asked you why a man who has been to a flood round on about seven Monday evenings since the summer needs a Monday evening at this time of year, and I did not ask because I did not want to be told, and I have decided I would rather be the man who did not ask than the man who asked and was told."

Marek Senn did not say anything about that for a moment.

"About seven," he said.

"About seven before the year's out. Every one a Monday at ten past six, nineteen men, one bar, a set of steps at the Cutbank, and a woman who finishes on four and came off four for the first time in about a month and has told nobody but me and a man with a rail, and it is not cured, and there is no insurer, and there is no licence and no legal person, and there is nobody in this city whose job it is to take a count out of a person." Vey got his keys out. "And from the Monday of week nineteen you are on that bank as a technician of the company that sells the course those nineteen men are being taught out of. That is the cost of the kindness I have just done you and I have told you the cost of it on the Tuesday and I am telling you again, and I would like you to write it down somewhere with a date on it and not in a book nobody reads, because I have a bad feeling about the two of us and a worse one about week twenty-one."

"The district that writes those cards is a customer of yours."

"The district that writes those cards is a customer of mine and has been for eleven years, and I have never met the man who writes them, and I have never once asked why there is a box for a name and a time on a sheet in a yard office and no line for a name and a time on the card on the wall beside it. I said that to you in this shop on the Tuesday and I have not stopped saying it since, and I am saying it now because in three days you are going to be in a building four days a week and it will stop being an interesting question and become a thing you know."

He got to the door.

"Two things and then I am gone," he said. "The induction pack. It is in the wallet. Take it, and read it, and if you have not read it by Monday morning you will be doing a job you cannot do properly, and I am not going to tell you which pages to read because that would be telling you what to think on your first morning and I have decided I am not doing that to anybody twice."

The pack was a ring binder in a clear pouch with a printed label on the front and eleven pages inside it and a card in a cellophane sleeve at the back. Marek Senn put it on the bench and read page four standing up, in the light, with the extractor going.

Page four was not what he had expected and it was much worse than what he had expected.

It was a diagram, drawn well, in four colours, of a beam and a sling and a man standing under a load at head height, and the diagram was good, and the colours made sense, and there were four numbered steps and the first three were things he would have told anybody to do. The fourth step was a line of capitals under the diagram, and the line was:

**RELEASE IS GIVEN BY ANOTHER VOICE. DO NOT RELEASE ON YOUR OWN JUDGEMENT.**

And under the line, in a smaller print, a sentence about a supplied card, and a card number with a revision on it, and the card number was the one on the front of his induction pack and the revision was 4-19.

He read it four times. He put the binder down and picked it up and read it a fifth time, and then he put the eleven pages in a drawer under the bench, and not on the shelf where the manila folders were, and he shut the drawer.

The light in the shop had gone some while ago.

He telephoned the bank of the Weir at about half past five and Nkechi Osei picked up on the second ring and said her name and then said nothing, which is a thing she does, and which is a courtesy.

"I have taken the job."

"That is Thursday's news," said Nkechi Osei. "Somebody told me on Wednesday without meaning to, and I have not told a living soul, and I am not going to tell you who, because the person who told me does not know that they told me. So that is one, and you are two, and I have not counted anybody else."

"Say the rest of it."

"I am an employee of the company that sells the course, from the Monday of week nineteen, four days a week, on a rota, at grade two, and there is a clause in my contract that puts me on their own two-day course in my first month and gives me a card with my name typed on it that does not expire, and I have not got the card yet and I am going to get it in about three weeks and I do not know what I am going to do about it." He put his hand flat on the bench. "You said in this shop about three weeks ago that you had asked for a room with a bit of paper in it and somebody in it who can say no, and that what I built was a bit of paper and a man. I have been turning that over every day since and I want to say one thing about it and then I will stop."

"I have not built the room. I have gone and joined the thing that fills the room, and I have got a lanyard, and a lanyard gets through a door, and I have spent fourteen weeks refusing to make things exist and I have now put my own name in four places in a fortnight, one of which is a signature on a wage." He heard the river. "So that is the honest version, and I am not dressing it up as an accident, and I have not told anybody else that I have worked it out and I am telling you first because you are the person who said the sentence and I owe you the news before I owe anybody else a good story about it."

"Right," said Nkechi Osei. "Can I tell you the thing about the number, and then you can decide what you think about it."

"Tell me."

"It is not seven. It was seven a month ago and it is not going to be seven for long, and I do not know what it is going to be, and neither does anybody, and if I told you a number now you would write it in that book of yours and it would be a number about a flood and not a number about a person, and you have been careful about that for fourteen weeks and I would rather you stayed careful." A boat went past on the far bank. "What I am going to tell you is that you have not changed the number. That is what I want said. You have changed who is standing on the bank when it goes wrong and whose letterhead is on the form, and there is a difference between those two things and it is a very large difference, and it is the only difference available to anybody in this city at the moment, and I have watched about four people take jobs in the last two years for exactly the reason you have just given me and not one of them has made a room."

"Nkechi—"

"I am not finished. You have signed something that stops on the last Friday of week thirty, and that is a Friday, and the round is on a Monday, and I want you to hear me say this once and then never say it again: **you have not given me a room and you have not taken one away, and there is still no person in this city whose job it is to be told that I am on four.** I am on four this evening. I came off three about a month ago and off four three weeks ago and I have not been able to go up past the second rail, and I have not told my sister, and I have not told the man I share a flat with, and there is nobody to tell, and I have been standing on this bank since a quarter past six every Monday for about two months and about six of those Mondays I have been on four, and you are going to be on this bank in three days as a man with a company badge, and I am telling you now so that when it happens it is not a surprise to either of us and so that you cannot tell me afterwards that you did not know."

Marek Senn stood at the bench with the telephone in his hand and the extractor off and the shop dark except for the lamp over the bench.

"I will be here at ten past six," he said.

"You will be here at ten past six and you will be on their time from the Monday of week nineteen, and if I come off four on a Monday and you cannot come, then I want you to tell me that on the Friday, in a sentence, before the Monday, and not to tell me afterwards. That is the whole of what I am asking and it is smaller than you are going to think it is."

"That is the whole of what I am asking as well."

"Good. Then go and eat something." The line did not go. "Marek. One more and then I am going to cook. When you have that card in your pocket with your name typed on it — and you are going to have it unless you do something about it before Monday, and I am not going to tell you what to do about it, because that is your one and I have not earned it — you are going to be the only man in a room with one. And I am not going to be the one who warns you about it, because you will get twenty warnings off people who are frightened of a card and none of them off somebody who has watched what happens on a bank. So that is all. I am telling you on a Friday night, three days before a Monday, because that is the only leverage I have ever had in my life and I am spending it on a man behind a counter, and I have not spent a thing on myself with it, because there is nothing about me to warn."

She put the phone down.

The river went on for about four minutes after that and then it did not, because she had gone inside.

He wrote it at the bench at about ten past ten with the shutters up and the door shut, and the shop had a strange smell of a bank in it that was not in the shop.

*64. Friday of week eighteen. I said yes in the same chair, with the same four sides on the bench, and the pen was the pen off the string and the chair was the chair by the extractor, and I read the end date before I read the money and I want that written down before anything else on this page, because I have been telling myself for three weeks that I was the sort of man who read the money first and I am not.*

*Three things well.* A man of forty-one refused out loud a thing he could have done to a boy of twenty in nine minutes and told me what he would have done instead. He wrote a question on the back of his own hand and then wrote it out again on a sheet so that he could not lose it. He put the end date in the third line in bold where I would see it first and told me he could have left it off. Three rooms, one afternoon, no time, and it cost him something, and he did not do it for me. **I have signed a four-sided piece of paper for those three sentences and not for the job, and I said that out loud in the shop before I signed it rather than after, and Oren Vey said thank you in a flat voice and then did not pick the paper up for four seconds, which is the only time in three visits he has left something on my bench.**

*What it is.* Five hundred and nine pounds and twenty a week before tax, and forty-six for a week on the rota, and one weekend in four on call, and three on-call weekends in a term of twelve weeks. Nothing has arrived on a date since the week before the term. The first wage day is a Friday in about a fortnight and it is the first figure in four months that I can plan a month around, and I have written that in a book and I have not said it in a room, and the fact that I have written it and not said it is the whole of what is wrong with this page.

*Do not know.* Whether I have given away the reason for a wage or got the reason for a wage. He asked me for the page four of his own pack at the door on his way out — no, he did not ask me, he put the pack on the bench and said he was not going to tell me which pages to read — and page four is four lines in capitals and a card number, and it has been in a drawer under the bench since about ten past six and it is not on a shelf. **I read a company's own fourth beat out of a company's own binder on the Friday of week eighteen at about ten past six and I am going to be in a room on Monday morning at half past nine with forty-one people who have not seen it, and I have signed a piece of paper, and the only thing I have done with the four lines is put them in a drawer, and I have not told anybody, and that is the eleventh time.**

*The second dependence.* She is not cured and she was not cured by this and she said so first and she said it in about nine seconds, and the sentence I am to take out of it is not *I have gained nothing* because that would make me the important thing. **I have not changed the number. I have changed whose letterhead is on the form, and there is a very large difference between those two things and it is the only difference anybody in this city can currently offer her, and she has watched about four men take a job in two years for the reason I have just given her and not one of them has made a room.** And then she spent the last of it refusing to be dramatic about what I am about to become and refusing to warn me, and saying that she is not going to stand on a bank and explain a lanyard to a man who has already worked out what one is, because working it out is not the same as carrying it and she would rather be asked for the one sentence than watch him get the other one from somebody worse. And then she told me what to ask for and it was one sentence: if you cannot come on a Monday, tell me on the Friday, before, and not afterwards. That is smaller than I expected and I am going to be able to do it, and I have written it down, and being able to do a small thing that a person has asked for in nine seconds is the only thing in this entry that is not about me.

He shut the book at about twenty to eleven and put it on the shelf by the day book, and did not read the last page back, and noticed, on the way to the door, that the Exchange's monthly meeting was on the first Wednesday of every month and the next one was the Wednesday of week twenty, and that Asha Reed's room was on the same Wednesday at the same hour in a different building, and that the four-column book in the Saltmarket was at forty lines and had been at forty lines for two weeks, and that he was going to be in a company depot on that Wednesday at ten to ten, four miles from both of them, on the clock.
