# Chapter 69 — Eleven Years Old

There is a building off the roundabout in Lattice with a district's mark on one half of the door and a company's name on the other half, and on the Monday morning of week twenty the whole of the top floor of it had twenty-eight people in it who had paid, or been sent, and Marek Senn was on a work order in Nine Bridges at ten with a meter bag and a green cord and was not in it.

He knew what was in it because a boy of twenty posted a buff envelope at a postbox on a corner at half past ten on Saturday morning with four hundred and forty pounds in it and an empty box on the top sheet and a second empty box under it, and he knew the dates because they had been in a letter with *from Deb* written on the envelope in capitals.

That was the whole of his Monday. He did a reed switch, he did a lamp, he was let through a gate by a man who did not ask him anything, and at about twenty past ten he drove past a roundabout with a building on it and did not slow down, and that was a decision and it took a second and a half.

---

Petar Vance told him about the Thursday at about eleven on the Tuesday morning, in the depot, with the sheet in his hand.

"Your intake's Thursday. Same building off the roundabout, both modules, eight to four, and the card comes at the end of the second day." Vance looked at him. "I put you on the course in your first week. That is my job and I have done it and I am telling you I have done it. The Thursday is not me. The Thursday is the department's own intake list and it went up in week nineteen and I did not put you on it and I did not argue about it either."

"Right."

"Now I am going to say a thing and then I am going to Ashfields." He squared the sheet. "There is a practical day this morning on a yard off the Meridian and it is the district's own yard and the district has booked it off us — that is a booking, it is on the sheet, it says *training hire, Tue*, and it has been on that sheet since before I got here. So this morning there will be a company instructor from this company standing on that yard teaching a district course, and the district sent an assessor to watch, and about twenty-one of the district's own people will be on the yard doing it, and I am telling you because I am going to be at the end of that canopy for the whole of it and nobody will notice that I am there, and nobody would have noticed if I was not.

"You are not on this morning's practical. Your intake is the Thursday. I am not taking you off it and I am not moving you and I have not moved you." He put the sheet down. "I am going to be straight with you about the two things I have not said. There are two people in this depot who have asked me whether you are going to be all right in a room, and one of them is me and the other one is the operations director, and neither of us said it to the other one, and neither of us said it to the district service manager, and the district service manager has not asked me, and I have been thinking about that for two days now and I have decided it is not mine to raise with him and I am not going to raise it with him, and I am telling you that it happened because on Thursday you are going to be in a room with a woman who is better at the thing than anybody in this city and I am not going to be the one who let you find that out on your own."

"Appreciated."

"Don't," said Petar Vance. "It is not appreciated. It is Thursday, and it is a Thursday, and the Thursday is my only day on a yard without a depot to be in, and you are going to cost me a yard, and I have put you on the course myself and I would do it again."

---

The yard was a district yard off the Meridian Corridor and it was not the yard with the blue card on it, and Marek Senn found that out at the gate by asking, and felt something go out of him that he had not known was in him.

It was a hard-standing yard with a slab office at one end and a canopy at the other and about thirty men on it, and there were four new-flamed pallets of block paving under the canopy and a powered band cutter on a tripod, and it had the same shape as nine other yards in this city and Marek Senn understood, standing in it at ten past eight in the morning, that the shape is the point and that the shape is about four hundred yards of hard standing with a canopy at one end and a way out and a man in a control post.

A district's own day, on a district's own yard, with a district's assessor behind a trestle table and the district's own people doing it, and the company standing on it because the district bought the morning off them.

What was on the yard was a trestle table under the canopy with a district assessor sitting behind it and a clip of attendance sheets, and a woman putting out about twenty folding chairs in a row, and about twenty-one men and women from a courier and a merchant and two councils arriving in ones and twos on the pavement.

And at ten past nine, a white car came in and a woman got out of it with a box of booklets under her arm and glasses on a chain.

Yvette Kohl did the whole morning in about two hours and ten minutes and she was, again, the best thing Marek Senn had ever watched a person do with a group of frightened adults in the open air.

She started with a photograph again. A different one this time: a man's legs on a stair and a handrail and a helmet that was not on his head, and she asked who had been on a stair above their own head height in the last year, and about eleven hands went up, and she said *so we are in the same room* and let it sit, and did not push it.

Then she did the thing, and it took forty minutes, and Marek Senn stood at the other end of the canopy with a meter bag at his feet and watched a woman of forty-six teach the fourth beat to twenty-one people on a district's yard, in the sun, with a district assessor taking notes on a clipboard about four yards away.

He had watched her do it once, in a borrowed back room, in week fifteen, with forty people in it, and he had stood against a back wall for two hours and not been able to say a word. This time he had a van behind him and a cord round his neck and four hundred yards of hard standing between him and the group, and it made no difference at all, and the thing that made no difference was the thing he had been writing in a book since the Saturday of week fifteen: it is not a person, it is a shape, and the shape is somebody at the front of a hard thing finishing it with their voice, and it does not matter what is round it, and it does not matter who is standing in the yard watching with a bag at their feet.

He watched a man of about thirty get it wrong three times and she did not correct him on the first two, and on the third she said *you are listening to the load instead of to yourself, and there is nothing on that pallet worth listening to* and he got it right, and about nine of the twenty-one got it right in one go and about eleven needed it twice.

And at about half past eleven, a man of fifty-eight came out of the slab office with a hose and a standpipe and started watering a strip of concrete by the fence, forty feet from the canopy, and worked there for the whole two hours and ten minutes, and did not come over, and did not look, and did not stop.

Aurel Kosta had been on that yard — not that yard; he had been on a yard off the Meridian, a different yard, twenty minutes away — for thirty-one years, and he was on this one on a Wednesday and a Friday because the district lends yards and crews go where they are told, and he was watering a strip of concrete by a fence for two hours and ten minutes so that he did not have to stand in a canopy with twenty-one strangers and a woman with a lanyard.

Marek Senn stood at the other end of the canopy for two hours and ten minutes and could not go over either, for the same reason and the other reason, and the two reasons were that Kosta had told him in week twelve that if he went in and said it came out of a college every man on that yard would know he was wrong, and that a company man standing next to a company instructor in a company lanyard on a district's yard on a district's course is not a man who has come to find out about a load. It is a man who has come to be counted.

At about ten past twelve Marek Senn went and did his work order on a customer's rig in the corner of that yard, and a minute afterwards, a woman came past him with a trestle table leg over her shoulder.

"Excuse me."

He got out of the way.

"Thank you." She set the leg down, and straightened up, and looked at him, and it was Pauline Ncube from the compound off the Meridian, and she said, "You are the technician who filled the form."

"Yes."

"Then I am not going to say anything about it," said Pauline Ncube, "because there is a district assessor at that table and I have got nine confirmations on a list from Thursday morning and I am not going to have my name and yours in the same conversation in front of a person with a clipboard. But I am going to say one thing, because you did it properly on a tailgate in the rain and I have thought about it all week.

"The woman. Mbeki. I have not rung her. My company has not rung her. There is a note on her file that says she has not got a card, and I asked for that note to be taken off, and it stays, and I have stopped asking." She shifted the leg. "That is all. I have got a table to build."

"Right."

"And you are on this course."

"Thursday."

"Then I will not say anything about that either," said Pauline Ncube, and went and built a table.

---

The register was on the trestle table from about half past nine to about twenty past one, in a clip, face up, and about four feet from where Marek Senn stood twice, and it had a printed grid on it and it had names in it.

He looked at it for about a second and a half on the first pass and about two seconds on the second, and he did not read a name off it, and it was the easiest thing he has ever not done and the hardest thing he has done since Sunday.

On Sunday he had said to a woman, in nine minutes, on a telephone in a shop in Lattice Ward, that there was a list in his employer's building with names on it, and that she could not have it, and that the reason was a boy of twenty and a job that ends on the last Friday of week thirty. And on the Tuesday of week twenty the list was on a trestle table under a canopy on a district's yard for four hours, with a woman in a district's coat sitting behind it, and there was no lock on it and no reason on earth why a technician should not walk over and read one line of it.

He did not read a name off it. He wrote it down that night in the way it had to be written down, and it is on the page, and it is the second time in two weeks that a thing he has refused has turned up in a room he was standing in, and the first time it turned up it was a sentence in a room of forty-one and a better man than him had read it out.

---

Yvette Kohl found him at about twenty past one, at the far end of the canopy, where he had been standing with his hands behind his back for an hour and forty minutes, and she had a box of booklets under her arm and no lanyard on, and she looked considerably better without it.

"You stood at the back for two hours in a back room in week fifteen," she said, "and you did not ask me a single question then either."

"No."

"You have not asked me one now either. I have been watching you for about an hour and a half from about two hundred yards and you have not asked me one, and you have not moved, and I have done this about three hundred times and I can tell you that a man standing at the end of a canopy not asking me anything is either a man who wants to ask me something or a man who has worked out that he is not allowed to, and I have no idea which you are and I have decided I do not want to know." She put the box down. "You are the one from the Saturday. The man who failed an exam in this building in the September intake. I looked at you for about four seconds and I have been looking at you for about four seconds."

"Yes."

"What do you want."

"I want the number," said Marek Senn.

She did not answer for about three seconds, and she did not pretend she had not heard, which he had not expected.

"That is twice in a fortnight," said Yvette Kohl. "You asked me in a market and I said no and I gave you a reason, and you have not been rude about it and you have not gone and asked somebody else, and I want to say that I noticed that as well, because about four people a year do that."

"Three times," said Marek Senn. "This is the third time and I am going to keep asking and you are going to keep saying no, and neither of us is joking, and I would like to be clear about that now so that we do not have to pretend about it in a car park in a fortnight."

"All right," said Yvette Kohl. "Then I will be clear about the other half. **I have a number and it is real and I have said it in rooms where somebody wrote it down, and every single time I have said it to a person on their own in a doorway I have watched a thing go out of their face, and the thing that goes out of their face is the question.** That is the whole of my professional life and I have not found a way round it and I have stopped looking. I am not withholding it from you. I am withholding it from the next four hundred people I am going to stand in front of, because they are going to have heard it, and there is no version of a corridor in which hearing it and not hearing it feels the same.

"And I am going to say the other thing, which is the part I actually want to say, and then I am going to go and finish. You are not asking me because you want to know how many accidents the runs used to have. Everybody who asks me for that number wants it to be a number about a man. **You want to know whether it is the thing you have been looking at since the summer, and if it is, then you have spent fourteen weeks on a thing that a good woman did on purpose, in eleven years, with a clipboard.** And I have watched you stand at the end of a canopy for an hour and forty minutes and I would rather you spent those hundred minutes somewhere with a person in it. Go and eat something. There is a van with a flask in it and you have been in the sun since nine."

He was not able to answer that for about four seconds, and she watched him not answer it, and picked the box up, and went.

---

They finished at about ten past four because the light went.

The certificates were typed on a machine on a trestle table under the canopy and typed by a woman of about sixty called Maureen who had typed them in a borrowed back room off a kitchen in week fifteen, and Kohl called her name out to the room before she started and said *that is Maureen and she is the reason any of this is a document instead of a promise*, and Maureen said *it is a typewriter, love*, and about nine people laughed.

Twenty-one certificates. **Twenty-eight people had sat down on the top floor of that building on the Monday morning and twenty-one of them were standing under a canopy on the Tuesday afternoon, and the seven who were not had gone home, and there is a sheet somewhere with a tick on it against seven names, and nobody has rung any of them, and Darla Vint would say that she had been saying so for nine years and that the column it goes in is the one a man changed on Monday in a hall off a corridor.**

One of the twenty-one had been typed twice because the first one had a spelling wrong on it, and Maureen kept the spoiled one and put it in her bag, and Kohl did not say anything about that either.

Marek Senn was behind the end of a wagon at about forty feet, with a meter bag at his feet, and a woman of about thirty came out of the canopy with a card in her hand and got into a car and did not close the door straight away, and he read it upside down from four feet away in a second and a half.

> *This certificate confirms that the named holder has completed both modules of the 4-19 supervised release course, and that the holder is able to perform and confirm a release where the conditions for release are not within the holder's own judgement. The holder is competent to confirm scope to a learner.*

He has read that sentence forty-one times. He read it once more, upside down, in the sun, from behind a wagon, on the Tuesday afternoon of week twenty, and then a boy of twenty came out of the canopy holding a card in both hands the way a person holds a thing they have not decided about, and looked about forty feet past the whole of the yard and at nothing at all, and went out through the gate on foot without putting it in his pocket, and Marek Senn stood behind the wagon and did not say a word to him and did not go over, and that was the correct thing to do, and it took everything he had.

There is no version of this where the man who has refused nine times all term says anything at all on the afternoon a boy of twenty walks out of a canopy with a card in his hand and has had it in a drawer in a room in Sennet Row for one hour, and Marek Senn had worked that out at a postbox on a corner at half past ten on the Saturday morning in about four minutes, and he had said it out loud to a boy of twenty who was about to put four hundred and forty pounds into the same box, and it had been: *if I say one word about it I will be the fourth thing in the room, and you will be the man who got the boy his card, and that is a job and there is nobody in this city whose job it is.*

Petar Vance was not there on the Tuesday. Dunleavy was not there. There was nobody on that yard that Marek Senn had any business talking to except Aurel Kosta, who was at the standpipe with a hose, and who did not look up, and who stayed at the standpipe for another forty minutes after the canopy was empty and the trestle table was folded and the register had gone into a clip and a satchel and a car.

Marek Senn got into the van at about twenty past four and drove to the depot and put the van on the charge and stood in the yard for about four minutes, and then he got out his notebook and wrote three lines in the small upright hand, and they were the whole of what he wrote that day, and the second of them was this:

*Aurel Kosta did not look up. He was at the standpipe for two hours ten minutes and again for forty minutes after the canopy was empty, on a company course day, with a company instructor on his yard, having said in a shop in week twelve that there is no school in it and it is a yard thing. He has not been contradicted. He has been made less true by about two hours and by a woman with a lanyard and he did not say one word and neither did I, and I was forty feet away, and I have not got a way of paying it down.*

---

*72. Tuesday of week 20. A yard, a canopy, a trestle table, and a hundred and forty minutes at the far end of it with a bag at my feet.*

*What I watched.* The same thing I watched in a back room in week fifteen, in the sun, this time for twenty-one people on a district's yard, taught by the same woman, and it is the same technique, and it took forty minutes, and she is not doing it on purpose, and there is no version of the sentence where she is. **I have now watched it four times — a school, a borrowed back room, a plate in a customer's storeroom, a canopy on a yard — and it is the same forty minutes every single time, and the thing I have been calling a hinge for fourteen weeks is the most reliable forty minutes in this city.** I am not writing that down as a discovery. I am writing it down because in about six weeks somebody is going to say to me that the technique is new, or that the omission was chosen, or that somebody in an office put it there, and I am going to have to say: I have watched it four times and it is the same forty minutes and the person doing it does not know, and being careful is the part of it, not the design. I have not got anywhere near being able to say that in a room and I am not going to be able to for a while.

*The register.* It was on a trestle table from half past nine until twenty past one, face up, in a clip, with names in it, and there was no reason on earth why a technician should not have walked over and read one line of it, and I did not, twice, and I want it on the page because on Sunday I told a woman on a telephone that I could see it and that she could not have it. **It was four feet from me for four hours and the reason I did not read a name off it is the reason I said no to her, and the reason is a boy of twenty and a job that ends on the last Friday of week thirty, and I have not seen that boy since a postbox on Saturday morning and I did not need to see him, and that is either a rule or a habit and I genuinely do not know which.**

*What I got wrong, in public, on a trestle table, and would like back.* On the Wednesday I wrote a name in a box on a sheet on a wall in a prefab office and told a boy of nineteen that the reason was a pen. On the Tuesday of week nineteen I told a boy of nineteen that the box was his and that a record could be used. **Both of those were a man with a lanyard deciding which side of a thing a man in a hi-vis was on, on company time, in a customer's yard, and neither of them was asked for, and the boy is nineteen and it is now on a sheet in a company-customer building, and I have not asked him a single question about either of them.** A man of fifty-eight worked out in about four seconds what that box was going to cost and told me in a prefab office, with the kettle on, that it would not cost me and would not cost him, and he was right, and it cost me anyway, in a different currency, in a yard in Ashfields on a Thursday afternoon in the rain, and I did not see it coming and I would not have seen it if I had been looking.

*What is on the Thursday.* Third time asked, third time refused, and I have put it on the page in the way she would want it put on the page: she has a number, it is real, she says it in rooms where somebody writes it down, and she has watched a question go out of a face every time she has said it to a person in a doorway. And she said one thing to me on a canopy that I have not been able to stop hearing, and I am writing it in her words because if I put it in mine it will come out sounding like a thing I have been working on: **you want to know whether it is the thing you have been looking at since the summer, and if it is, then you have spent fourteen weeks on a thing that a good woman did on purpose, in eleven years, with a clipboard.** She does not know that she is right. I have known since the Saturday of week fifteen. I am not going to tell her on a canopy and I am not going to tell her in a month and I am not going to tell her on a Monday at ten past six on a bank, and the reason I am not going to tell her is that I do not know what I would do with it afterwards and I have not got a second thing to do it with.
