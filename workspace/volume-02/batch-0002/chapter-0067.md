# Chapter 67 — Four Words

The Saturday of week nineteen was the first Saturday since the Friday of week twelve that Marek Senn was in the shop from eight in the morning and it was the first one in about four months that was not a day of somebody else's, and it took him about twenty minutes to work out that he was enjoying it and about another ten to work out why.

He was enjoying it because his contract is four days, Monday to Thursday, and Saturday is not one of them, and a man who has been paid nothing on a date since the week before a term has been handed a Saturday by a four-sided piece of paper, and has not thought about it once.

He had a job booked for the Tuesday and nothing on for the Saturday and he had come in at eight because the bench is warmer than the flat and because it was a Saturday.

---

Rafi Pell came in at about ten with a rucksack and a hood up and stood in the doorway for a second so that Marek Senn could see the whole of the get-up: the rucksack, a new-ish pair of work gloves tucked in the side pocket, a hi-vis rolled and jammed under the strap, and a hard hat on top of all of it, carried rather than worn, which is a thing men do.

"I'm going."

"You are going."

"It's the yard off the Meridian. New Saturday. Twelve pounds and the voucher." He shifted the rucksack. "I'm on at eleven. Don't ring me about anything before about six, and if you ring me at six while I'm on a gate, I will not be able to hear it and I will be annoyed with you for having rung."

"I will not ring you before six."

"You will ring me at ten past six."

"I will ring you at ten past six on a Monday and I will not mention it," said Marek Senn. "And I am not going to say the thing I have said nine times, and I am not going to ask you about the sleeve, and I have noticed the hard hat and I am not going to say anything about the hard hat."

"It's for the roof of the wagon."

"I know what it is for."

Rafi Pell looked at the floor for a second, the way a person does.

"Right," he said. "Well. That's the whole of me. I'm working Saturdays now, which is — it's a Saturday thing, it's twelve pounds, it's not the six weeks." He said the last part quickly, to get it out, and Marek Senn did not react at all, because in week sixteen Rafi Pell had put a sentence in a book in a market with an order to it, on purpose, so that nobody could pretend the two were the same thing, and that sentence was six weeks old now and it had not gone anywhere. "I've never said the first one out loud to you. I have said it to nobody out loud. It is in a book with Bea's name on it and that is where it is, and you did that, and I have not forgotten, and I have not said thank you, and I am not going to say thank you on a Saturday morning with a hard hat."

"Nobody said thank you."

"Nobody ever says thank you," said Rafi Pell. "Six o'clock. Monday."

He was gone, and the bell took about four seconds longer than it should have.

---

Marek Senn walked down to the postbox on the corner at about half past ten to put a receipt in it, because a receipt is a thing you put in a postbox and it is the most ordinary errand in the world and he has put about nine hundred of them in that box.

Tam Arrick was standing at the box with his back to the street and an envelope in both hands.

He had a hood on and a coat with the zip done up and he had been standing there long enough that there was a mark on the pavement, and he did not turn round when Marek Senn came up the road, and he said, without turning round:

"Don't."

"I have not said anything."

"You were about to." He put his thumb under the flap. "Right. I am going to do it now and I want you to stand about four feet away and not say one word for about ten seconds, and then I am going to tell you the two things, and then I am going to go, and I have got the bus at eleven and I have got a shift at two."

Marek Senn stood about four feet away and said nothing for about eleven seconds while Tam Arrick put his thumb in the slot and pushed.

The envelope went in with a small noise. It was a buff envelope and it was about the thickness of three sheets and it had a window in it, and through the window Marek Senn could see the top of a printed form, and at the bottom right of the window there was a box, and the box was empty, and the box was the one that said *employer*.

Inside the envelope, on the top sheet, in the second box down on the left, there had been a postal order for four hundred and forty pounds, and a photograph, and a signature, and a date, and in the fourth box down on the left there was a question and it had a line under it and the line was blank.

"The employer box is empty," said Tam Arrick, to the box.

"I noticed."

"It's got a woman at the training place who rang me on the Wednesday and read me back *not in employment* in a nice way and then said *that's fine, most of the ones on this one are*, and I have thought about that sentence for about ten days and I have decided that I am going to have it on the form for ever." He turned round. "The two things. One. I stood here on Monday morning at a quarter to eight and I could not do it. I got the bus to the end of this road and I walked back. I have not told anybody that and I am telling you, and you are not going to write it down, and if you ever mention it I will buy a different one. I could not do it on the Monday because I could not do it on the Monday, and I have no more reason than that, and I have had five days to find one and there is not one."

"That is fine."

"It is not fine, it is a fact, and I have made the decision anyway, and the second thing is the one I actually came down the road to say and I am saying it now before my bus."

He put his hands in his coat.

"There is a box on that form, fourth down, on the left, and it says *declaration — any condition, injury or medication affecting your ability to work at height*, and under it it says that if you declare a condition you may be called for an assessment before the practical module, and that declaring does not affect your fee. And I have got to write in it: left forearm, two tendons repaired, incomplete, no grip in the middle finger since before the term started, three months' recovery, cleared for light duties only and a doctor in Sennet Row has it in writing and will say so if anybody rings him.

"And the box is empty. I looked at it on Monday and I looked at it on Thursday and it is empty, and I have decided to post it with the box empty, and I am telling you that I have decided it and not that I have forgotten it, because I have not forgotten it, and I am not asking you what to put in it and I know that you are not going to tell me what to put in it, because you told me in this shop on the Thursday of week sixteen that you were not going to and I have been holding you to it for three weeks and you have not slipped once.

"So the price is four hundred and forty pounds, which I have, and two days off the kitchen, which Deb has put me down for, and a form with a blank box on it, and in about six months somebody in a building off the roundabout is going to read that form and not know that my hand does not close. **I have decided that the blank box is worth it, and it is worth it to me and not to anybody else, and there is nobody in this city I could ask who would be on my side about it, so I have asked you, and you have not said anything, and I am going to the bus.**"

He got about two steps.

"Tam."

"Don't."

"One thing and then I will not say another thing about it for a month." Marek Senn did not move off the pavement. "I have been an employee of the company that sells that course since Monday. There is a version of this form that costs four hundred and forty pounds to you and nothing to anybody, and there is a version of this form that costs four hundred and forty pounds to nobody because a company pays for it, and the difference between those two is one box and a word, and the word is *employer*, and I have a lanyard round my neck that I could have gone to a man in about four minutes on Friday and asked him whether the form takes a trading name in that box, and he would have told me, because I have started and the fifth clause of my contract is that I take the course, and I have thought about doing it every day since Monday and I have not done it and I am not going to, and I wanted you to know that I thought about it, and that is the whole of the thing and I am not going to say another word about it for a month."

Tam Arrick stood on a pavement in Lattice Ward and looked at him.

"You are the fourth thing in the room," he said.

"I am the fourth thing in the room," said Marek Senn, "and I have a badge, and I thought about using it, and I did not, and the reason I did not is not a principle, it is that if I do it once then in a year I will do it for somebody and I will not have said no to it in the first place. That is the whole of my method and it is about eleven words long and it is not a rule, and I have not got it written down anywhere, and if I ever do I would like you to know that you were the first person I said it to."

"The bus is at eleven," said Tam Arrick.

"Your bus is at eleven and my shop is open and I have nothing in it, and I am not going to follow you to it."

"Good." He pulled his hood up. "Monday of week twenty. Both days. And you are not to say one word about it, and I am going to come out with a card with my name typed on it, and I am not going to be the only man in the room who has got one, because Rafi Pell has got one in a plastic sleeve and Reuben Daw is about to have one, and I have been reading about all three of you like a book and I am the one with the two cut tendons, and I have made my peace with that in five weeks and I did not make it with anybody's help."

He walked off towards the bus stop with his hands in his pockets, and he did not look back, and Marek Senn stood at a postbox on a corner in Lattice Ward and put a receipt in it and then stood there for another minute with his hand on the cold iron.

---

Reuben Daw rang the shop at about two o'clock on the Saturday afternoon of week nineteen.

"I am on the fish office gate," he said. "There is one phone and it is in a hut and the man lets you use it if you have got a pack of your own, and I have not got a pack of my own, and he has let me use it, and I have got about four minutes."

"Go."

"The Saturdays are back."

Marek Senn stood at the bench and did not sit down.

"Six of them," said Reuben Daw. "The contract starts again. Three months, Saturdays, and it is the same money as before, which is not much, and it is the same foreman, and his name is Errol and he has been there twenty years and he is not a bad man, and that is the part I cannot get round. He is not a bad man. He said a thing in a doorway on the Wednesday of week fourteen and it was four words and it was not said to be said, and the Saturdays went, and that was the whole of it, and there was nothing behind it and nobody sent anything and no form went anywhere. Six Saturdays. That is what six weeks is. I have been counting them on a card in my wallet and the card is the only place in the world where that has been counted, and it has got six marks on it, and the sixth of them is the one I lost last.

"And they are back, and it is today, and I rang you from a fish office gate because you are the only person I have told anything to in six weeks and I told you on a Sunday in the wind and you did not write it down, and that is the reason you are the one I am ringing on a Saturday in a hut with a man waiting for his pack of his own back."

"What did he say," said Marek Senn. "The four words. I have never asked you and I am asking now because it is the sixth one and I am not going to have you carry it to a seventh."

There was about four seconds of a hut.

"The way out was *he's at the college now*," said Reuben Daw. "That is four words. In a doorway. On a Wednesday afternoon in under a minute, and I was standing there, and he was on his way to his car, and he was not even talking to me, he was talking past me. And I had been on that yard nine weeks. Nine weeks, and I have never once told anybody in this city what I did before that and nobody asked, and I have not said the word college about myself since I was sixteen and my mother would have taken the roof off.

"And today is the way back and it is also four words and it is also in a doorway, and it is the same man, and he said: **you'll want the card.**"

"Then what."

"Then he told me the rest of it, which is that the Saturday contract has got a condition on it now, which it had not had at the start of the summer, and the condition is that there is a named holder on site for the release work, and there is one man on that whole Saturday crew who has got a card and he is on Wednesdays. And there is a refresher that comes to the yard on a Saturday morning and it takes about two hours and it is free and it is done by a woman off a lanyard, and if you are a Saturday man then it is not on anybody's clock, because a Saturday man is not on a clock. And it is the fourth Saturday of a month, and it is one Saturday."

Marek Senn did not say anything for about two seconds.

"Reuben. Say the arithmetic back to me. Slowly."

"He said it. I have said it." The wind came into the hut. "Six back. One to go and get the card. Five. **I lost six on a Wednesday in a doorway for a word, and I am giving one back on a Saturday in a doorway for a card, and I am five, and I was six, and I have a card.**"

"Yes."

"That is the arithmetic," said Reuben Daw. "That is what I have got. And I have not rung you to be told what to do, and I have not rung you to be asked whether it is worth it, because I worked it out on a gate on Friday night and I have had a night, and I am going to do it, and I have not got anybody to tell and that is the whole of why I am on this phone.

"And the last part is the part I have not said to anybody and I am saying it to you because you are in a shop and I am on a gate, and neither of us is going to be standing next to the other one when it happens. It is worse going back. That is the thing. Going out, nobody asked me for anything. I stood in a doorway and a man talked past me and six Saturdays went and there was nothing in it for me to do and nobody wanted anything off me at any point in the whole of it. Coming back, I have to give a Saturday to be told that I am allowed to be on a wagon, and the man who says that to me is the same man who took it, and he is not a bad man, and **he did not have to do it either.** That is what I cannot get past. There is no rule that says the yard has to be kind to me on a Saturday and there is no rule that says it has to be cruel, and he has been neither, and the six weeks are the same six weeks either way, and now I have to hand one of them over to be let back in with a card in my pocket.

"And I am going to do it, and I have wanted to do it since about half past nine this morning before I had even got to the gate, and I have written that down as well because I know what you are like."

"How long have you got on that phone."

"About a minute." Reuben Daw's voice did not change at all. "One more thing, because you will not ask and I have got a minute. The boy. The one at the post off the Meridian, the one you were stood next to on the Wednesday with your cord on. He is filling the sheet in. Every release. His name and the time. I saw the sheet on the Thursday morning when I went for the van and there are nine entries on it and they are in his writing and they are not neat and they are all there.

"I asked him what happens if he writes one and somebody says he got it wrong. He said the fella would say so and it would be changed. And I said but if the fella is the one you were waiting for, how do you know he is the right fella, and he said he does not, and he said it the way you say something you have been thinking about for three weeks, and Marek — that boy has spent three weeks not being allowed to finish a thing on his own and he has just been handed a pen and a reason and he is doing it, and I do not think anybody is going to ask him whether that was a good idea, and I have written his name nowhere and I am not going to."

"That was the right call," said Marek Senn.

"I know. That is why I have told nobody." The line clicked. "Right. I have got to give the man's his fags back. And Marek — you are on a rota now. You are on Mondays and Thursdays with nobody in that building. If I am ever in a doorway and I need somebody to be there, I will not ring you, and I want that on the book, because I have watched you go into four rooms in six weeks and I am not putting you in a fifth."

The line went.

Marek Senn stood at the bench for a minute with the phone in his hand and then put it down and did not write anything down for about eleven minutes, which is the longest he has gone in a book.

---

Dessa Kwan rang at about four.

He knew who it was before she said anything, because of the rule she set for herself in a shop on the Monday morning of week sixteen and has not broken.

"You told me you would ring me before you handed anything over," said Marek Senn. "You have not handed anything over."

"I have not handed anything over. I have read it out to myself about nine times and I have got to the end of it twice, and the second time I got to the end of it and I shut it and I did not want to ring you, and then I remembered that I had said I would, and if I had not rung you then the not ringing would be the first thing I did with it and I did not want that to be the first thing." She was in a kitchen; he could hear a machine. "So I am ringing you, and I am going to read you about nine hundred words and you are not going to say anything, and at the end I am going to tell you something about thirty plastic chairs and you are going to like the chairs less than the nine hundred words."

"Go."

She read it. It was about a Saturday morning in the back room of a building four hundred people a week walk past to get to a kettle, and about forty people who had taken a Saturday off work to be told they were not stupid, and about nine of them who did not have a job, and about the fact that a card with a name typed on it is evidence that you are safe, and that in about six months somebody somewhere was going to ask an employer whether a woman had done the release course, and the answer was going to be yes, and the thing that got her hurt was going to be the thing that got her the card.

Marek Senn listened to the whole of it without saying a word.

"Right," said Dessa Kwan. "That is nine hundred words and it is not finished and I am not going to finish it, and you are not going to tell me that it is unfair, because I have been waiting since the Saturday of week fifteen for somebody to say that and I have worked out that it would be the cheapest possible thing for you to say and I would have to be grateful, and I have decided I am not going to be grateful to you about anything ever again. So you have not said it. Good.

"Now the chairs, and then I am going back to my kitchen because it is half four.

"Somebody came to the Exchange at about eleven this morning with a van. A woman with a district badge and a list with a tick on it, not a name — a tick, Marek, the same as the woman at our door on the Saturday of week fifteen, and I did not say anything to her about it and she did not say anything to me. And she asked for thirty plastic chairs, and Bea was not in, and Ena Truslove was in and she said yes."

"She said yes."

"She said yes in about four seconds and she asked whether they were going back on the same day and the woman said there was no rush on that, and Ena said there was no rush on that, and the woman wrote *no rush* on the list, and that is it, that is the whole of it. **Nobody from the Fold nursery was asked. Nobody from the Fold nursery is going to be thanked. The chairs are going to a room on the third floor of a building in Bower Street that has nothing in it for eight weeks a year, for a Saturday, for about twenty women on somebody's books.**"

"And Ena knows about the Saturday. Does Ena know what it is for."

"Ena knows there is a Saturday," said Dessa Kwan. "Ena does not know whose, and she was not asked, and she has been a member for eleven years and she put a card in the tin in a room full of people in week sixteen and said in that room that the chairs had gone into a college once already and that nobody had been thanked, and she has now done it a second time inside a month, and she has not been thanked, and I was in the kitchen when the van came and I did not go out, and I want that on your book in my words: **I did not go out.**

"Which is the first time I have not gone out. Eleven years, and I have not gone out once. So you can have that with the nine hundred words."

"I have got it," said Marek Senn. "Both, in your words, and the last one first."

"The last one first," said Dessa Kwan. "Yes. That is the right order. Marek — do not do anything about the chairs."

"I am not going to do anything about the chairs."

"You are not going to do anything about the chairs," said Dessa Kwan, "because there is nothing to do about chairs. There are thirty chairs and there are about twenty women and there is a Saturday. That is the best possible use of thirty plastic chairs in this city and it is going to be done by somebody and the somebody is not going to be a nursery, and if you put your hand anywhere near it then it becomes a thing where a nursery was involved."

"I understand."

"You do not understand, you have understood that shape about four times in a fortnight and it has not once been the right shape," said Dessa Kwan, "and I have not got time. Half four. Kettle."

She put the phone down.

Marek Senn put the phone down and stood at the bench and then turned round and looked at the load book on the shelf by the day book, and it was a quarter past four on a Saturday, and the round was on the Monday at ten past six, and the Monday was in two days, and there were about seven of them left before the year's out, and he would be on that bank in two days as an employee of a company that sold a card that a woman in a wheelchair could not afford.

*70. Saturday of week 19. His, and then the boy's, and both of them in one morning, and I am writing this about both of them and not about me, and I have not done that twice in three weeks.*

*Four words, out.* A foreman in a doorway on the Wednesday of week fourteen, on his way to a car, talking past a man of twenty-two who had been on that yard nine weeks and had not said the word college about himself since he was sixteen. *He's at the college now.* **Nobody asked him for anything. That is the whole of the way out, and I have been carrying it for six weeks as a bad thing that happened to him, and it was not a bad thing that happened to him, it was nothing that happened to him, and the nothing was in a doorway and it was not aimed at him.**

*Four words, back.* Same man. Same doorway. A Saturday in week nineteen. *You'll want the card.* And behind those four words there is a condition on a three-month Saturday contract that was not there at the start of the summer, and one man on that crew who has a card and is on Wednesdays, and a woman with a lanyard who comes to a yard on a Saturday morning and it is not on anybody's clock because a Saturday man is not on a clock. **Six back, one to get the card, five. He lost six Saturdays for a word and he is handing one over for a card and he was six and he is five, and a man of twenty-two is going to be the only man on that Saturday with a card in about a month, and he has wanted to do it since half past nine that morning, before he got to the gate.**

*The way back is worse, and no rule was broken to make it happen.* I have written that out four times in the book as something that was going to be a discovery and it is not a discovery, it is arithmetic. Going out, nobody wanted anything off him. Coming back, he has to give a Saturday to be allowed to be on a wagon, **and the man who says it does not have to say it either, and there is no rule that says the yard has to be kind to him on a Saturday and no rule that says it has to be cruel, and it is the same six weeks either way.** I have been trying since the Tuesday of week fourteen to find a rule that has been broken and there is not one and there is not going to be one, and I think I have been looking for the rule so that I could be the one who says it has been broken. He has worked it out on a gate on a Friday night with a night of notice, and he has told me first, and he has told me first because of a phone call in the wind in week sixteen that I did not write down. That is the correct reason and I am not going to pretend it is anything else.

*What I did not say to either of them, and it took me the whole day not to say it.* A boy posted a form in a buff envelope at a postbox on a corner in Lattice Ward at about half past ten on a Saturday morning, with the *employer* box empty on purpose and the declaration box — any condition, injury or medication affecting your ability to work at height — empty on purpose, and a postal order for four hundred and forty pounds inside it, and a photograph, and a signature, and a form that will go to a room off the roundabout. And a man of twenty-two has agreed to give one Saturday to a morning in a yard so that he can be let back on to six. **One of them is buying a card and one of them is paying for one, and the two of them have never met and they are on opposite sides of this city and they are both writing to me, and I know what it looks like, and I have written it here so that somebody can see that I knew, and I am not going to do one single thing with it.** That is not a pattern. That is two Saturdays.

*I have a badge.* I have thought about using it. There is one move available to me on either of those two facts and it takes about four minutes and a man with an interest in my keeping his technician, and I have thought about it every day since the Monday. I have not done it, and I told the boy on a pavement at half past ten that I had thought about it and that the reason is not a principle but that I will not be able to say no to it the second time if I say yes to it the first, and that is the whole of my method and it is about eleven words long and I have not written it anywhere and I have not got a rule. He said *you are the fourth thing in the room* and I said yes. That is the entire exchange and it took about four seconds and it is the closest I have come in fourteen weeks to saying the thing out loud, and it was said to a man buying a card on a pavement, and neither of us will ever mention it.

*Do not know.* Whether the boy at the post off the Meridian is being helped or being used. He is filling in the box. Nine entries and not neat and all of them there. I put my own name in that box once on the Wednesday to keep the first line off him and I did it because a boy of nineteen handed me a pen, and on the Thursday a man of fifty-eight told me in a prefab office with the kettle on that the reason the box has been empty for thirty-one years is that nobody wanted anything off that yard badly enough to make a box, and that now somebody wants something, and it will not be me and it will not be him and it will be a boy of nineteen, and he worked that out in about four seconds standing next to a man he has never had a conversation with. **I have made a mechanism and the mechanism is running and there is nobody whose job it is to ask the boy whether he wanted it.** I have written that sentence and I have not deleted it and I am not going to, and it is going to be the last line of the year if I have anything to do about it.
