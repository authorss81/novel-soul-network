# Chapter 63 — Good Engineering

Petar Vance was on the depot floor at seven twenty on the Tuesday morning of week nineteen with a mug of tea and the service sheets for the district, and he did not look surprised to see a twenty-two-year-old standing at the key board with his own name in the box on a Monday sheet, and Marek Senn understood before anybody said anything that Vance had been in this building for eleven years and had known what was on the floor for eleven years and had never asked.

"Hand set," said Vance.

"Out of date. I should have had it on the bench on the first Monday."

"It should have been on the bench on the first Monday by somebody who was not going to be in nine buildings by nine o'clock, and that has not happened in the eleven years I have been here, so do not get a complex about it. I have got two in the workshop." He handed over four sheets. "You are with me until about two. After two I am on Ashfields and you are wherever Dunleavy puts you."

"What does Dunleavy put me in."

"He will put you on the road and he will not tell you why and the reason is that he has four technicians and two of them are off and the third one is on the second module next week." Vance squared the sheets. "He has never been unkind to anybody in this building in nine years and he does not intend to start, so do not go looking for it."

They were out at twenty past eight.

Marek Senn had four hours of it before the telephone rang and about ninety seconds of the ninety in which he enjoyed it, and he counted the ninety afterwards, in the book, and wrote the number down, because he wanted somebody to know how long it lasted and not to be told it was longer.

The ninety seconds were in a stairwell in Ashfields.

They had been called to a third-floor store in a building a letting agent had emptied over eight years, and the unit was on a landing with a light on it that was not on, and the fault was a supply unit that had been switched on and off in a power cut and had come back with a hold open. There was a customer man in a shirt with no name on it, and a manager in a corridor on a telephone, and eleven other people in that building who had to get past them, and the corridor was the only way through, and a plate on a landing is not a thing anybody looks at.

Marek Senn put his hand on the housing and ran the release twice, and the fault was obvious in about a second and he could have had the panel off in another ten, and instead he found that he was aware of the corridor, and the people in it, and the fact that a man behind him had stopped, and that if he had had a hand on a stack in that corridor instead of a plate in a housing he would have been in exactly the same shape of problem with a different thing in his hand. And the thing he was in the middle of — a supply unit, a landing, an eight-year-old void — was the same shape of problem as four sets of steps up to a wagon at a yard off the Meridian, and it was the same shape as a man who cannot hold a kettle, and it was the same shape as a man of fifty-eight who said a word out loud to himself on a wagon and took his hands off, and it was the same shape as a woman of thirty-three who said there is nobody in it who can say no.

The plate asked for the load. The plate did the first three steps. The plate waited four seconds for a voice that was not coming, and on the fourth second it did the thing that the small card on the side of the housing said it would do, which was that it told the machine at the top of the stairs that the release had been given, and the machine at the top of the stairs was a winding drum on a rig that had been in a void for eight years with a load on it that was not a load but a weight, forty kilos of steel plate, hung on a strop at head height, that a fitter had put there in a fortnight in the year the building was cleared and nobody had cut it down.

A weight, on a strop, in a dead building, for eight years. Nobody was under it. If anybody had been under it, that is the last instruction on a card in this trade — if no word, withdraw and report — and the machine does it, and it is right, and the person under it is dead.

"Mind the top of that," he said, and the man in the shirt with no name on it said, "Oh. Right. Sorry. Is that live."

"It's dead power and it's live weight," said Marek Senn. "That's a different thing and there is no card in this trade for the second one, and I want you to write on your own sheet that I said that, and I am going to spell it for you while you write it."

He wrote it. It took about ninety seconds. And then the fitter came up and cut the strop down and it took four minutes and nobody was hurt, and Marek Senn went back down the stairs feeling like a man who had done about four good hours' work in a morning.

The four minutes before that were the ninety seconds, and then it turned, and the way it turned was that the customer manager, in the corridor, said: "Are you the one who did the demonstration? At the college. The one on the notice."

"No."

"Someone said there was a man from a college coming round doing a demo and asking people questions. Are you not him?"

"I did a refresher on Monday morning. I sat at the back. I have not done a demonstration and I am not going to, and if anybody has told you I did one then they have told you something that did not happen."

The manager looked at him and then at the lanyard, which was round his neck on a green cord with a photograph on it, and Marek Senn watched the man decide, in about a second and a half, that the man with the lanyard was somebody official, and watched what happened in the man's face when the answer was no.

"That'll be the fella from the other company," said the manager. "Sorry. He had a card as well. Same sort of card. You're all the same colour."

And he went back to his telephone.

That is the whole of it, and it is the whole of the ninety seconds and the whole of the four hours, and it took one and a half seconds at the top of a stairwell in Ashfields and it has been true for eleven years.

He did not say anything about it in the van. He did not say anything about it to Vance, who was two streets away with a mug of tea and eleven years of knowing. He wrote a line on the customer's sheet that said the release routine was verified and that the strop was cut down and that the site should be told in writing that there is no card in the trade for a dead power and a live weight, and he wrote the customer's man's spelling of it next to his own, because Vance had taught him that in about four minutes on the Tuesday of week fourteen and Vance had never mentioned teaching it.

Then Vance rang at about eleven.

They were parked outside a laundry in Saltmarket, which was a job that took forty minutes, which was finished, and Vance came round the van and put his elbows on the roof.

"Right," he said. "Two things and then I am going to Ashfields. One. The yard off the Meridian, the one past the grain terminal. Dunleavy's got a call-out list for the Wednesday and you are on it, and I am on it, and I put us both on it, and I am going to tell you that I put us both on it and I am not going to tell you why, and you can work it out or you can not."

Marek Senn put the hand set on the passenger seat.

"What is on that yard."

"Two units on the wagon-side steps and a beacon on a control post that has been failing intermittently since the summer and that four people have reported." Vance took his elbows off the roof. "I have been to that yard about thirty times in eleven years and I have never once been asked a question by a man in the office, and the office is a prefab with a kettle in it and a chair and a window, and there is about forty men on that yard, and I have never once been asked who they are."

"Who are they."

"That is not the question and you know it is not the question," said Petar Vance. "I have a district, and a district has about nine crews, and the crews have names, and if a man on a yard is not one of my nine crews then he belongs to somebody who is not me, and this city has about four companies and a district and a college. You are not going to walk in there and read a card out loud. I have thought about that for eleven years and I have never once done it and I have never once regretted it, and I want to say the reason, and then I am going to Ashfields.

"Because the moment a service engineer from a supply company stands in a yard office and says anything about how a load gets released, that yard is never going to be the same yard again. It does not matter what he says. It does not even matter whether he is right. **A card on a wall is a card on a wall, and the second it is a thing a man came and talked about, it is a thing somebody can be asked about, and the yard will spend the next two years being asked about it by people who have never been on it, and the man who came will be the reason.** I said that to your friend Oren Vey in a corridor at the start of the month and he wrote it on the back of his hand. I have not thought about it since, which is how I know it is true."

"What is the second thing."

"The second thing is about you, and I am going to say it once and then I am going to Ashfields, and if you bring it up again I will tell you I do not remember." He looked at the hand set on the passenger seat and then at the lanyard. "There is no clause in this company, in this district, in this city, that lets you stand in a room with one of our plates and run a practice. Not alone and not with a customer. Not for four minutes. I asked a woman in standards on your behalf — not for you, I want that on record, I asked her as a service engineer because I did not think of it and then I found out three days later and I have been angry with myself since, because I did not think of it. She wrote back four lines and it was no.

"And I have been in this building for eleven years and I have watched about nine men come in here and get bad at their jobs in about four months, and not one of them because they were told something they did not know. They got bad at their jobs because there is a shape to a morning in this trade and the shape is comfortable, and you are going to be in that shape from today, and there is nobody in that building who is going to tell you that you have stopped checking things, because telling you that you have stopped checking things is not in anybody's job, and because on a Monday and a Thursday there is nobody in the building at all."

"Petar—"

"You have got a couple of weeks before your card and I am going to tell you one thing about it and then I am going to Ashfields, and I am telling you because you are going to be working with a man who has one and I would rather you heard it from me in a car park in Saltmarket than worked it out on a yard." Vance put his mug on the roof of the van. "On a Monday morning in about four months, or a Thursday, or a Tuesday, a person is going to come to you in a corridor with a clipboard and a question, and the question is going to sound like a small thing, and it is going to be exactly the shape of the thing you are good at. And you are going to be able to answer it, because you are the best technician this depot has had in about four years, including the two who are off, and I have watched you for two days and I have not seen you get anything wrong and I have seen you get a strop down that has been up for eight years.

"And on that morning, in that corridor, there is going to be a voice in your head that is a woman's and it is going to say the third step, and it is going to be the right thing to say, and it is not going to be you. And you are going to say it anyway."

He got in his car.

"That is the last free thing you are going to get off me," said Petar Vance, through the window. "I have been a service engineer for eleven years and I have never said that to a man and I have not said it to you in a room, and I have said it in a car park in a lay-by in a town nobody comes to, and if you tell anybody I said it I will put your hand set back on the bench."

"I am not going to tell anybody."

"I know. That is why I said it."

Talia Venn rang the shop at twenty past twelve on the Tuesday of week nineteen and Marek Senn was in a van on a forecourt in Nine Bridges with the engine off and a cup of something from a machine, and he answered it on the second ring because he knew what the second ring meant by then.

"Fourteen minutes," he said.

"You have been counting since about half past four in the morning and I have been awake since six, and neither of those is a defence of anything, so neither of us is going to use it." She was in a corridor; he could hear a door and a trolley and a floor that was not a hospital floor. "I am going to say two things and then I am going to go and do a round, and if I say the second one quickly it is because I have to be on a ward in ten minutes and not because it is smaller."

"Go."

"One. Keogh rang me on the Wednesday of week seventeen. It was about a statement on the practice on the round — the round, yours, the one at the Cutbank with the nineteen men, and a woman on it, and I have never been to it and never seen it. She asked me a question and I gave her an answer inside about four seconds, and the answer is the true one, and I have not said it to you and I am telling you now that I am not going to, and I would like it in whatever it is you keep that you did not ask me for it.

"And then she asked me to evidence the count. Which is the thing I told you in week fifteen would happen in about four weeks and would happen to me and not to you, and I did say no, and I used the sentence I told you I would use, and I have now used it twice in three weeks and both times it was the true sentence and both times I was the only person in the room who knew there was nothing to evidence.

"That is one. I have not told you about it before because I did not want you to come, and you did not come, and you said you would not, and I want to say that I noticed. And you have not asked me what I said to her, and I noticed that as well, and it is the second time in a year you have not asked me something."

"You did not ask me either," said Marek Senn.

"No. I did not. There is a difference between the two of us and it is not in my favour, and I have spent a fortnight deciding whether to ring you and tell you that I know what your face does when you are not asking somebody something, and I decided that I would not, because you would have heard it as a favour."

Marek Senn sat in the van on a forecourt with a cup going cold in his hand and did not say the first thing that came into his head, which was the true sentence, and she said the second thing before he could.

"Talia."

"I have not finished. Two, and it is quick, and then I have to go and do a round." Her voice did not change at all. "Your lanyard."

He did not answer for about four seconds.

"You have a lanyard," said Talia Venn, "and it is round your neck and it has got somebody's name on it and it gets you through a door, and I have a registration number and I cannot get into a yard in this city without somebody deciding I should. And I am not saying that out loud to be cruel and I am not asking you for anything, and I have never once in fourteen weeks asked you for anything that I could have got out of you by being difficult, and you know that, and that is why I am able to say this sentence in nine seconds instead of nine weeks.

"And the thing I have not said, and am not going to say, and am telling you now that I am not going to say, is that the thing I have been refused for two years is a list. Not a form, not a letter, not a certificate. A list. A register of the people in this city who can be asked whether a release was confirmed and who was the one who confirmed it, and it exists, and I know it exists, because every district in this country has had one since before either of us was working, and I cannot see it, and there is no version of asking that is not a version of asking somebody to open a door for me.

"You have had the other half of that since Friday and you have not said anything about it, and I am not going to ask you, and I want it minuted in whatever it is you keep, that I did not ask, and that you did not offer, and that those are two different facts and both of them are on us."

"Both of them are on us."

"Say that back to me."

"Both of them are on us," said Marek Senn, "and I am not going to write down that you did not tell me what you said to Keogh, and I am not going to write down that I did not ask, and neither of those two things is going in a book, and I have written this telephone call into a book in a drawer under a battery in a room you have never been in, and I wrote it on the Tuesday night and I have not sent it to you, and that is a third fact and it is mine and I am telling you it exists and that is all."

There was a pause of about three seconds, and then a floor went under her voice and a lot of other noise with it.

"Two days of not saying it, and then a sentence, in a van, in a car park, and you have not told me one word about the lanyard and neither have I, and Marek—"

"I am going to say the thing and then I am going to be quiet, and I am not saying it because I am being clever. I am saying it because if I do not say it now then in four months I will have it ready and I will have practised it and it will be a sentence.

"You asked me on the Thursday of week fourteen whether I would come into a room and say the true sentence in it and be a bad witness and there would not be a copy afterwards. I have not been asked to do that. I offered. It is mine and I have not spent it and I am not going to tell you that I am saving it up, because I have been carrying it for two weeks like a plate of water and the honest position is that I do not know whether I would do it and I have not the right to promise you a thing I have not tested.

"And I am a company employee from the Monday of this week, and the shape of what that offer was — one man, in a room, saying a true sentence and being a bad witness — is now a man from a supply company saying a true sentence about a supply company, and in four years the true sentence will be in a file and it will say *the technician said*, and that is the sentence that did the damage in the first place, and I have worked that out on a Sunday in about four minutes and I have been carrying it since the Sunday and I did not tell you because I did not want to hand you the reason before I had the thing.

"That is the whole of what I have. There is no second thing. Go and do your round."

She was quiet for about a second and a half.

"I am going to say one sentence and then I am going to go and do a round, and I am not going to say it kindly, and you are not to write it down, and I have already forgotten that I have said it." Her voice went flat. "You offered me a bad witness on a Thursday and you are holding it in a van on a Tuesday in week nineteen because you have a lanyard, and both of those are true, and the second one is the first one with a salary attached, and you are twenty-two."

"Yes."

"We had an hour."

"We had an hour," said Marek Senn.

She put the phone down and he sat in a van on a forecourt in Nine Bridges for about four minutes with a cup of tea going cold on the passenger seat and did not start the engine, and then he started the engine.

He had a customer in Lattice at half past one and a work order in Ashfields at four and he was on the depot floor by half past two with a sheet and a hand set and about a hundred and forty pounds of work in front of him that would arrive on a Friday.

He enjoyed it. He wrote that down later, which was the part he could not have predicted on the Friday of week eighteen.

He wrote it at twenty to ten that night at the bench with the door shut and the extractor on, and it came out in about twenty minutes, and it is the only page in that book so far in two days that came out at speed.

*66. Tuesday of week 19. A van, four work orders, a strop that had been up for eight years in a dead building in Ashfields, and a man in a corridor who decided in a second and a half that a lanyard is somebody official.*

*Ninety seconds.* I want the number in the book because I have been asked in my own head what the answer is and I do not want to answer it generously. Ninety seconds at the top of a stairwell, and it turned on a man looking at a green cord with a photograph on it and then deciding I was somebody, and then being told no, and then looking at the cord again. **He said *you'll be the fella from the other company* and *sorry, you're all the same colour*, and I have written that sentence down four times in six weeks and it is the whole of this case and it was said by a man in a corridor who was apologising to me.** And there is no register anywhere in this city on which that fact can be struck off, and I have looked, and there is one thing in the whole of this that would do it and it is the fourth condition of a share I signed, and the fourth condition says I may not demonstrate, teach, assess or assist at any practice in that department, on any premises of it, at any time, and it is not limited by hours, and it does not have an expiry date on it, and it came off a trust on the Wednesday of week sixteen and it is still here, and **it is the reason the lanyard has not got me into a room in a fortnight, and it is the only thing in fourteen weeks that has worked, and it worked by accident and for a reason that has nothing to do with what it is for.**

*What I am good at.* A strop. A forty-kilo weight on a strop, hung at head height in an eight-year-old void, and there is no card in this trade for a dead power and a live weight, and there is no form for it and no insurer and no column in anybody's returns, and the only reason that thing came down is that a fitter happened to have been up there in the year the building was cleared. I had ninety seconds of the four hours that were pure and then it turned and I am putting the ninety in so that nobody can read this page and think I had the four hours. I have not had four hours of anything. I have had ninety seconds and a long tail, and the long tail is the job, and the job is four days a week.

*Petar Vance put us both on the yard off the Meridian for the Wednesday and told me he had and would not say why.* He has been there thirty times in eleven years and has never been asked a question by a man in an office. He gave me the reason and it is the reason I gave a man of fifty-eight in week twelve about not saying it came out of a college: **the moment a service engineer from a supply company stands in a yard office and says anything about how a load gets released, that yard is never the same yard again, and the man who came is the reason.** Eleven years. He put a man's own sentence back in front of him at a distance of two hundred yards and did not notice that he had done it, and I did, and I have not told him.

*How a person stops it.* Nobody yet, and I have now got a company card to come in three weeks that says in four lines of small print that I am competent to confirm scope to a learner, and Petar Vance has told me in a lay-by in a town nobody comes to that the moment will arrive and it will sound like a small thing and I will be able to answer it, and that I have not got a single thing in this city that could stop me answering it. Not the fourth condition: the fourth condition stops me teaching, and this is not teaching. Not the four names: they can end a share and not a corridor. Not the working share, which expires at five o'clock on the Friday of week thirty. **I have gone through everything I have got and I have not got one thing that stops me saying a third step in a corridor, and I have not said one, and the reason I have not said one is that I have not been asked yet, and that is not a rule, that is a Tuesday.**

*What the ninety seconds are for.* Whether the ninety seconds is the honest measure of the job or the honest measure of me. I can do the strop and I can do a reed switch and I can do a supply unit in a void, and I can be let through a gate by a man who has never heard of me, and every one of those four things is worth money to somebody and not one of them is the thing I have spent fourteen weeks on, and I did not know that on the Friday of week eighteen when I signed four sides in a repair shop for three sentences a man said in three rooms, and I have written down that I have not worked out what the job is and it is still true three weeks later and the only new information is that I am good at four things that are not it.
