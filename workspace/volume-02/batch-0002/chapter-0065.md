# Chapter 65 — The Correct Number of Seconds

The fault on the Tuesday morning was on the third rig of four in a builder's merchant in Ashfields and it took him most of an hour, and the Thursday of week nineteen put him in a compound off the Meridian Corridor at ten in the morning because a second unit on the same rack had a supply fault and the merchant had moved the whole thing to a working yard and the yard was a customer of somebody and the work order had come back onto his own sheet with a different address on it.

It was a hard-standing compound with four bays under a canopy, a fence, a portacabin office with a heater going and a smell of wet cardboard in it, and about nine men working. There was a courier firm's van in bay two with its tailgate up and its roller door tied back, and a stack of banded pallets of block paving under the canopy at the end of bay two, four high, on a clamped stand, with a powered band cutter on a tripod beside it.

Marek Senn had the green cord round his neck and the meter bag in his hand and he was on his knees at the base of the third rig with the cover off when he heard the radio.

A woman's voice, two hundred yards off and slightly too loud, in the flat register of somebody who has said the same eleven words about nine times already this morning:

"Bay four, pallet twelve, confirmed. Bay two, pallet nine, going."

The band cutter started and stopped and the fitter came round the stand and put a hand on the top pallet and looked at the band and said, "It's not gone."

"It's gone," said the woman. "Say again, bay two, pallet nine, going."

And Marek Senn, on his knees on a hard standing with a meter in his hand, heard the whole of it happen in about nine seconds and did not understand any of it until the sound.

The band came off. The stand released. Four pallets of block paving, at head height, went down across about nine feet of wet concrete, and the sound was not the sound he had been expecting, and afterwards the two men on the back of the courier's van both said the same thing about it, which was that it was quicker than they expected.

Somebody screamed, and it was not a scream about pain, it was a scream about a noise.

He was on his feet and round the end of the rack in about four seconds. The woman on the ground was about forty, and she was on her back with her right arm out from under her and her eyes open, and she was not unconscious, and she was talking.

"Don't — the second one's still up."

"Somebody's called," said Marek Senn. "What is your name."

"Sondra. Sondra Mbeki. It's my arm." Her voice went up and then came back down, which is a thing that happens. "There's another one stood on the end, it's not gone, don't let them—"

He got the second stack's tag out of the splinters and shouted for the fitter to take the end pallet off with the clamp, which took two men and forty seconds, and it did not go.

And then the compound stopped, in the way a compound stops, and there were four people on the ground and a woman running with a phone and the fitter standing with his hand still on the cutter and a man of about fifty at the portacabin door who had a piece of paper in his hand and had been writing on it.

"Have you called an ambulance."

"Called. Nine minutes." The man at the door looked at the green cord, and then at the badge on it, and then at the badge again. "Are you — are you the company? Because I've got to ring head office and they said the company fills the form in."

"Yes," said Marek Senn. "Give me the paper."

He took it. It was a single side on a clipboard with a printed grid, and there was a version number in the corner, and it had a company's name on it, and the fields went down the page like this:

*Site. Date. Time of event. Activity in progress. Technique card in use — revision. Card holder involved — name. Card number. Name of the person who confirmed the release. Employee number. Time the confirmation was given. Nature of injury. First aid given. Attended at hospital. Reported by. Supervisor.*

Marek Senn stood in a portacabin doorway at about ten forty-five in the morning and read that page for about four seconds and understood every single thing about it, including the two boxes with the two words in them, *name of the person who confirmed the release* and *time the confirmation was given*, which are not the same box as *name of the person whose release it was*, and which have no relationship to each other at all anywhere in this company, and which are the only boxes in the form that anybody will ever look at.

He went back out and knelt down.

Sondra Mbeki was thirty-nine and she had been on that route six years and she was talking, and the talking is the part he has written down, and he has written it down about nine times.

"That's my arm," she said again. "My right one's gone. Sorry. Sorry, is it my arm or is it—" She stopped. "That's a lot of arm."

"It is," said Marek Senn.

"Right." She breathed out. "I did it right, though. I want that said. I did all of it."

"I know you did."

"I did all of it," said Sondra Mbeki. "I put my hand up. I stood where the sheet says, which is that side, and I did not go in, and I waited, and I did not step in. I counted. I counted, I want to say that, because you are going to think I did not. I have never had to wait for anybody in six years. I have been doing this route since I was thirty-three and I have never once had to hold my hand up and wait for a voice, and it is a stupid thing to say to say I liked it, but I liked it. It is the only part of this job where somebody has ever told me to stop."

Somebody had put a blanket over her. Somebody else was holding her hand and it was not Marek Senn and he was glad.

"The last thing I heard was the word," she said. "Bay two. So it was us, wasn't it. It was for us."

He did not answer that immediately, and it was the only time in his working life that he has not answered a person immediately, and the reason is that he could hear the radio from where he was kneeling and the fitter had it clipped to his belt, and he had heard the whole exchange, and he knew that *bay two, pallet nine, going* was not her release, and he knew the sequence, because he had been in this compound for forty minutes and he had watched Pauline Ncube do nine of them.

Her pallet was eleven.

"No," he said. "It was for eleven."

Sondra Mbeki lay on a wet concrete floor in a compound off the Meridian Corridor and looked at him for a second and a half.

"Right," she said.

That is the whole of what she said about it. Not one word more, then or afterwards, and Marek Senn has gone through the day in his head about forty times and she did not ask him again and she did not ask anybody else and she told the paramedic that she had put her hand up and waited and had not gone in, which is true, and she told the same thing to the man at the hospital at about two in the afternoon, and she has not told anybody since.

---

Pauline Ncube came across the compound at about eleven o'clock with a folder under her arm, and she was thirty-eight, and she had been doing this for six years, and Marek Senn had watched her do nine releases that morning before he knew what a release was.

She did not ask who he was. She looked at the green cord and she said, "You are filling the form," and he said, "They asked me to," and she said, "Good, thank you," and went and looked at the stack for about two minutes and took a photograph of it with a phone and put the phone away.

"That was mine," she said, coming back. "I confirmed nine this morning and I have got them on a list with times on, and I have got *bay two, pallet nine, going* at 10:41, and I have not got you on it, and I want you to understand that I am not saying I did not say it. I said it. It went out on two and it is a radio. I said it for a different pallet and I said it at the same second."

"I know. I heard both."

"Then you know the other half." She was not being defensive, and she was not being brave, and she was doing the thing that a good supervisor does, which was to say the thing out loud the first time. "I confirmed nine releases on this compound this morning and eight of them were right, and one of them was right and was about somebody else, and there is a line in the instruction that says confirmations are valid for the load named in them, and the line is mine, and I wrote it, and it is correct, and it is no use whatsoever, because the fitter is standing at a machine with four stacks round him and the operator hears the word and not the number.

"That is the whole of it. I have been doing this for six years and I have never had it go wrong before, and it went wrong in a compound where I had done eight of them correctly the same morning, and I am not going to stand here and tell you about the eight."

"The form," said Marek Senn.

"Yes. The form. Right." She put the folder on the tailgate of the courier's van, which was where he was writing, and she squared it. "I have got a form of my own for my list. Yours is different. Yours is a record of an incident, which means it goes to a regional office and it gets read, and if you put my name in the box that says I confirmed the release then in four months a person in an office is going to read that I confirmed a release and a woman without a card was hurt, and that will be true, and it will be about me, and it will be the only thing anybody knows about that morning."

"I know," said Marek Senn.

"Then you know why I am telling you to write the last confirmation before the event, and the time of it, and the pallet number, and to write *pallet 11 — no confirmation given* on the activity line where it belongs. That is all true. It is not in their favour and it is in mine, but it is true, and I would rather be on a form that is completely true than on a form that makes me look better."

He looked at the form for a while, in the rain, on a tailgate.

The box said *name of the person who confirmed the release.* The box said *employee number.* The box said *time the confirmation was given.* There was a box for the card number of the person involved, and no such card existed, and the box would stay empty or say *none*, and *none* in that box is a fact about a woman and not a statement about the procedure.

There was no box for *the confirmation that was not given to this person.* There was no box that would let a form say: this woman's release was not confirmed, she waited the correct interval, no word was given to her, and the word she heard belonged to a pallet eleven yards away, and if the word had not been given at all then she would have withdrawn and reported and nothing would have fallen on her.

There was no box for any of it, and the form was fifteen boxes long, and it was the correct form, and there was a version number in the corner.

So he wrote her name in the first box, and her employee number in the second, and the time, and he wrote it truthfully: the last confirmation on that channel before the event was at 10:41 and it was Pauline Ncube's, and the pallet was nine, and he wrote *11 — no confirmation given* on the activity line because she had told him to and because it was true.

And then he read the page back to himself, in the rain, on a tailgate, and it said: a courier with no card had a stack of pallets released by a company supervisor at 10:41, and had sustained a forearm injury, and had done everything the procedure asked.

And it was completely true. Every word of it. And no sentence in it was about the sentence on a card in a binder in a drawer under a bench in Lattice Ward, and it never would be, and there was nothing in the fifteen boxes that could carry the one thing that had happened, and he had written it correctly in about four minutes, which is a thing he was later told was fast.

She signed it. He signed it. She wrote her employee number at the bottom and a time, and she said, "Do not put the fitter's name in the *holder* box. He is not a holder. It is on the cutter line, that is all it is."

"It is on the cutter line," said Marek Senn, "and that is how the form says to record it."

The back of the van smelled of wet cardboard and diesel and there was a blanket on the ground and a woman on a trolley in a doorway and about eleven men standing about four feet apart not looking at her, which is a thing about eleven men.

---

She went to hospital at about eleven and came back into the compound in a wheelchair at about half past three with a sling and a piece of paper, and the piece of paper was for an operation, and Marek Senn was on the third rig with the cover off when she came round the end of the rack.

"Mr Senn."

"That is not a good use of my morning."

"You did the form." She stopped the chair. "I have read it, because the man in the office read it out to me before I went in, and I want to say one thing about it and then I am going, because I have got a lift at four."

"Go on."

"You wrote that I did all of it," said Sondra Mbeki. "You wrote *held per instruction, did not enter path, waited interval, no confirmation given*. That is exactly what happened and I have never in my life had anybody write down that I did something right at the same time as they wrote down that I was hurt, and I have worked for four people in six years and not one of them would have done that, and the reason none of them would have done it is that it does not help anybody.

"So I am going to say the thing I actually came over to say and then I am going. Somebody rang my firm at about eleven and asked whether I have done the release course. I have not. I have not done a course. There is no card with my name on it anywhere. And the woman on the phone was very nice about it and she said *that's fine, most of the ones on this one are*, and then she asked me to confirm that I had not done the course, and I said yes, and she said thank you, and she put the phone down.

"That is what I came over to say. I have not got a card and now there is a note on a file somewhere that says I have not got a card, and that note is going to be there for ever, and it got put there by the injury. So that is the price of the form, and I have had four hours to think about it and that is the whole of what I have come up with."

Marek Senn was on his knees at the base of a rig with a meter in his hand.

"I am not going to say that is right," he said.

"No," said Sondra Mbeki. "It isn't right. It is just what happened. You did not do it, you wrote down what happened, and what happened had my name in it, and now there is a note about the other name." She moved the chair round a foot. "Is there a form for a woman who has not got a card."

"There is a box for it," said Marek Senn. "I wrote in it. That is all the use that box gets."

She thought about that for a second.

"Right," said Sondra Mbeki, and went to find her lift.

---

He drove back to the depot at four because the van was due and the calibration was due, and the calibration was still due, and he did it on the bench at about ten past five with nobody in the building, and Petar Vance was not in on the Thursday and neither was anybody else, and the light over the office was on and there was a key board with forty-one keys on it and his name was in a box on a Monday sheet on the spike and he had not taken it down.

He sat in the van in the depot yard for about ten minutes and then drove to Lattice Ward and opened the shop at about twenty to six and did not turn the extractor on, and the shop was cold, and he sat at the bench without the light over the bench on for about half an hour.

Then he turned the extractor on.

---

He wrote it at about ten past eleven at night, and it took him forty minutes, and it is the shortest page he has written since the Friday of week eighteen.

*68. Thursday of week 19. Hers, not mine.*

*What she did.* She put her hand up. She stood on the side the sheet says. She did not go in. She counted, and she told me she counted, and she told the paramedic and she told a man at a hospital at two in the afternoon and she has not told anybody since. **She waited the correct number of seconds.** That is the phrase and it is the phrase the whole of this case has been circling since the Saturday of week fifteen and it turns out that the correct number of seconds is the worst number in the world: long enough to prove she did it, and not one second of it belonged to her.

*What I have not written anywhere else.* What caused it. I have been in that compound forty minutes and I have been watching a woman in a lanyard do nine releases correctly and I have been on my knees at a rig with a cover off and I did not see any of it coming, and I have been sitting in a van and in a shop for four hours going through it looking for the thing I missed, and there is nothing. The stack was banded. The stand was clamped. The band was cut. A man was told to come in and came in. A woman four feet away put her hand up and did not come in, and did not go in, and counted. **A good supervisor said a correct word at the correct time about a different pallet, and the operator heard the word and not the number, and a line in an instruction that I have read on a plate and on a wall in a prefab office and in a binder in a drawer is the line that says confirmations are valid for the load named in them, and the line is true and the line is mine — I mean it is the industry's, it is in all of them, it is the sentence and not a person — and the line is no use at all, and a good woman wrote it, and I am not going to make that into a thing about her.**

*Whether it is what I have been looking for.* Whether this is what I have been looking for since the Tuesday of week fourteen. I have sat here for four hours with the book shut and I have gone round it about nine times, and the honest position is that I do not know and I am not going to be the man who says he does. **A woman was hurt on a Thursday afternoon and there are nine things that had to be true for it and I have been in the yard for forty of the ninety minutes, and I have written that down once and I am going to leave it there.** I have a feeling about it and the feeling is a feeling and I have got a whole book of reasons not to be the man who finds a proof in a wound, and the strongest of those reasons is that the woman who caused it is thirty-eight and has been doing it for six years and did nine of them right that morning, and the second strongest is that this woman said *I liked it, it is the only part of this job where somebody has ever told me to stop*, and if I make that into a hinge then I have made it into a thing where a good company supervisor is the villain, and she is not, and the crews would follow her, and the number behind all of it is real and I am not going to be a man who says it is not.

*What the form has no box for.* Fifteen boxes. Name of the person who confirmed the release. Employee number. Time the confirmation was given. There is no box for the confirmation that was not given to this person. I wrote her name in it and her employee number and 10:41 and it is all true, and the form in four months will say that a company supervisor confirmed a release and a woman without a card was hurt, and the only thing anybody can do with that form is believe it, and it is completely, entirely, arithmetically true, and I filled it in in about four minutes in the rain on a tailgate and I did it correctly. **The machine does not care what the name is for.** It is not that a document is a bad thing. It is that a document has fifteen boxes and the truth has about forty, and fifteen always fit and forty never do, and the twenty-five that will not go in are not discarded — they are simply not there, and in four months a man in an office cannot tell the difference between a thing that was not recorded and a thing that did not happen, and neither can I, and I was the one holding the pen.**

And the thing under it, which I am only writing because it is the only part of today that is mine. I have now put my own name in a box on a piece of paper in a building in this city four times in eight days. I did not want to. That is the whole of the finding. **I have not wanted to once, and I have done it four times, and the four of them were: a wage, a work sheet, a sheet on a wall in a yard, and a form on a tailgate in the rain, and on every occasion there was a good reason, and on every occasion the good reason was that somebody was standing in front of me waiting.** If I had to write the sentence for it and I am not going to write the sentence for it, because the writing down is the part that is the machine, and the four things are on this page and this page is in a drawer and that is the correct place for them and I have not sent it to anybody.

And the last thing, which I have not written anywhere and am not going to: at about a quarter to eleven this morning a woman on a wet concrete floor said *I have been doing this route since I was thirty-three and I have never once had to hold my hand up and wait for a voice, and it is a stupid thing to say to say I liked it.* And I have been trying for four hours to work out how to feel about that, and what I have come up with is that it is the fourth beat, and it is not the sentence on a card, it is the whole of what is underneath the card, and I have been in this case for fourteen weeks and I have never once met anybody who liked it.

I have not solved anything. There is a woman in a sling and a form with fifteen boxes in it and a man in a portacabin office who has written nine confirmations on a list with times on, and none of those three things is a thing I can fix, and I am going to bed.
