# Chapter 70 — No Box for It

There were two rooms in this city at a quarter to ten on the Wednesday morning of week twenty and Marek Senn was on the depot floor at ten past nine with a work order in his hand and a green cord round his neck and a full shelf of hand sets in front of him, and the fact that he was in neither of them had stopped being a decision some time in the second week of the term.

The first one was on the first floor of the Ninth Street block and it had eleven people in it, nine of them first years, and one voice.

Asha Reed said the same sentence she says on the first Wednesday of every month, which is that the case is Senn's and the technique is nobody's yet, and Toby Ansell asked her the same true question he asked in week sixteen, which is whether there is anything in it that can be taught, and she said no again, out loud, in a room where she was the only voice, and four people looked at the floor.

Then she said the other thing, which was new, and she said it slowly.

"Eight days ago there were eleven of us in here and one of us read a supplier's page out loud to forty-one people in a hall and put his own name against it. That is a fact and it is in the department's file and I have read it, because I am allowed to read the file now that I have said no twice, and I want to say the thing it has done to the room. **It has made four of the people in here better at this and it has made me worse.** I can now name a place where this was done out loud with a member of staff's name on it, and before the Monday of week nineteen I could only name a plate in a room. So I have got a name and a plate and a supplier and I have lost the ability to say *there is nobody in it who can say no*, and I would like somebody in this room to understand that those two things arrived in the same week and I do not think one of them was the point."

Nobody said anything.

"Also," said Asha Reed, "on the Monday I sat at the back, next to the wall, and there was a third-year in the row behind me who had been in this room in week sixteen and had not come in, and I have known that since the Monday and I have not said anything to anybody about it, and I am saying it now to the room because if I say it to one person it becomes a thing and if I say it to the room it is a fact about a room. He did the right thing and I do not know that he did the right thing and I am not going to pretend in here that I do."

The second one was behind a kitchen in the Saltmarket and the meeting was ten to eleven and there were about nine people in it, which was the fewest anybody in that room could remember, and Bea Nunn had a tin.

**One.** Ena Truslove, of the Fold nursery, about thirty plastic chairs.

She had come in on the Saturday morning, on foot, from the Fold, which is four streets, and she had not asked anybody to ask her.

"They went on the Saturday in a van," said Ena Truslove. "A woman with a district badge and a list with a tick on it, not a name, and she asked for thirty and I said yes in about four seconds and I am not going to be coy about the four seconds, because I have been not-coy about the last two times and I got a card in this room for it and it was the first time in eleven years anybody in this building had thanked me for anything.

"They are going to a room off the roundabout for a Saturday, for about twenty women on somebody's books, and nobody has told me whose, and nobody asked me, and nobody from this nursery is going to be thanked, and those three things are the same three things as the Tuesday of week fourteen, and this time I said yes in four seconds, and I would like it written in that book that I said yes in four seconds, and I would like it not written anywhere else."

"Written," said Bea Nunn.

"Not entered," said Bea Nunn.

There was a pause of about two seconds.

"Explain the difference," said Ena Truslove.

"The rule is the caller's words, in the first person, and a signature at the end, and the taker's name in the fourth column. You have not written anything and you have not signed anything, and I am not going to write it down for you and call it yours, because the minute that is in that book it stops being thirty chairs and it starts being *a member lent a room to a district*, and there is a card on that shelf in this room with the words *this is the fourth one I have written* on it and I am not having that argument twice in one term.

"Also, and I am saying this as the person who invented the rule: a thing that only moved something is a diary, and this book is not a diary, and I have got about nine years of cards in here and every single one of them is a thing somebody needed somebody else to know. If you want thirty chairs in it then write thirty chairs in it in your own hand and sign it, and put it in the tin, and I will take it at the next one and I will read it out, and it will take four minutes, and it will be the truth. If you do not want that, then it was a Saturday, and I have had a lot of Saturdays in this building and none of them are in this book."

"Four minutes," said Ena Truslove.

"Four minutes is what it is worth."

"Right," said Ena Truslove. "Then it was a Saturday." She stood up. "And I am going to say the last thing and it is for the room and not for you, Bea. On the Tuesday of week fourteen a college took my chairs and made a room public and I was the reason and nobody thanked me. On the Saturday of week fifteen a company gave a certificate to about forty people in the other side of my kitchen. On the Saturday of week nineteen a district took my chairs for a Saturday for about twenty women and I said yes in four seconds and it is the best use those thirty chairs will ever get. **Three Saturdays, three rooms, and not one of them has ever been a room that anybody voted for, and every single one of them was better than anything this building could have voted for, and that is the thing I would like somebody in this city to be frightened of, and it is not me.**"

Nobody argued. It took the full four minutes and about nine of them were in the room.
