# Chapter 79 — Thirty Chairs

Rafi Pell came at about ten with a rucksack and said four things in the doorway in a minute and a half, which was the most he had ever said in a doorway.

"One. I am not going to be late, the bus is at ten past and the gate is at eleven and I have got the voucher and the hard hat is for the wagon roof. Two. Somebody rang the shop on Wednesday asking me whether I had done the release course, and I have not told you which company, and I am telling you that I have not told you, and I would like that on a page, because nobody has asked me for anything and I would like that to be the state of it as of the Saturday. Three. That woman rang me back on Thursday and left a message and I have not rung her and I am not going to on a Saturday, and I have got a card in a plastic sleeve that a man of sixty-one put in a market and I have thought about it more than I have thought about a lot of things and I have still not worked out what it is for."

"That is four things."

"That is three and a bit. **Four. The boy at the post.**" He shifted the rucksack. "He has not been on the post since Tuesday. Not Wednesday, not Thursday, not Friday. He is on the yard, the other man said, and he is on the yard and he is not at the post, and the foreman has not said one word to anybody about why, and the sheet's still on the wall by the kettle and it has got nine entries in it in his writing and then a gap of three days and no line at all."

"Three days is Wednesday, Thursday, Friday."

"Three days and he has been on the yard," said Rafi Pell. "I am not going to tell you who told me and I have not written it down and I am not going to, and I have been on a Saturday crew for about a month and I did not used to know a single one of those names."

He was gone up the street with the rucksack and the hard hat on top of it and Marek Senn stood in the doorway and then went and shut the shop for an hour, which he had never done on a Saturday.

---

He rang the depot's mobile at eleven o'clock on the Saturday morning of week twenty-one and Denny Molloy answered on about the fifth ring, and Marek Senn said who he was, and Molloy said "right," and waited.

"I am not ringing about the hours and I am not ringing about Wednesday."

"Then what."

"Two things and I have got about four minutes because I'm at the shops." He was not being difficult. He was a man of thirty-nine who was behind on his rent and had said *right* to a stranger. "**My name is M. Senn. I am the newest technician. I am four days, on the road Mondays and Thursdays, and the Monday and the Thursday have been covered by you and by a man called Vance for three weeks, and the discretionary hours that got frozen on Wednesday afternoon are hours that you were covering for me.**"

There was nothing on the line for a second.

"Petar told you that."

"Petar told me that in a van on Thursday morning at eleven o'clock and he told me on purpose and he said he had been waiting three weeks for me to ask how a four-day week with two clear days gets done in a building with four men in it, and I did not ask, and the reason I did not ask is that it is a favour, and I have had that sentence in my head since last Saturday and it is not mine."

"You've been in the building two weeks."

"Three weeks on Monday. I have been off sick since Tuesday, which is a lie, and I have been at a counter in a kitchen in Ashfields since Tuesday putting together a claim on a thing I do not want to name in a shop, and I did not ring in because I did not know what I was going to say." He stopped. "You are the first one who has rung me. Everybody else has come and asked me whether I was all right."

"**Are you all right.**"

"No," said Denny Molloy. "That's the second thing, and you can have it, because you're the one that asked and it's not fair that a man works out his own rota and then nobody tells him. I am not all right and I am not going to be all right until the end of the month, and I am not blaming you and I want that said, because there is a piece of paper in that building with your name next to it and a question in capitals and if I say the word *rota* out loud in a canteen in three weeks' time then I am going to be a man with a grievance, and I have got a boy of seven and I am not going to be that."

"Then say it to me now. You have said it to me now."

"I have said it to you now." A pause. "**Right. Now you tell me why you have not got a card, because that is the question on the paper and I have read it four times and there is no answer on it anywhere, and if you tell me a reason I will not repeat it, and if you tell me it is a company matter then that is the answer and I will stop asking.**"

Marek Senn stood in a shop with the shutter up and a bad shoulder and a phone in his hand and had about nine seconds.

"I did not go," he said.

"Go where."

"To a course. Two days, the company's own, in the first month, on the clock, and the card at the end of it. It is the fifth clause of my contract and it is not negotiable and the man who put it in said so to my face and he was right to. It was on the Thursday of week twenty and I did not go, and nobody stopped me, and I have not got a reason I am willing to give anybody, and I have written that in a file in the building in a form that says *reason*, in four lines, and that is the whole of it."

"You did not go to a course because of a reason and you will not say the reason."

"I did not go to a course. That is the whole sentence. I have not got another one and I have looked."

"That is about eleven words," said Denny Molloy, "and you have not said one of them to anybody else in this building, have you."

"No."

"**Then don't.**" He said it fast. "Don't. Not because of me. Because of a man in that canteen who has a phone and a grudge about being asked for his hours back, and the second you say that out loud in there it is a story about a man's reasons, and in about four months somebody in an office is going to ask me why a technician who is not carded is covering my days, and the answer I want to be able to give is *he never said*. That's the answer. Keep it."

"I will keep it."

"Right." A door. "I'm going back to this claim. And the hours come back, don't they."

"On or about the first of the next month, and to you before anybody else, and I have read it in writing and it is dated and it is his signature and not mine."

"Nobody's told me that."

"Nobody has told you anything," said Marek Senn, "and that is the entire problem with that building and I have now been in it three weeks and I have worked it out on a Saturday morning on a telephone to a man who has not spoken to me before today, and it has taken me three weeks and you about nine seconds, and I am not going to pretend those are the same."

---

Talia Venn rang at about half past eleven.

"Three days," she said. "I have had it three days and I have not put one word of it anywhere, and I am going to tell you the two things I have done with it and then I am going to ask you one question and I am going to ask it properly, out loud, so that you cannot pretend it went past you.

"I have written to Keogh saying that a district practice standards office does not hold company personnel records and cannot produce them and is not going to try. **And I have not sent it yet.** It is in a draft and it has a subject line and it is eleven lines and it is correct, and I have not sent it because in about four months it is going to be read by somebody as the first step in a district contractor's campaign, and I would like it on the record in my own hand in about four years that it was sent on the Saturday of week twenty-one before anybody had asked me to send it."

"And the second thing."

"The second thing is that I read the whole of it twice and I have not slept properly since Wednesday and I am not going to tell you which four pages, because you do not need to know and because I can hear myself turning it into a favour." A chair. "Now the question. **Did your company publish it.**"

"No."

"Right," said Talia Venn. "And I am going to say the rest of it before you can answer anything, because you have about nine seconds and I know what you are going to do with them.

"That document contains a register of named course holders. Two hundred names, dates, card numbers, and a column somebody has written the word *CARD* down thirty-one times. **On the Sunday of week nineteen you told me on a telephone that there is a list in the company you work for, that you have seen it, and that you are not going to give me a name off it, and you gave me the reason and the reason was a boy of twenty and it took you nine seconds and I have not forgotten a word of it.** And on Wednesday morning a bundle with a register in it appeared in a building four miles from my flat, and on Saturday morning I am ringing you to ask whether the thing in the bundle is the thing you saw.

"That is the question. **And I am not going to ask it**, and I want that in your book in my words and I want you to notice that I have just told you exactly what I am going to ask and then not asked it, and that is the first time in a year I have done anything like that and I did it for no reason except that you would have said yes."

Marek Senn sat on a stool in a shop with a shutter up.

"I did not read a name off it," he said. "Any of it. Not on a Sunday on a telephone and not on the Tuesday of week twenty-one when it was on a table four feet from me under a canopy for four hours, and there is a man in a depot I have worked beside for three weeks who asked me the question himself this morning and I told him I have not got a card and I have not got a reason and there is a form with a date on it in the building, and I told him not to repeat it, and he said keep it, and I am going to."

"I know you didn't," said Talia Venn. "**That is not the question and I did not ask the question.**"

"Then what was it."

"It was a test and it was not a good one and I am not going to pretend it was anything else." She let a second go. "It was: does the thing that happened in this city in the summer of last year have a name on it, and if it does, is the name in a bundle on a counter, and can a person like me get it. And you have answered the first two, in order, over two days, without being asked twice, and the answer to the third is going to be no for about four years, and **I am going to have to write *not by a company* in a form on the Tuesday and I am not going to be able to write the reason, and the reason is that I know a man who would say it if I asked him and I am not going to ask him, and that is going to be in a return as a blank line and a blank line in a return is how a whole term started.**

"So that is what it cost. A blank line, on the Tuesday, in a return, because I would rather have a blank line than a person in a bundle. And you did not make me choose that and I am not going to thank you for it, and I am also not going to be a person who says that to a man on a Saturday and then rings him on the Tuesday to check he has not been talking."

"I have not been talking."

"I know. Ring me on a Tuesday and do not bring me a document. **And Marek — the boy at the post. I do not know about that and you have not told me and I am not going to ask you, and I want it on the page that a woman in Crown Terrace asked me on Thursday whether a release on a site is done by a carded person and I had to say I did not know, and there is now a piece of paper in this city with a boy's name and his site on it, and the two of those are going to meet in about a year, and when they do I am going to be the person in the middle of it, and I would like you to have got there first.**"

The line went.

---

The Saltmarket at about one o'clock on the Saturday of week twenty-one had the Exchange's back room open and a tin on the table with about nine cards in it, and Ena Truslove was at the table with a pen.

"You said you would tell her I said it in four seconds."

"I told her you said it in four seconds. I did not tell her you had thought about it for three days."

"Good." She wrote on a card in block capitals and slow, and it took her a while, and the pen went over the same word twice. Then she signed it, which she had not done for the other two, and put it in the tin.

Bea Nunn did not take it out and read it. She looked at it, and looked at the tin, and looked at Ena Truslove.

"You have signed it."

"I have signed it."

"Then it is a card and not a conversation, and it will be read out at the next one, in four minutes, with the taker's name in the fourth column, and mine goes in there." She wrote something in a book and did not look up. "It stays at forty lines, Ena, because I am not reading it today and I am not reading it this month. **And I want to say the next part once and then I am going to make the coffee.**

"What you have just put in that tin is the first card in nine years that somebody has put in here *and not wanted anything out of it*, and I have about four hundred of them in a box under this table, and the whole architecture of that box is that everybody who put a card in it was frightened of something. You are not. You are annoyed. **And a card that is not frightened is a different kind of object in a book, and in about four years somebody is going to want to write about this room, and they are going to have to decide what to do with a card that was not a problem, and that is going to be a genuinely difficult problem, and it is going to be a woman's problem, and it will not be mine because I am sixty-one and I will not be here.**"

"I would like it read out," said Ena Truslove.

"It will be read out. In four minutes. In about three weeks." Bea Nunn stood up. "And I am going to be told by about nine people in this building that thirty chairs is not an item, and they will be right, and I am going to read it anyway, and the reason is that you said yes in four seconds three times and nobody has said it back to you once, and a book that only holds the frightened is a book that gets a vote every time, and that is how a room stops being anybody's."

She filled the kettle and the market went on.

---

He drove out to the fourth Saturday at about half past two because it was a Saturday and he had a Saturday and he had spent five of them doing nothing on purpose.

The yard was the same yard. The gate took his card and the man lifted the barrier. The woman with the lanyard was already there with a box of booklets and about twenty men under a canopy and a trestle table, and about forty men on the hard standing who were not the course.

Lenny Trigg was at the back of the group with his arms folded.

And before the forty minutes there was a short part that Marek Senn had not been there for last time, and it was read out by a man in a district coat with a clipboard, standing under the canopy in front of twenty men, and it was this:

"Right. Before we start. There is a condition on this yard now and it is not ours, it is the customer's, and the condition is that there is a named release holder on site for the whole of the session. I have asked for it four times and I have been refused three times and I have been told this week that the answer is no. There is one man on this crew with a card and he is on Wednesdays, and he has not come today, and we are going to go ahead anyway, and I am telling you that I am going to go ahead anyway, and I am telling you why, so that it is on a piece of paper rather than on a person.

"Because if I stop this session then in about a month there is no session, and every one of you goes home with nothing, and in about a year somebody in an office writes *no session, fourth Saturday, no session* on a form and it is true. **And if I go ahead then somebody who is not here has been more careful than me, and I would like that on the record in those words, because I do not want to be the man who stopped it.**"

Twenty men looked at the ground.

A voice at the back said, "It's that lad, isn't it. Pell."

Marek Senn stood at the far end of the canopy with a meter bag at his feet and a lanyard on and understood the entire shape of the week he was standing in, in a second and a half, on a hard-standing yard, in the sun, with nobody able to hear a word of it.

A rule that governs a page cannot govern a mouth. A certificate is a page that says a mouth happened. A condition on a Saturday contract that a named holder is on site is a box on a sheet that has been empty for thirty-one years. A man of sixty-one in a market inventing a rule that the taker's name stays in. A woman of twenty-four with four boxes in a drawer.

And a man of twenty-one, in a rucksack, with a hard hat on top of it for the roof of a wagon, who is not in the room and who has a card in a plastic sleeve, and who has not been asked for anything by anybody, and who rang the shop on Wednesday to ask whether a woman from a company wanted him to come and do a Saturday, and did not say yes and did not say no, and whose refusal is not a refusal because nobody has offered him anything, only a question.

The forty minutes happened. It was the same forty minutes. About nine hands went up and about eleven needed it twice, and Lenny Trigg got it right first time and stood there afterwards with his arms out of his sides.

At the end the district woman said thank you, and picked up the booklets, and said to the man in the district coat, "Can I ask you something and will you not like it."

"Ask."

"Who is the named holder."

"Off this yard. Wednesday."

"Then who is standing in the missing-holder box in my form this afternoon."

Nobody answered her and she got in a car and went, and Marek Senn watched it go and understood that in about four years somebody in an office was going to read that a Saturday was run with no named holder present, and the answer was going to be in a box, and the box was going to have a name in it or it was not going to have a name in it, and both of those are a form deciding what a person is, and neither of them was asked.

He got in the van and drove about four miles and stopped at the side of a road and sat there for a while, and then he walked the rest into Lattice, because the shop is in Lattice and the walk is about forty minutes and he has not got a bus money and did not think about it.

He went up Fell Road at about half past five. There was a laundrette under his own flat and a woman behind a counter in it who had asked him four times to come round on a bus and not to look at it, and it was the third time in nine weeks, and he did not go up, and there was no reason, and the reason was going to have to be that one.

*82. Saturday of week 21, and this page is about four things that all happened to somebody else and I am not sure yet whether that is a habit or a hiding place.*

*Two records that have now met, and a boy who is not standing in front of either of them.* A bundle in Lattice with a page in it that has a boy's name and his site on it, and a sheet on a wall in a customer's prefab office with the same name going into a box nine times at a time in his own handwriting, and a gap of three days in the second one with no line in it at all. **Nobody has asked that boy anything. He is nineteen. He has been on that yard twelve weeks and at that post three, and as of this morning he has not been at the post since Tuesday, and the foreman has not said one word to anybody about why.** I cannot warn him and I have written the reason down twice this week in different words and both of them are true and one of them is a mechanism and I have not been able to work out which is the real one and I have not been able to ask anybody. What is new and is not in any of it: **the two records were made by two different people for two entirely reasonable reasons, and neither of them knew about the other one, and that is the whole of how this city produces a person who can be found.** I have spent a term believing that a document is a thing somebody does on purpose, and it is not, and the proof took a boy's handwriting and a bundle with an elastic band round it.

*What I did on a Saturday morning, which is the only thing on this page that was mine, and what it is worth.* She told me on Friday to go and say a name to a man on a day when it is not going to be a favour, and said it in one line, and she is the only person in this case who has ever spent a sentence on an instruction instead of an argument. **I telephoned a depot mobile at eleven and a man of thirty-nine who has been off sick since Tuesday, which is a lie, picked up on about the fifth ring and said *right*, and waited, and I said my own name, and then I said the rota arithmetic out loud, unprompted, and then I said I did not go to a course and I have not got a reason, and I asked him not to repeat any of it, and he told me not to say it in a canteen because of a man in there with a grudge about his hours, and I said I would keep it.** About eleven words each way. He asked why I have not got a card. **So the truth about the fourth beat went down a mobile telephone on a Saturday morning in about nine words and stopped there, and it stopped there because a man with a boy of seven would rather have *he never said* than a story, and that is not a condition and it is not a principle, it is a man doing arithmetic about his own household in about four seconds on a telephone.** I have noticed that the right thing has been cheap eleven times out of twelve this term and I do not trust the arithmetic. Nobody will ever know this happened. There is no column for it, there is no register, and if he tells it in a canteen in three weeks then it was not cheap.

*And the arrangement does not need me.* I had been carrying a Monday at six minutes to ten past six like a man carrying a medal, and a woman with a bar took it off me on Friday morning in forty seconds and sent me to say a name to a man, and told me to do it on a day when it is not going to be a favour, and Saturday is not a favour, and Saturday is the only day I have got. **A favour is a rule with the wrong people missing. I wrote that in a shop in week twenty and said it out loud in a yard to a stranger and told him to keep it, and I did not know until Friday that it was aimed at my own Monday.** Here is the part I have not written anywhere else: she also told me that the gap is not mine, that I cannot leave it by asking, and that somebody will be put in it who is on a rota and does not know what it is for. So if I do nothing on Monday, which I am not going to, the arrangement survives me and the price is collected from the next man instead, and the only thing my sentence on Friday morning changed is that one woman on a bank now knows whose gap it is. **An instruction that arrives and is obeyed is a slot. The slot was full at ten to eight on Friday morning and it is full now, and nobody voted, and I did not fill it, and I have spent a term looking for the man who built it.**

*And what is at the other end of all of this, which is a chair, about twenty men, and a man reading a form out in a district's coat.* No named holder on site, because the one man on that crew who has a card is on a Wednesday and has not been asked to come. And a district woman said out loud, to twenty men, that she was going to go ahead anyway, and said the reason, and the reason is that if she stops it there is no session in a month and every one of them goes home with nothing. That is right. It is completely right. It is the same four seconds as thirty plastic chairs and a hand truck and a Saturday for about twenty women, and I have now watched that four seconds arrive three times in six weeks in three buildings and I have not been able to stop one of them and I am not sure I want to. **A thing that works, that nobody voted for, in a room with a door, will be there next month. That is not a discovery. It is the mechanism. It is the same mechanism as a slot shaped like a person, and the reason the case has not moved for fourteen weeks is that I have been looking for the man who built it and there is no man and there never was, and the thing that is filling the gap is a set of form with a box in it, and every time somebody says yes in four seconds the box is the price.**

*Do not know.* Whether a man of twenty-one with a card in a plastic sleeve is going to get a telephone call about a Saturday, and if he does, whether he will say yes, and if he says yes, whether the fourth Saturday is a favour to him or a favour about him. **Nobody took a vote. Nobody has ever taken a vote. I have written that sentence into four books in two volumes and the sentence is the entire case, and on a Saturday afternoon in the sun on a hard-standing yard I watched a man in a district coat put a name in a box to stop a condition being enforced, because the alternative was nothing, and I could not tell from thirty feet whether that was a person or a slot, and I have watched both today and they looked the same, and that is the only new fact I have got out of this page and it is a terrible one.**
