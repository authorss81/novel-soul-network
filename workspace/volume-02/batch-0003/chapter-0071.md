# Chapter 71 — The Reason Field

He rang the depot's office line from the bench at four minutes to nine on the Friday morning of week twenty, and Oren Vey answered on the second ring and said his own name, which he has never once done in two volumes of a man's life, and then said nothing at all, which was the correct thing and cost him something.

"Say it once and I will stop you asking," said Marek Senn.

"You have got about nine minutes' worth of something in you and I would like all of it."

"**I did not go to the intake on Thursday.**"

The line did not go. There was a sound of a man moving a chair, and then nothing, and Marek Senn counted to eleven before anything else happened, which he did not tell anybody about and wrote down afterwards.

"Right," said Oren Vey. "Was that a decision, or did you not go."

"It was a decision. I made it last night in about nine minutes and I have not got a reason, and I did not want one, and I am not going to have a reason ready for you this morning, and if I have one by lunchtime I will ring you back and you can have that instead."

"No. You will not do that." A breath. "Listen to what I am about to do, because I have thought about it since Friday afternoon and I want you to have it in the order I have got it.

"You are not being disciplined. Your wage is not affected and nobody at that depot has been told anything, because I have not told anybody anything. The clause is not being waived and I am not going to waive it, and if I did wave it I would be the man who wrote a clause he can wave, and I have never done that and I am not starting now, in a bad year."

"Then I would like to know the cost, because you have not given me one and you gave me the price of the rota in a shop in week eighteen before I signed."

"That is fair, and it is why I am not going to hang up." The chair again. "Here is the cost and I am going to give you the number and not the mood, because you are twenty-two and I have watched you carry a mood about for a fortnight and it has not got you anywhere.

"From Monday, a card is a condition of being sent to a release callout. That is not new and it is not about you. You are not carded. There are four technicians in that building, two of them are off this week, and one of them is on the second module of a course and has eight days to go. In about three weeks somebody in that depot is going to ask me why the newest one cannot go out on a release, and I am going to have to say a sentence with your name in it in a room, and I am not going to give you their names and neither are you."

"I have not asked for their names."

"You have not and I want that noted." A pause of a second and a half. "And here is the other half, and I have been sitting with it since Friday, and I am saying it because there is nobody in this company I can say it to except you and you are the last person I want to say it to.

"**That clause is mine.** I put it in the offer myself. I put it in because I could not think of a way to say a person is competent without a card behind it, and the sentence on the card is eleven years old and came out of a pack I did not write, and I have had two days to go looking for a version of that clause with the card out of it and there is not one. I am not going to take the clause out. And I have nobody inside this company I can ask whether it should come out, because everybody inside this company is paid by me, and the one man who is not paid by me is twenty-two and is sat in a shop in Lattice Ward at nine in the morning telling me he has not got a reason."

The extractor came on. It was a Friday and it was on a timer and the timer does not know what day it is.

"Now the part you are not going to like," said Oren Vey. "There is a course-exception record in our system, and it has got four fields, and one of them says reason, and I have to complete it, and I am going to ask you to write it. Not me. You.

"If I write it, it is a manager's note and it will read like a manager's note, and in about two years somebody will take a view of a technician on a rota. If you write it, it is the only true sentence about this in the company, and it goes into a system in this building with a date on it and your handwriting on it, and I am not going to stand here and tell you that is nothing, because the last time you put a hand to a page on somebody else's wall it was a boy's pen and a box that had been empty for thirty-one years, and you have been carrying that for nine days."

He heard Vey open something. A keyboard, and then a mouse, and then nothing.

"It is open," said Oren Vey. "Four fields. Date, course, decision, reason. I have put the first two in. Say it and I will type what you say, and then I will read it back to you before it goes in, and you will have about four seconds to change it, and that is more than the company's system gives anybody."

Marek Senn put his hand flat on the bench, on a closed book he had not opened.

"I did not attend," he said. "I have not got a reason I am willing to give you and I did not want one. I would rather that said it than that it said something I have not thought about."

There was a pause of a second and a half, and the cursor went.

"That," said Oren Vey, "is the most useless thing I have read in eleven years. It is also the first true thing in that file, and there are four hundred and six of those files in this company, and in about two years four people will read it and none of them will be able to do a single thing with it. It is going in."

"Read it back."

He read it back. It came out exactly as it had gone in, which was the whole of what Marek Senn wanted from a system.

"Right," said Oren Vey. "And the last thing, and then I have got a depot in nine minutes. I am going to ring the Thursday intake myself and ask them for a date, and I am going to be the man who asks, and that goes in their system with my name on it, because I am not going to have a clerk in a building off a roundabout ring a technician and ask him whether he wants to come. And in about a year somebody is going to work out that the operations director chased a course booking, and I am going to have to be able to say that in a room as well. That is my half and I am not going to put it on you and I am not going to be thanked for it."

"Nobody is thanking you for anything in this call."

"Good. Now the one I have actually been waiting two days to say, and if I do not say it today I am going to spend the rest of the year having said it to a chair. If I rang you this afternoon and asked you to go and stand in a room and say a third step to somebody who is frightened, you would do it. You would do it well. I have watched you do the shape of it four times from forty feet and you have never once been in the room, and I would get it out of you in about eleven minutes, and **there is nothing in this company that could stop me**, and I have been an operations director for nine years and I have never had that about a person before, and I have decided that I would rather have a clause and a sentence in your handwriting than have you in a room. Do not thank me for that either. I have got four hundred and six files."

The line went at about eleven minutes and a half, which is two and a half minutes longer than either of them had meant it to be.

Marek Senn sat at the bench with the extractor going and did not open the book, and then he did open it, and then he did not write in it, and instead he got the ledger out from under the bench and turned to the first page and read Bea Nunn's rule and the line under it, which says that a rule cannot stop a man in a doorway, and he read that twice, and then he went and did a job in Nine Bridges that took ninety minutes and that he did in about forty.

He rang Sennet Row at two o'clock in the afternoon and a telephone rang four times and then a boy said, "No," before he had said anything at all.

"Right," said Marek Senn.

"I said no to that and not to the thing you are going to ask me. Ask me the thing you are going to ask me."

"I am not going to ask you. You have just told me, which is the same as being asked and being worse, and I would rather you had said nothing and let me guess wrong for a fortnight."

"I said it because you have rung me back four times this term and you have never once asked me a question I have already answered, and I have got about eleven minutes before I have to be somewhere, so use them and then I am going to tell you the rest of it and you are not going to say one word about any of it."

He said the rest of it.

"I was there from about ten to twelve. It was a boundary release on a second-year's practical, in a hall with about nine people in it, and I was an observer, and at about eleven she asked me to do the last one because the man who was supposed to do it had gone to a toilet and did not come back. So I did it. I put my hand up and I said the thing and I got it right, and the load came down about a second and a half later, which I have been told is correct, and I did not have to be told anything. **I have had the card three days and it is the best thing anybody has ever handed me and I hate it and I am not going to say that word twice.**

"There is a line in their form that says *holder's card no* and I gave her the number off the card, and she typed it in, and her machine printed my name. Not wrote it. Typed it. Out of a card. And there is a line under it that says *conditions declared* and it is blank, and I did not put anything in it, and Marek, I want to say the next part quickly before I lose my nerve for it. **I did not put anything in it because of the box on a course form at a postbox on the Saturday of week nineteen, and I have not told anybody that I did it on purpose, and I am telling you, and I am not going to tell anybody else, and I would like it to stay exactly like that, which is that I did it and you did not tell me to.**"

"You did not tell me you were going to say anything," said Marek Senn. "Go on, you have got eight minutes and you are going to use about four of them on the thing that matters."

"The thing that matters is that she asked me again where the standard came from. On the phone on Thursday she asked it and I said the card and she wrote it. **In the room she asked it again with the form on the desk and she said it while I was watching her type it, and I watched the letters go in, and that is the price and it is paid and it is in a building I have never been inside and I cannot get it back out and I did not have to do one single thing to make that happen.** I did not have to lie. That is the thing. You keep telling me to say the card and it turns out the card is a wall, because about four hundred people in this city have that sentence on a card and not one of them has ever read a page of anything, and I am one of the four hundred, and there is not one of them who could be asked.

"And there is a second one. She asked whether I would do another one on Thursday morning, and there are two on the form now, and I have not rung back about the second one, and I have not said no either, and I am telling you that and not asking you, because you told me in a car park in the rain that you have no opinion and that if I asked you for one you would give me the same sentence twice and it would be worth nothing. So I have not asked. **Do not tell me not to ring back.**"

"I was not going to."

"I know. That is why I said it."

The rain had started again in Nine Bridges and Marek Senn could hear it on a roof a long way from the one he was under.

"One more, and it is about you, and then I am going." The boy's voice did not change. "There is a company number printed on the bottom left of this card. It is the only telephone number I have got in the world. You have had it since I gave you the shop and I have used it four times this week and every single time I have had to say *I am not in employment* to a woman at a counter in a nice way, and I have decided that is the price of the card and that I knew the price on a Saturday morning at a postbox in week nineteen and I did it anyway, and I would like it written down somewhere that I knew.

"And the last thing. **I did not tell them about you. Not because you told me not to, which you did, in a car park, in the rain, in about nine hundred words. Because they did not ask.** They asked a boy of twenty where a sentence came from and there was no box on the form that said *and who did you get him from*, and if there had been one I would have said your name, and I want you to sit with that for a second, because I have had three days to work out whether I would have and I have not been able to make myself decide that I would not have."

Marek Senn did not say anything for about four seconds.

"Right," he said. "Then I am going to say one thing and it is not about Friday and it is not about the card, and then I am not going to say anything else about any of it for a month.

"The box on the course form. You have just told me that you did it on purpose. **I am not going to say one word about that, ever, and I am not going to be able to help you with it, and I am not going to tell you that it was the right thing to do, and I am also not going to tell you that it was the wrong thing, and I have never in my life managed to do both of those at once for anybody.** But I am going to ask you for something and you are going to think I have gone off my head.

"Do not tell me about it again."

There was a pause.

"Why," said Tam Arrick.

"Because in about a year I am going to want to tell somebody, and I am going to want to tell them that a boy of twenty left a line blank on purpose in a building in Crown Terrace and did it without being told to by anybody, and it will be the best thing anybody has ever said to me in a term. **And I want it to be yours and not mine. I have spent fourteen weeks refusing to put a name in a building so that a person could not be found afterwards, and what I have just done is ask a person to hold a thing for me so that I could find it later, and those are the same act with the sign on the other side, and I did not want to do it and I am doing it.**"

Nobody said anything for a moment, and then the boy said, "Right," and then said, "I have to be somewhere," and then put the phone down.

Marek Senn put his own down and sat at the bench in a shop in Lattice Ward with a green cord round his neck that he had not taken off since seven in the morning, and he did not write in the load book for about an hour, and when he did he washed his hands first, because that is the part of the routine that has not changed since the Tuesday of week fourteen.

*74. Friday of week 20. Both of these, and neither of them is about the number, and I want that written down first.*

*What a man does with a clause he put in himself.* Nine minutes on the telephone at four minutes to nine and the same nine minutes on Thursday night deciding, and neither of the two was a rehearsal for the other, and I noticed that at about minute six and did not say it. He did not shout and he did not forgive me and he told me the cost with a number in it instead of a mood, because I have been carrying a mood for a week and it has not got me anywhere. From Monday a card is a condition of being sent to a release callout. Four technicians in that building, two of them off, one of them eight days from the end of his second module. In about three weeks somebody in that depot is going to ask why the newest one cannot go out, and the sentence will have my name in it in a room. **I wrote that down when I got back from Nine Bridges and I read it twice and the second time it was not about me any more, and I have been not asking about those four technicians for ten days and I have now got a reason to.**

*And then he told me the clause was his.* He put it in himself, in week sixteen, and I have been carrying it as the price of a wage, and he has just told me that he went looking for a version of it with the card out of it and there is not one, and that he has nobody inside his own company he can ask. He then said, out loud, that if he asked me to stand in a room and say a third step I would do it, and that nothing in that company could stop him, and that he would rather have a clause and a sentence in my handwriting than have me in a room. **I have been trying to work out for a fortnight whether a wage is the worst thing that has happened to me this term and he has now told me that the man who is paying it has thought about it more carefully than I have, and neither of those facts is a reason to stop and both of them are a reason to write this down.**

*The reason field.* Four fields. Date, course, decision, reason. **Five lines in four minutes, typed by him, read back, and I had four seconds to change it and I did not use them.** It is the first time in fifteen weeks I have put a sentence in a building where somebody I work for could read it, and I have done it because a man of forty-one refused to be the one holding the pen, and the reason he refused is that a manager's note is a manager's note and mine would not be, and the reason he could know that is that I have done it four times in three weeks. It is not the incident form. It is nowhere near the incident form. **A course-exception record has four fields and an incident record has fifteen, and the truth about either of them is about forty, and the thing that is different this morning is that I only wrote one sentence and I wrote down that I have not got a reason, and a box that says *I have not got a reason* is a box nobody has ever put anything in in the whole of this case and it cost me nothing to type and it will be read by about four people in about two years and none of them will be able to do a thing with it.** He said that. In those words, more or less. And then he said do not thank me, and he said it twice.

*What I did not say to a boy in Sennet Row, at two o'clock, and it took him eleven minutes to get to.* That the department's telephone was to him and not to me and has been since the Thursday. That the reason I know any of it is that he rang the shop, and the reason he rang the shop is that the number printed on the bottom left of a company's card is the only number he has got in the world, and the price of that card was named out loud on a pavement on the Saturday of week nineteen by the man who bought it, and it has been paid in full this afternoon, in a hall in Crown Terrace, by him, and I have done nothing to earn it and nothing to prevent it and I am not going to be the man who says that to him.

*And the thing I have not got a rule for.* He has told me he will not tell them about me, and not because I told him not to. **There was no box on that form that said *and who did you get him from*, and if there had been one he would have said my name, and he has had three days and cannot decide that he would not have.** That is the sentence of this page. Every form I have filled in for fifteen weeks has had a box in it somewhere that a person can be found in, and every one of them is a box somebody designed on purpose for a legitimate reason, and I have spent the term putting my handwriting in the ones that were pointed at me and refusing the ones I invented for myself, and a boy of twenty has just worked out in three days what it took me a term, and he worked it out in a hall, and he is right, and the reason he is right is that nobody has to be cruel for a box to do it.

*Do not know.* Whether the second one on the form on the Thursday morning is a thing he is going to ring back about or a thing he is going to let go of on its own. He has told me he has not rung back and he has told me not to tell him not to, and I have done neither, and there is nothing in this city whose job it is to know. **I have got a page here that is the shortest I have written in a fortnight and it is full of a sentence I cannot say in about nine words in a shop, and I have noticed that a man who cannot be found in a box and a man whose handwriting is in five buildings in six weeks have arrived at the same place from opposite ends of this city in about the same week, and neither of them is the author of anything, and one of them is twenty and one of them is twenty-two.**
