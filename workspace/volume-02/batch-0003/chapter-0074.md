# Chapter 74 — Ten Past Six

The last thing on the Monday was a strop on a rig in Nine Bridges with a man of about fifty standing over him holding a torch and asking him, at intervals, whether that was normal.

It was not normal and Marek Senn had it in about ninety seconds, and the fault was a cam follower worn to a wedge on one side only, which meant the rig had been running cocked for about a fortnight and somebody had been compensating by hand on every lift and had got used to it, and the reason it had not gone into a book was that the operator had started writing *fine* in the daily column and had not asked anybody what fine meant.

"Do not move it," he said, with the plate off. "That is a pound and a half of steel either side, and if you put it back and run another month the follower goes and takes the cam with it. Write *worn follower, one side, running cocked* on the top line of the daily and leave the rest of it alone."

"Will that get me an inspection?"

"That gets the book to show a defect. An inspection is somebody else's decision and it is not mine and it is not yours, and if you write the word *defect* in that column every morning for a week somebody will come, and if you write *fine* you will get another month and then a cam."

The man wrote it in his own spelling, with two r's where there should be one, and Marek Senn photographed the plate before he put it back, because the fault is the evidence and the evidence is going into a book at a customer that nobody is obliged to show anybody. It is the only thing he has been good at for nineteen months, and it is not the thing anybody in this case has ever needed.

The shop shut at five and he was on the bank of the Weir at six minutes to ten past six on the Monday of week twenty-one, which was four minutes earlier than he had ever been.

The water was down about a foot and clearer than it was in week fourteen and colder in a way that had nothing to do with the weather, and there were nineteen men on the mud, and the bar was on the steps at the Cutbank with its flat end at the top, and nobody was standing at the top holding a clipboard because there is nobody in this city whose job is to stand at the top of steps with a clipboard.

"Nineteen," said Nkechi Osei.

"Nineteen."

"You were early." She had her hood down and her hands in the sleeves of a coat that had been somebody's. "I have been here since ten to. I do not get early. I get exactly on time and then I stand about a minute so that I am not first, because the first man is the man who gets the step they want."

"Who has the step they want this week."

"Whatever is highest." She said it and then looked at him. "You are not going to ask me what the number is."

"I am not going to ask you what the number is."

"Eleven on the Monday of week fourteen. Nine on the Tuesday. Eight on the Wednesday." She said it out loud to the river and not to him. "It is on four and it has been on four since the Friday of week eighteen, and I told you that night because you had just signed a contract in a shop, and I have told nobody else, and you have not said one word about it in seventeen days, which is the correct number of days for you."

He had not said one word about it because he knew what four looked like, and he had known since the Friday of week eighteen, and there is no such thing in this city as a person whose job it is to look at a stranger's wrist, and the closest thing to one that has ever existed is a man standing on a mud bank at ten past six in the dark noticing that a woman puts her weight on her right leg when she waits.

He did not say one word.

"Right," said Nkechi Osei. "Two men and the bar. Then two and the rope. Then the wall. Same as week fourteen. And you are on the rope because you are the only one here who has done it twice, and I am not going to apologise for using you, and you are not going to tell me not to."

---

They went down at ten past. Eleven steps, wet stone, the bar going down flat end first, and the man on the low end was a new one, about thirty-four, and he took nine.

Nobody said anything. The round stopped for about four seconds in the way a round stops, which is not a stoppage, it is a silence with nineteen men in it, and then the man said "sorry," and then somebody behind him said "go on, we're fine," and then the round went on.

Nine steps is not eleven steps. He had a hand on the wall at the bottom instead of on the bar, and the bar came down without a hand, and Marek Senn did not say a word about it, and neither did the man in front of him, and neither did the man behind, and at the top Nkechi Osei stood and looked down the steps for a second and a half and said nothing, and then she said, "Right. Up."

They went up eleven times.

At about twenty past seven he was on the wall leg, in the gap, with the river going past at about a foot and a half and the light coming up off the water on the east side, and the round went on, and he had a job to do on that bank that had nothing to do with a bar and that he has never once been able to write about in a way anybody would hear.

It was a man of about thirty-eight on the wall leg, and he had the rope, and he was good at it, and at about half past seven he brought the rope in and put the hook on and turned round and said the thing.

He said it the way a man says a thing he has been told to say by a woman in a sun, with a hand out flat a beat after the pull, and it was not a bad version and it was not a good version, and it was the same four steps, and everybody on the bank heard it, and about four men turned round.

Nkechi Osei was six feet away with the bar's tail under her arm.

She did not say anything. She looked at the man for about two seconds and then at the water, and then she said, "Chains are away for the week," in a voice that was about the volume of a woman reading out a locker number, and the man said "right," and the round went on.

Marek Senn stood on the wall leg with his right shoulder going and did not say a word, and he was the only man on that bank who knew exactly what he had just heard and why it was there, and it was there because a woman had given two hours and a certificate away in a back room off a kitchen on a Saturday morning in the second week of the term for nothing, in the sun, and nobody had voted, and about four hundred people a week walk past that room to get to a kettle.

**Nobody on that bank had a number for it. There was no form, no register, no column, and the man himself could not have said where he had learned it, because the woman at the door had had a list with a tick on it and not a name.** So the only record of the fact that a release on the Weir wall leg was being finished by a voice in a shape that was now being copied by strangers was in the head of a man of thirty-three who was on four and who had told nobody, and there is no job.

---

She caught him at the top of the steps at about ten to eight with the bar's tail still under her arm.

"Right," she said. "Next Monday."

"I will be there."

"You have not said yes to that in three weeks and you have not said no either, and I have not asked, because a man who says yes to a Monday when he has a wage is telling me something about the wage and I do not want to know what." She shifted the bar. "**I have noticed that you have started saying it earlier in the evening. In week fourteen you said it at about seven. In week eighteen you said it at about half past six. Tonight you said it at four minutes to ten past six before you had taken your jacket off.**"

He had not thought about that and it was true, and there was nothing to say.

"So I am going to ask you the question I said I would ask you, and you are going to answer it, and you do not have to tell me the answer." She looked at him. "Next Monday. Can you come."

"I do not know."

"Right," said Nkechi Osei, and she did not sound angry, and she did not sound like a woman being let down. "**That is the first time.** Three Mondays and you have not known. And I am not going to make you tell me which Monday, and I am not going to make you promise, and here is the whole of it and I want it said on the bank and not afterwards, because afterwards is a telephone and I do not trust a telephone with this.

"I did not ask you that to catch you out. I asked it because you are the only person on this bank who is paid to be somewhere else four days a week, and one day somebody who works out your rota is going to see that there is a Monday evening in your week with nothing in it, and they are not going to ask what is in it, they are going to take it. **I have watched that happen to a man on a different round and it took a Saturday off him and nobody in an office ever knew what it was for.** And the man it happened to is a man who is one of the good ones and I have never once been able to say so to anybody, because saying so would be saying there is a rule, and there is not one. There is a rota with a gap in it and a man who is good at a job and does not know why he is free on a Monday, and that is all it is, and in four years one of those things is going to go wrong in a way that somebody in a depot has to answer for, and there is nobody in that depot who will have been in the room."

Marek Senn stood on the mud with a rope coil under one arm.

"Whose rota," he said.

He had not meant to say it and it came out flat.

Nkechi Osei looked at him for a long moment.

"**Ours.**"

"I have not asked you who arranges it."

"You have just asked me whose it is, which is a different question and it is worse, and I am going to answer it and I am going to make you carry it, and I have thought about that for a week and I have decided that carrying it is the correct thing and I am not being kind to you." She put the bar down on the steps. "It is not my rota and it is not his. It is arranged and it has been arranged for a long time and nobody has written down that it is arranged, and you have been in it, and you did not put yourself in it, and I want you to hear the shape of what I am saying, because you have a rule that a rule only used when somebody else is not in it is a favour, and **that is true, and the sentence came from a man of twenty-one standing in a doorway counting to two out loud in week sixteen, and you have used it on a company once already and it is a good sentence and it is going to cost you something in about a fortnight, and it is not going to cost you a wage, it is going to cost you a Monday.**

"So do not tell me next Monday. Tell me on the Friday, in one sentence, before, and not afterwards. That is all I have ever wanted and it is smaller than you keep expecting it to be."

"Where were you on Saturday," he said.

It was not a change of subject he had planned. It came out of the fact that she had given him something and he had not got a sentence for it, and the only sentence he had was a question about a Saturday, and so he asked it.

Nkechi Osei looked at the river for a bit.

"Second one," she said. "The one off the Meridian. Went with about nine of us from the market, and there was a woman doing it in the sun, and she was very good, and there was a certificate at the end, and it cost nothing and nobody asked which firm anybody was with." She shifted her weight onto her left leg. "I have been on this round for eleven years and I have watched about nine men come off it, and every single one of them did it because a woman was good at something in the sunshine."

"That is not the same thing."

"It is to me it is the same thing, and I have never been able to say why, and I have had three days to try." She was not being dramatic and she was not asking for anything. "**A thing that arrives in a room and nobody voted for, and works, is going to keep arriving, and the reason it keeps arriving is that it works, and I am not able to make that go away and I have stopped trying.** You know the rest of it. You have been in the rooms. You know what the thing is when it is in a room, and you have not got a word for it when it is not, and I am a woman with a bar and I have not got a word for it either, and I have had eleven years."

They put the bar on the top step and washed their hands at the standpipe, and she had walked up Fell Road because her van had gone down it at about half eight with a man in it, and she did not mention that and he did not ask about it.

He got her to the top of the hill and she had the gate.

"Give me the four seconds on the Monday," he said. "Not the count. The four seconds you counted in on the Tuesday of week fourteen when the bar went off the lip. I want to know whether you are still coming down on four."

Nkechi Osei put her hand on the gate and looked at him, and laughed once, which she had not done in front of him in five weeks, and it lasted a second.

"I am still coming down on four, you daft man," she said. "**The number has not changed and the number was never the problem. The number is the only part of this that everybody is happy about, and you have been writing it in a book for a term and you do not know why it is there, and I have tried to tell you twice and you wrote it down both times like a man copying a fault code.**"

---

He was in the shop at about ten to nine and he had the book open on the bench and he wrote the round up first, because it was the day and it went in the order it happened.

Then he sat and looked at what he had written and thought about a man of thirty-eight saying four steps on a wall leg at half past seven on a Monday, and about a woman of sixty-one in a market walking sixty feet of cobbles with a hand truck on the Saturday and saying that gratitude has nowhere to go and so it does not move, and about a man of fifty-eight watering a strip of concrete by a fence for two hours and ten minutes because he did not want to stand in a canopy, and about a man of nineteen filling in a box on a sheet nine times at a time because somebody handed him a pen.

He wrote for about an hour and a half, which was longer than the entry deserved, and then he stopped, and he read it back, and it was about nine other people and it was the fourth page in a row that had been, and he had not decided that and he was fairly sure he had not decided it either.

*77. Monday of week 21. The round, and the count was nine, and I want the nine in front of the four that follow it because that is the order it happened in.*

*Nine.* A man of about thirty-four on the low end took nine steps and had a hand on the wall instead of on the bar. The round went quiet for about four seconds, which is not a stoppage, it is nineteen men not saying anything, and he said sorry, and a man behind him said *we're fine*, and Nkechi Osei said *right, up*, and we went up eleven times. **Nobody told him. Not me, not the man in front, not the man behind, and not her, and there is no form, no register and no column, and a man who took nine steps in week twenty-one is a man who will take nine steps by the end of the season, and the only place that fact exists is on a mud bank in about nineteen heads and mine is one of them and I have not got a box.** A count is not a thing anybody in this city has a job about. I have been writing it down for fourteen weeks and the book is a book in a shop and nobody has standing to read it, and a records request would be a request about ninety houses, and I am not going to do that to a bank.

*What I heard at half past seven and what it means and what it costs.* A man of thirty-eight on the wall leg, good at it, brought the rope in, put the hook on, turned round and said the thing, with a hand out flat a beat after the pull, the way it is done in a canopy in the sun. **The same four steps. On a bank, in the dark, on a Monday at ten past six, six weeks after a woman gave two hours and a certificate away in a back room off a kitchen for nothing.** He was not doing it on purpose. He cannot have been, because the woman at that door had a list with a tick on it and not a name, and I have never once seen him and I have stood next to him for a fortnight. So there is no record that a release on the Weir wall leg is being finished by a voice, in a shape, by men who did not know they were learning it, and the only record in the world is in the head of a woman who is on four and told nobody, and there is no job, and if there were a job it would be her and she would have to stop being on the bank to do it.

*The favour, and I have been living in it for five weeks.* She asked me tonight whether I can come next Monday and I said I do not know and she said *that is the first time* and she was not disappointed, she was **checking**, and the thing she is checking is whether a gap in a rota is a hole with a person in it. I have not thought about that once. I have had five Mondays with nothing in them and I have never once asked what the gap is, because a man who is paid to be somewhere else four days a week is supposed to be grateful about a free evening and is not supposed to run an audit of his own rota on a mud bank in the dark. **A rule you only use when somebody else isn't in it is not a rule, it is a favour. I have got one sentence that is any use to me and it is not mine, a man of twenty-one gave it to me in a doorway in week sixteen and counted to two out loud in front of a witness first, and I used it on a stranger in a doorway on Saturday morning and told him to keep it, and I have now heard myself use it in my own head about a rota, and it is aimed at me, and it has not cost me anything for five weeks and it is going to cost me something before the middle of next month.** That is the whole of the movement so far and it arrived at ten past six on a Monday behind a woman with a bar.

*Whose rota.* I asked. I did not mean to and it came out flat, and she said *ours*, and then she told me what I have written above, and she told me on purpose, and she said the reason she told me is that carrying it is the correct thing and she is not being kind. **I have not said a word to her about the Friday. I have not got a sentence ready and I have got until Friday and the sentence is about a rota and not about a bank and it is going to be the smallest thing I have ever said to anybody.**

*Do not know.* Whether the man on the wall leg had ever met the woman who taught him, and I am almost certain he had not, and if I am right then in about nine months there will be four men on four banks doing it in the dark to about eleven strangers and every one of them will be doing it because a man on a Saturday was pleasant to them, and none of them will be taught anything and all of them will be safer, and the accident rate on those banks will be the best it has been in nine years, and I will not be able to prove one word of it, and **there is a number in this city that somebody is keeping about exactly that, and she has refused to tell it to me three times, and I have started to think that the reason she has refused it three times is that she has already worked out that if I have it I will spend it, and she is right, and I have not got anywhere near being able to say that to her, and the Monday is in seven days.**
