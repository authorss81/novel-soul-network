# Chapter 77 — One at a Time

The telephone rang the shop at nine minutes past eight on the Thursday morning of week twenty-one and it was Pauline Ncube's voice and she said her name again, which she now did every time.

"Right," she said. "I am going to be quick because I have got a site at ten and this is not my desk. **The woman rang me. About eleven last night, from a number I have not got, and she said she had seen a page with her name on it and a line about a card on it, and she asked me two questions and I answered both of them truthfully, and the second answer is the reason I am ringing you.**"

"What did she ask."

"The first one was whether the note was real, and I said it was real and it was mine and I wrote it in a box because there was a box for it. The second one was whether anybody had asked her permission, and I said no, because nobody had." A van door. "She was not angry. I want that on the record from me to you, because you will assume she was angry and it matters. **She was about as angry as a person is when they find out they have been asked to write down a true thing and it has gone on a wall.**"

Marek Senn stood at the bench with his hand on the switch.

"What is the reason you are ringing me."

"Because there is one thing left in it that I cannot do and you can." She took a breath. "I have written to the eleven. All eleven, this morning, from a company address, saying what the document was, that we did not make it, that we are investigating, that they may ask us to confirm or correct what is on the page about them, and that we will destroy their occupational-health note at the end of the term if they ask us to in writing. I have written that last part into all eleven letters and I have never written anything like it before and I have got a line manager who is going to ask me why."

"That is a good letter."

"It is a **stupid** letter and it is the only one I can write, because the alternative is a letter that says we are investigating, and I have seen that letter go out about four times in six years and every one of the four people it was about got a phone call from their own firm within a fortnight, and I am not doing it again to a woman who is in a sling."

"Then why me."

"Because of the form," said Pauline Ncube. "Thursday of week nineteen. Rain, a tailgate, about four minutes, you wrote all fifteen boxes in your own hand and you wrote the line about the pallet on the activity line because I asked you to and because it was true, and then you read it back to me and you told me it was completely arithmetically correct and completely wrong, and you have been the only person in two years who has said the second half of that sentence to me. **And she does not know that you were the one holding the pen. Nobody has told her and I have not told her and I am not going to, because as soon as she knows a technician wrote it, a technician is a person she can be angry at, and she has got nobody in this to be angry at, which is a monstrous thing to be relieved about.**"

"Pauline—"

"I am not finished and I have got a site at ten. **What I want from you is a sentence for her. In her words, that she can read, about what happened that morning, that is longer than a box. Not an apology, I am not asking you for an apology and I would not send one. A sentence that says what actually happened, which is in your head and is not on the form, and I have read fifteen boxes for two days and there is not one sentence in them that is the true one.**"

"No."

"You have not asked me why."

"I am not going to."

"Then I am going to tell you why, because I would rather you did it badly than did it not at all." A road. "There are twenty-five things in that morning that are not on that form and I know roughly what eleven of them are because I was standing there, and if you write them down then you are a company's employee writing a company's incident out in longhand, and it will go to her, and it will be in her possession for the rest of her life, and **the only way she can use it is to go to a district, and the only thing in it that a district would want is the confirmation that a company supervisor said a word about the wrong load, and if that is in her hand in her own words, then in about two years there is a document in a regional office with a company's supervisor in it, and the woman's name in the file with it, because she is the injured party, and that is how a woman who was hurt on a Thursday becomes a fact about a person called Pauline Ncube.**"

"**That is the reason and it is a mechanism and it is the reason I did the form correctly in the rain and I would do it again.**"

There was nothing on the line for about three seconds.

"Right," said Pauline Ncube. "That is the correct answer and I asked you anyway, and I want you to know that I am not going to be a woman who says she did not ask."

"I know."

"**You are the second person in a year who has refused me a sentence I needed and the first one was a woman in Bower Street and she was kind about it and you have not been kind about it at all and I am going to think about that for a week.** I have got a site at ten. I am not going to ring her again tonight because she has rung me once and I am not going to be a person she has to ring twice."

The line went and Marek Senn stood in his own shop and had put a woman who was in a sling behind him, for the second time in three weeks, using the same eleven words, and the words were correct.

Petar Vance rang at eleven, from a van, and he did the thing he does.

"I have got three things and I am going to do them one at a time and I am going to give you the number first so that you do not spend the whole of the morning on it. **One. Denny Molloy.**"

"Forty-one?"

"Thirty-nine. Four years, two in the depot before that, does the rigs. Married, a boy of seven, and he is behind on his rent from the beginning of this month, and I have seen the letter because he asked me whether it was normal and I said it was not the same as being sacked and it is not."

"What happened to his hours."

"The Saturday and Sunday overtime is suspended from Monday, pending a review of the Lattice depot's records, which is a phrase that came out of an office on Wednesday afternoon at about four o'clock and I have read it and I do not believe anybody in that office believes it either. **It is not about Molloy. It is about every discretionary hour in that building, and there are about four men with discretionary hours, and he is one of them, and one of the other three has not worked an hour of overtime since the year before last.**"

"And the other two are the ones who have been covering my road days."

Marek Senn sat down on the stool.

"**Yes.** That is the one and it is the only one and I have been sitting in a van for ten minutes deciding whether to tell you or let you work it out, and I have decided to tell you, because you have been on this four days for three weeks and you have not once asked how a four-day week with a Monday and a Thursday clear gets done in a building with four men in it, and I have been waiting for you to ask and you have not, and not asking has been the answer and I have been letting you have it.**"

"I did not ask because it is a favour," said Marek Senn.

"Yes," said Petar Vance. "And there it is, in a shop, on a Thursday, at eleven in the morning, in about eleven words, and you did not have to be clever to get there and I have watched you get to harder things. Now listen to the rest of it, because the rest of it is the part I actually rang about.

"He is behind on his rent, and he is a man who has never once been late, and he asked me on Tuesday who the newest technician was. Not as a complaint. **As a question about a form.** There is a line in it in capitals in somebody's capitals, beside a name in a rota, that says a card is a condition of a callout and *ask him why he is not carded*, and Denny Molloy has been in that building every day this week and he has read it, and he asked me on Tuesday who the newest technician was, and I said nothing, and I have said nothing to about four people since Wednesday, and **I have never said nothing to four people in nine years and I have not slept since Tuesday.**

"He is not asking to be told anything. He is asking because there is a piece of paper in this city that says a man in a company has no card, and the man in a company is four days a week on a road with a rota that says he is free on Mondays, and there is a version of the arithmetic where a man who is free on Mondays is a man who is not being used."

Marek Senn put his hand flat on the bench.

"I am going to ring him," he said.

"You are not going to ring him."

"Petar—"

"**You are not, and here is the reason and it is the same reason as everything else this week, and it is that the second this depot works out that a technician in a van answered a question about a rota, then Denny Molloy stops being a question about a rota and starts being a question about you, and you are the newest, and there is a piece of paper in this city with your name on it, and I am trying to keep you off pieces of paper for about four more weeks and I am doing it badly.** You want to do something for that man. Do it by being the best technician he has ever worked beside and by not being the reason he has to ask. And when the review is over and the hours come back, he will not remember this week. They never do."

"Two. The service line," said Marek Senn.

Petar Vance made a noise that was almost a laugh.

"I have got to that. **It has not been answered since about half past four on Wednesday afternoon.** The out-of-hours number, the one on the bottom of a card, the one that about two thousand people in this district have written in a book or stuck in a fridge. It is a mobile, it is on a rota, and the rota is four men, and one of them is a man who has not done an out-of-hours in his life and does not know that he is on it, and one of them has been off sick since Tuesday, and one of them is not answering because he has decided that a company that put a piece of paper on a wall is a company he does not want to be reachable from, **and that one is not being unreasonable, Marek, and if anybody in that building tells you he is, you tell them to go and do the rota themselves for a fortnight.**"

"Who is the fourth."

"It is you," said Petar Vance. "You have it on your card. It is your job from Monday."

"I have not got a callout. I have not got a card either, and a card is a condition of a callout, and the whole city read that on Wednesday."

"**Then that is your first out-of-hours and I am sorry about the timing and I am not going to help you with it, because if I help you the man who is not answering finds out that somebody helped you and he is entitled to be angry about that instead of about the wall.**"

"Three," said Marek Senn.

"Three is a man rang your shop at about half past two and left a message and it is on the machine and it is the fourth message on that machine since Wednesday and none of them is from a person who is angry. Read them in order."

---

The messages were on the machine in the order they had come in and Marek Senn listened to all four.

The first was a woman at about eleven on Wednesday asking whether the company was the same as the one named on a document, and he had taken that call live.

The second was a man at about four on Wednesday afternoon, a stutter, saying his name and saying he had read a page and that his own name was on it and that he did not know what to do and would somebody ring him back, and he had given a number, and Marek Senn rang it at about half seven that evening and got a woman and she said he was at his sister's and could not come to the phone, and he left no message because he did not have a name to give her, and that was the correct decision and it was also the reason a man of thirty-one is sitting in a kitchen in Nine Bridges at half past eight at night.

The third was a woman at about ten on Thursday morning, brisk, asking for the occupational-health note to be sent to her at a private address and not to a work address, and then, before he could answer, saying that she had already sent that request to a district office in Crown Terrace, so there was no need.

The fourth was the man at half past two.

He said his name and said he had a rig off on a Friday to Monday, a big one, food handling, and that it had gone on a Tuesday afternoon and had not gone again, and that the number he had for it was the number on a card that a man in a lanyard had given him at a gate off the Meridian in week nineteen, and that he had tried the number from about ten o'clock and it had rung and gone to a machine, and that he had tried it at eleven and at one and at half past one, and that he had got a woman at about half past one who said it was not hers.

"You gave me that card," he said. "At the gate. You said it was the number for the out-of-hours and you said if it rang and nobody was there, leave it and somebody would ring you back."

"That is what I said."

"Then that's what I did. I left four." The man had a factory of about ninety people in him and a rig off since Tuesday. "I am not angry at you. I want to say that first, because I know what this looks like. I am just telling you that I have got about four days of product and no way to ring anybody, and I have got your number off a card, and if you cannot do it I would like to know that so I can stop ringing it."

Marek Senn looked at the mobile telephone on the bench with his own name on it and about four days of somebody else's product on it.

"I cannot do it," he said. "It is not mine to do and I am on it from Monday and not before, and that is the truth and I am sorry about it, and I am going to do one other thing this afternoon and I am telling you what it is so that you can hold me to it."

"What is that."

"I will ring every one of the four men on that rota and I will ask them, one at a time, whether they will take a callout on Saturday morning, and I will not tell them it is for you and I will not tell them why I am asking, and whatever they say, I will ring you back before six and tell you exactly what they said."

The man thought about that.

"That's a decent offer," he said. "That's better than what I got at half past one."

Talia Venn rang the shop at about four minutes past three on the Thursday afternoon of week twenty-one and she did not say the sentence about the day.

He had been waiting for it since about half past one, and when it did not come he found that he had been braced for the wrong thing, and when she had said nothing about it he noticed the not-saying the way you notice a step that is not there.

"That is four days in a row that I have not asked you anything," she said, before he had said anything at all. "That is not a record, it is a habit, and I have not decided to keep it and I am not going to claim it."

"You do not have to explain it."

"I am going to, because I have got a thing to say and I would rather it was not the first thing. **Something is wrong with your depot and you have said *fine* to me in the last second and a half, and I have known you for a year and a half and you have never once said fine to me about anything, including a plate of pasta.**"

"The depot is fine."

"Marek."

"Everything in the depot is fine. There is a bundle of paper on a chair and it has been dealt with and it is not a thing I am able to talk to you about this week."

"I have not asked what the bundle is. I want you to hear that. I have had a copy of it since about eleven this morning — Keogh passed it to me at about nine with a note that said *you will want to see this* and no opinion, and it is a training register and a rota and eleven health files, and about two hundred names, and I have read the same four pages four times on my own time and I have not put one word of it in a form." A chair, in a different building. "**And I am not going to ask you a single question about it, and I am going to tell you the price of not asking so that you cannot pretend it is a kindness.**

"In about three weeks there is going to be a woman in a regional office who is going to want to know whether a district contractor can be supplied with a company register. Not from a company. From a person. I am the person she will ask, because I am the one with a registration number and a route and no fixed employer, and I have wanted that question answered since the Wednesday of week seventeen. And I have just realised, sitting in a room in Crown Terrace on Thursday afternoon, that I am about three days away from being told that a document with two hundred names in it is on a counter in Lattice Ward, and that the only way I am ever going to get one is for somebody who has seen one to decide to give it to me, and that nobody is going to hand it to a district office and that everybody is going to hand it to a person they trust, and that the person they trust is going to be you, or a man in a district office who has met you once, or a woman of thirty-three on the Saltmarket route.

"And when that happens, every one of those people is going to have to decide whether they are a leak, and there is no form for that decision and there is no column for it, and **I am going to be one of the people who has to make it, and I am not going to be able to make it quickly, and it is going to cost me about a fortnight, and I am not going to be able to put it in a return.**"

"Tell me what you need."

"Nothing," said Talia Venn. "**That is the whole of the answer and it is the first one I have given you this year that has cost me something before it finished, and I am not going to be thanked for it and I am not going to be gracious about it, and you are not going to tell me I did the right thing, because I have not done the right thing. I have done the thing that does not put a woman in a sling in a bundle, and I have done it by not asking you a question, and I have made it your job to be somebody who can be asked, and I do not know yet whether that is a gift or a bill, and I have decided to send it to you either way.**

"And I am not going to be a person who finds out about your life on bad news, and I said that on a Sunday and I am saying it again on a Thursday and it is now twice and I am counting."

"Talia."

"Go and do the thing with the four men. I have got a return to write that I am not going to write."

The line went.

He rang the four men on the rota one at a time between half past three and a quarter to six on the Thursday of week twenty-one, in the order their names came on the sheet, and he did not tell anybody why he was asking, and he wrote down what each of them said in his own words because that is the rule in a book in a market and he has never once broken it.

The first said he could not think about it and would let his wife decide and would ring back on Saturday night.

The second said yes straight away, without asking what it was, and then asked what it was, and Marek Senn told him, and the second man said a word that is not in this entry.

The third said no and gave a reason about his own hands and it was a real reason and Marek Senn wrote it down as a real reason.

The fourth was not in. Marek Senn rang the mobile number off the rota twice and it rang out.

He rang the man with the rig back at five to six and told him exactly what each of them had said, in the order, without a word of his own, and the man thanked him and said that was good of him and that he would be all right.

At about seven he sat down at the bench with the load book open and a pen in his hand and he tried to write Nkechi Osei one sentence for the Friday, and he wrote four attempts and crossed out three of them, and the three that were crossed out were all about him.

The first one was that he might not be able to come on the Monday. That was a lie in the shape of an apology, and he knew it inside a second.

The second one was that he was sorry and it was a rota thing and he was working on it. That was three sentences and it was about a man's feelings and it had nothing in it for her.

The third one was that he had been asked to come off Mondays by somebody who had not asked him. Also a lie, and a worse one, because he had not been asked by anybody and he was the man who was about to ask.

He put the pen down.

**It is not about him. It has never been about him. He has been treating it as a question about whether a man of twenty-two turns up on a mud bank, and it is a question about whether a favour is a favour, and the only sentence that answers that is a sentence about somebody else's hours.**

It took him until about ten past nine at night to get it down to something he could say out loud without winching, and he wrote it in the book in one line and did not cross it out, and it is four clauses long and it is the smallest thing he has said to anybody in a term, and he has to make a telephone call before nine o'clock in the morning, and he has not got a clause that anybody could put in a form.
