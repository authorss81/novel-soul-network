# Chapter 72 — The Fourth Saturday

Rafi Pell came at about ten with a rucksack and a hood up and a hard hat on top of it, carried rather than worn, and he had been on Saturdays for about a month and had said the six weeks out loud exactly once, in a shop, in week nineteen, and had not said them since.

He put the rucksack down in the doorway because he had learned to do that in about eleven weeks.

"Right," he said. "Before you say anything. Yes, twelve pounds. Yes, the voucher. Yes, the yard off the Meridian, yes, and the hard hat is for the roof of the wagon, and no, I am not going to be at six o'clock on a Monday, and if you ring me at ten past six on a Monday I am not going to be annoyed, because I have been on a gate at ten past six on a Monday and it is my own fault."

"Noted."

"There is a fourth Saturday," said Rafi Pell. "Once a month, off the Meridian, a different yard, a woman comes out with a lanyard and a box of booklets, two hours, free, and it is not on anybody's clock because a Saturday man is not on a clock. That is not what I came for. **What I came for is that somebody rang the shop on Tuesday.**"

Marek Senn put the cloth down.

"Not you. They rang the shop and asked for me by my name, which means somebody has given a yard my telephone number, which I have not done, so somebody has guessed it, which I would like to be told about at some point. It was a woman and she said my name and said *the yard* and asked whether I would be interested in coming out on a Saturday morning, and she said there is a certificate at the end, and she said the fee is nothing because there is no employer, which means she knows, and she said it kindly, and Marek, I have been a courier for six years and I know exactly what that call is."

"Did you say anything."

"I said I'd ring back and I have not rung back. I have not said no and I have not said yes and I have told you, and I want it written on your page that as of this morning nobody in this city has asked me for anything, and that is a state and I would like it kept as a state, and if it changes I will tell you it has changed and I will not pretend it did not." He shifted the rucksack. "You have had a Saturday for a month. You have not used it once. I have noticed it every week and I have not said anything and I am saying it now at ten past ten on a Saturday morning because it is the last moment on a week that I can say it."

"I have noticed it as well."

"I know you have. It is why I said it." The bell took him out into the street and Marek Senn stood in the doorway and watched a man of twenty-one walk to a bus with a hard hat on top of a rucksack.

---

The hand set was on the bench with a card clipped to it saying the calibration was out of date, and the card had been out of date since the Monday of week nineteen, and it said *second Monday of last month*, which had stopped being true some time before the ink was dry.

A company would have caught it. A district office was shut at ten on a Saturday and a company depot was shut at ten on a Saturday because it is shut at the weekend, and there was no version of the week in which four contracted days and somebody else's rota covered a Thursday.

He had known that for eleven days. On a weekday it had been a nine-minute decision with a person standing in it. On a Saturday it was not a decision at all, it was a shape, and the shape was that a man is now four days a week in a building that is not his and the thing that is his has a card on it saying it is out of date, and the two facts do not touch anywhere.

He put the old card in the bin and wrote a new one, and dated it, and signed it, and put it on the bench where he would see it on the Monday, and the reason he did it on the Saturday instead of the Tuesday was not virtue. It was that on a Tuesday he would have rung somebody first.

---

He left the green cord in the van.

That was not an accident and he had thought about it for about a minute in the yard at half past nine, and the reason was that the yard took a card at the gate off anybody, and he had been on about forty gates in five weeks, and the last one had opened eleven feet without a word, and he wanted to know what a gate did when the man at it had nothing on.

The gate did nothing. There was no card and no list and a man in a fluorescent jacket who lifted the barrier and said "morning" and looked at the concrete instead of at him, and Marek Senn drove past the office and parked where there were eleven other cars, and got out, and was a man in a work jacket with no badge on him at all.

The yard was hard standing with a canopy at one end and about twenty men on it, and a trestle table with a district's clip of attendance sheets on it, and a woman putting out folding chairs. The woman with the lanyard arrived at about twenty to ten in a white car with a box of booklets under her arm, and she was not Yvette Kohl, and Marek Senn stood at the other end of the canopy with his hands behind his back and felt the whole of the thing arrive on him like weather.

It is the same forty minutes.

He had watched it five times now: a hall with forty-one people in it, a borrowed back room with forty, a plate in a customer's storeroom that took him four minutes, a canopy on a district's yard for twenty-one, and this. Same photograph at the front, different. Same four steps. Same pause, and the pause is not hesitation, and he has known that since the Tuesday of week fourteen and had it in a book ever since. The same man in the room finishing a hard thing with his voice, and the hard thing getting a little easier each time, and the getting-easier being the whole of what anybody has ever been sold.

A man of about forty got it wrong twice. She did not correct him the first time. The second time she said something about the load and he got it right and looked at nobody, exactly as a man of fifty had done for her in a market in a back room in week fifteen.

At about ten to eleven she asked a question of the room and every one of the twenty turned round and looked at each other, and about nine hands went up, and she took the last one, and it was a man of about sixty with a bad back who had worked out of a van until about four years ago.

He got it right first time. He stood there afterwards with his arms out of his sides like a man who had put something down.

A boy of about twenty-two came and got a chair out of the stack at about ten past eleven and then did not go back to the group, and stood four feet off, and looked at the back of Marek Senn's shoulders for a while, and then said:

"You haven't got a lanyard."

"No."

"You've not done the course either." He was not being clever about it, he was reading a yard the way a young man reads a yard. "She does the whole thing in about two hours, that one. Somebody said you could be done by Christmas if you were quick about it. Are you not going to?"

"Not this Christmas."

"Why?"

It was a fair question and it was asked in a doorway in about a second and a half, and Marek Senn had had a term and a half to get a sentence ready for it and had not one.

"Because there is a reason and it is about six other people and I am not going to say it to you on a Saturday," he said. "And because I could say it and you would nod, and then in about four months somebody would ring you and ask you to confirm a release to somebody learning, and you would be twenty-three and holding a card, and you would do it, because you would be good at it. And then there would be two of us."

The boy thought about that for longer than he needed to.

"That's the fourth beat, isn't it," he said. "My cousin did this in Ashfields and the fella who came out said you can hear it in about nine hundred yards."

"Nobody has said that to me before."

"Right." The boy put the chair down on the stack and went back to the group.

Marek Senn stood in the sun at the end of a canopy and did not move for two minutes, and the reason he did not move is not modesty.

---

Marek Senn was at the fence line with a full bladder and a bad shoulder by twenty past eleven and he was about to go and find a gap in a hedge when the man of sixty came past him and stopped, and did not ask who he was, which he had expected, and said:

"You're the one who came and wrote on the sheet."

"No."

"Then you weren't." Lenny Trigg shifted his feet. "I want to ask you something and I'll go straight away after. There's a Saturday coming, is there not. Fourth one. She said it was a rule."

"Who said it was a rule."

"Somebody said it was a rule. I've heard it four times. I don't know who said it first." He rubbed his back. "I don't want the card. I've been on a wagon thirty-one years and I know more about a load than anybody with a certificate, and I want to say that to somebody who doesn't sell them, and you're the first one in a yard in about two years who hasn't got one on."

"I have not got a card."

"Right." He took that in and did not appear surprised. "Then you're the right one to ask. **Is the Saturday a rule, or is it a favour?** Because if it's a rule then I'm coming because I'm meant to, and I'll come in the rain. And if it's a favour then I'm coming because somebody did me a kindness, and in about three weeks the kindness will have worn off, and then I'm not coming, and then somebody in an office is going to be very interested in why twenty men on a Saturday didn't turn up, and it won't be them that's not come. It'll be the one who arranged it. And there is not one of them in this yard and I have looked."

Marek Senn looked at about eleven feet of chain-link and thought about a doorway in Lattice Ward in week sixteen and a man of twenty-one standing in daylight counting to two out loud in front of a witness.

"A rule you only use when somebody else isn't in it is not a rule," he said. "It's a favour."

Lenny Trigg did not move for a second.

"That's the first time anybody's said that to me," he said, "and I have said it to my wife twice and she told me to stop."

"Two more things and then I'll let you go, because I can see you've got somebody waiting." The man did not. "**Do not say it to anybody else. Not because it is wrong. Because in about a month it will be on a board, and on a board it will have a reason on it, and the day it has a reason on it is the day a man who does not come has let somebody down, and that will be you, and you have not done anything to anybody.**"

"You have given me the sentence and told me to keep it," said Lenny Trigg. "That's a strange way to be generous."

"I am not being generous. I've just watched a gate open eleven feet for a lanyard and a company's instruction go up on a customer's wall over a district's own card without anybody taking the old one down, and I have worked out in about eleven seconds that the whole of it is this, and I have not got anywhere near doing anything about it."

He looked up at the canopy, where a woman was putting booklets into a satchel and a man of nineteen was carrying two chairs to a stack.

"Never mind," he said. "I don't expect you to say anything else. You haven't got a lanyard and you haven't got a card and you are the first person in about two years who has not tried to give me one of them." He put his hand out, dry and hard as a fork tine. "Lenny Trigg. Thirty-one years. I do the second wagon."

They shook on it and Marek Senn went and found a gap in a hedge behind eleven parked vans and stood in it for a while, and then drove to Lattice.

---

The thirty chairs were in the Saltmarket at about half past two, on a hand truck, in a row.

The woman pushing it was Ena Truslove and she was doing it herself because there was nobody in that market on a Saturday afternoon to do it with. They had gone on the Saturday before in a van at about eleven and they had been in a room on the third floor of a building in Bower Street for about nine hours that morning, and about twenty women had been on a district's books, and nobody had asked her what they were doing with her chairs and nobody was going to thank her. She had brought them down a set of steps and across about sixty feet of cobbles and was not going to explain herself to anybody and did not.

"You have not been thanked," said Marek Senn.

"No."

"Nobody asked you."

"No." She stopped the truck. "That is three times and I have been thinking about it on and off since the Saturday and I have worked out the shape of it, and I would like to say it to you and then I would like to go home.

"It is not that nobody is unkind. It is that there is no box for the person who gave them. That is all it is. **I have been in a room for eleven years where everything anybody does for anybody gets four columns and a rule and the name of whoever took the call, and the whole of that is a box for the person who did the thing and a box for the person who was on the telephone.** And a district booking for a Saturday for about twenty women on their books has a room and a date and a number of chairs, and it does not have a field anywhere on the paper for the woman whose chairs they are, because she is not a supplier and she is not on a list and she is not in employment of anything. So the thirty chairs went and came and there is no line anywhere with my name on it, and the reason nobody has thanked me is not that nobody is grateful, it is that **gratitude has got nowhere to go and so it does not move.**"

"You could put a card in the tin."

"I've thought about it. I have thought about it walking up sixty feet of cobbles with a hand truck." She looked at him. "And that is the other half of it and it is why I have not. If I put thirty chairs in that book in my own words and sign it, then in about a year that book says a nursery gave a room to a district. And that is not a lie, and it is not the thing I did, and there is no column in that book for the two Saturdays either."

"You want both Saturdays in it."

"I want the four seconds in it. I said yes in about four seconds, twice, and I have never once had that said back to me in a form, and I am sixty-one years old and I have run a nursery for eleven years and I have never in my life been on a rota for anything." She took hold of the truck again. "Four seconds is not a big thing. I would like it in a book that is a book and not a diary. That is all. And you can tell Bea Nunn that and she will say the same thing she said on the Wednesday, and I will put it in the tin and she will take it at the next one and read it out and it will be four minutes and it will be true."

"I will tell her."

"Do not tell her I have thought about it for three days. Tell her I said it in four seconds."

She went. The hand truck went over the cobbles with a sound like somebody counting, and Marek Senn stood in the Saltmarket and looked at a bench and then at thirty plastic chairs stacked against it in the order they had been stacked in the first place.

---

He walked back up past the laundrette under his own flat at about half past four on the Saturday of week twenty with nothing in his hands, and he did not go up, and it was the second time in nine weeks, and there was a woman in there on a Wednesday of week twelve who had asked him four times to come round on a bus and not to look at it.

At about seven he sat down and wrote the shortest page in the book.

---

*75. Saturday of week 20. Three rooms, and I did not earn one of them, and I want that on the page before anything else.*

*What a Saturday is.* Four days a week, Monday to Thursday, on a rota, and a Saturday is not one of them, and for the first time in about four months a Saturday is a day of mine. **I have had five of them and I have spent every one of them doing nothing on purpose, and I want that written down in the shape it deserves, which is: five Saturdays of nothing, in a job, for money, and not one of them went anywhere.** A man of twenty-one stood in my doorway at ten past ten this morning and said *you have had a Saturday for a month and you have not used it once*, and he said it as a fact and not as a favour, and it is the first time in a fortnight that anybody has told me a true thing about myself that I had not already written down, and I would like it on the page in his mouth and not in mine.

*The yard.* Fifth time. Same forty minutes, same photograph, same pause, and the pause is not hesitation and I have known that for five weeks and have never once been in a room where I could show anybody. Twenty men, about nine hands, a man of about forty wrong twice, a man of sixty right once and then stood there with his arms out like a man who had put something down. **I stood at the other end of the canopy for an hour and ten minutes and I did not go over, and this time it was not Kosta and it was not a district assessor with a clipboard, and it was because I have no lanyard on and there is nothing in that yard that says a technician came to look at a load.** I want the correct reading of that written down and it is not humility and it is not discipline. It is that a lanyard has been doing my scruples for me since the Monday of week nineteen and I have not tested whether I have any.

*The doorway, and what I gave away.* A man of sixty-one, thirty-one years, second wagon, no card, no interest in one, asked me one question: is the Saturday a rule or a favour. And I said the sentence, out loud, in a yard, to a stranger, in somebody else's words. **I have been carrying that sentence since the sixteenth week and I used it on a company once already and now I have used it on a stranger in a doorway, and I have told him not to repeat it, and I told him not to repeat it because of what I saw on a customer's wall in week nineteen, and he has taken it as a kindness.** That is the third time in three weeks that the right thing has come out of my mouth with a bad motive attached, and I do not know what to do with that, and the honest answer is that I did know at the time and did it anyway, and I did it because I have got exactly one sentence that is any use to me and it is not mine.

*What the chairs cost, and it is not thirty chairs.* Ena Truslove walked sixty feet of cobbles with a hand truck she pushed herself, and she has now been thanked for a thing three times and has found out why, and the reason is structural and I would like somebody to write it down in a bigger book than this one: **gratitude has got nowhere to go, so it does not move.** There is no field anywhere on a district booking for the person whose chairs they are, so the fact that the chairs went and came has no line on it with a person against it, and three Saturdays have now ended with nothing moving anywhere. She has thought about a card for three days and has decided to write it in four seconds and not in three days, and she has told me to say the four seconds and not the three days, and I am going to say the four seconds.

*Two registers in one city, and neither of them will do the other.* In that market on a Saturday a woman with a district's badge will have a register with a tick on it and not a name, exactly as she had at a back room door in week fifteen. In that yard this morning a woman with a company's lanyard had a clip of attendance sheets and about twenty names on it. **That is two registers of trained people inside about four hundred yards of each other and neither one can be produced to a woman in a wheelchair in Bower Street, and neither one can be produced to the other, and in about a year somebody is going to publish one of them and there is going to be a third place that has both.** I noticed that at about one o'clock this afternoon with my back against a wall and I have not told anybody and I do not intend to be the one who does.
