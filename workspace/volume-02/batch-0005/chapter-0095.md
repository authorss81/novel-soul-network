# Chapter 95 — Nobody Took a Vote

Rafi Pell had the Friday off and used nine hours of it and Marek Senn found out about three of them at eleven in the morning, standing in a queue behind him at a bus shelter outside a transit hall in Nine Bridges, which is not where either of them had planned to be.

"What are we doing."

"**I'm going to see the man in the booth,**" said Rafi Pell. "**I've been thinking about it since the Tuesday of week twenty-four and I have got to the point where I cannot not do it, and you are going to stand there and say nothing, and I have told you that in advance so that you can't be in it.**"

"Fine."

"He's got a book of them in there. Grey ones. About the size of a playing card and twice as thick." Rafi Pell had his rucksack on and his coat with a form of identity in it and a card in his wallet, and he was talking at about the speed of a man who has said a thing out loud for the first time. "**They're for people who lose them. I have seen the book. He hands them out and everybody who takes one loses it again, and he says so, and he says it as a warning, and he is not wrong.**"

"Why are you going."

Rafi Pell did not answer for about nine seconds.

"**Because there are four beats printed on it and the fifth line is blank,**" he said, "**and I have been walking about for a fortnight with a card in my wallet that says I can be confirmed by a supervisor, and I do not want that, and I have not got a way of saying that to anybody, and the reason I have not got a way is that the only way anybody has ever talked to me about this is by telling me I am safer with a thing, and if I say the sentence I actually want to say then I am a man of twenty-one who was in a canteen and has a grievance.**"

They went in.

The booth was about four metres by three and it had a window and a heater and a rack of timetables that had not been reorganised since about the middle of the summer, and a man of about sixty behind the counter who had been there, by his own account, since before the shelf was a shelf.

"Two of them, were you after."

"One."

"**You are the second one this year.**" The man did not look up. "Everybody asks for two. Two is for a person who is thinking about it. One is for a person who has got a thing they have to do and has not got anybody to say it to."

Rafi Pell did not say anything.

"Book's under the glass. Take it out, I cannot let you out with it and nobody can stop me making a note." He turned a page. "**It is a transit sequence card. It is the oldest printed thing in this building and we reprint about nine hundred a year and we have never changed a word on it, and the fifth line has been blank since before I came, and the reason it is blank is not a mistake, and if anybody asks me why it is blank I am going to tell them I do not know, and I do not know.**"

Marek Senn said, quietly, "Who set it."

"**Nobody knows. It is not ours and it is not a council's and it is not a company's. It is a sequence for a platform crew and it has got four steps on it and a line for the fifth, and the line is for something that somebody has to write in themselves, and a hundred and forty of these go out a year and nine come back with the fifth line filled in, and I have never once seen one of the nine.**"

Rafi Pell took the card out from under the glass. It was grey, and about twice the thickness of a playing card, and printed in a typeface that had not been used for a printed timetable in this city for a very long time, and there were four numbered lines and then a ruled line with nothing on it.

"Right," said Rafi Pell.

"**Take the card,**" said the man. "**And do not bring it back, because if you bring it back I have to write it down.**"

He wrote the fifth line in the booth on a plastic table with a biro off a string, and he counted to two first, out loud, in a bus shelter's worth of glass room, and the man behind the counter looked at the ceiling while he did it and did not comment, which Marek Senn has thought about since.

It took him four minutes. He wrote, and stopped, and rubbed two words out, and wrote them again, and then he read it out loud once, which he had not meant to do.

"*I do the step without the count. If the count does not come out loud, I do not use the step.*"

"That is it?" said the man behind the counter.

"That is the fifth line," said Rafi Pell. "**It is not a fifth step. Everybody will want it to be a fifth step and it is not. It is the other way round: the four steps are the steps and the fifth line is me finding out whether I need the word from outside at all, and the answer for me is no, and I have known that for about two years and I have never once found out whether it was true, because you cannot find out without trying it on purpose.**"

"And if you get it wrong."

"**Then I fall over a rail and I have not got a card in my hand saying a man confirmed me, and I get up.**"

The man behind the counter wrote something in a book that was not the timetable book and Marek Senn did not see what and did not ask, and he has not asked since, and that restraint is the only thing he did on that whole day that he is certain of.

"**Do not sign it,**" said the man. "**Everybody signs. And then it is a document. And then it is a document about a thing a man did on a wagon, and the document has a date on it, and the date is the day a company could use.**"

"It's got my name on the front."

"It has got a name in a box on the front and nobody has to look at the front to know whose it is." He shut the book. "**Write your two names on it and leave the date off. That is my opinion and I have not got a form for it.**"

The wagon share is nine people in a lock-up off Weaver Street with a bench that came off a skip and a van they own between them, and Rafi Pell had been a member of it for about eleven days.

He said so in the doorway of the shop at about half past four on the Friday afternoon, with the card in his hand and a plastic bag of bolts in the other, and he said it before he came in.

"**I have joined a co-operative,**" he said. "**It is not the Exchange and it is not the Choir and I want both of those said out loud and I want you to notice that I have said them out loud in that order, which is the order of how much they would have mattered.**"

"Congratulations."

"Do not do that." He came in and put the bag down. "**It is nine of us and we rent the lock-up between us and we own a third of a van and we pay about nine hundred a year between the nine of us to stand in a room, and I have been in it eleven days and have been on the bench twice.**"

"Anything else about it."

Rafi Pell put the grey card on the bench.

"**Nobody took a vote about me.**"

Marek Senn looked at him.

"**That is the sentence. That is the whole of it and I have been carrying it since about the Wednesday and I have said it to about four people this week and every one of them has said why have you, and here is the answer: because if they had taken a vote about me then it would be a thing that had been voted on, and a thing that has been voted on is a thing that can be voted on again, and the next vote would be about whether I do it on a Saturday or a Sunday.**"

"Who took the vote."

"**Nobody. There is no vote. The bench was voted on, the van was voted on, the rent was voted on, and there are nine names on a bit of paper by the door with what everybody pays on it, and that bit of paper is the only thing in that room that anybody would call a document, and it is a list of nine amounts and nothing else.**" He put his hand flat on the card. "**I told the woman who runs the diary at the depot about it on the Monday, because I have a card now and cards have to go somewhere, and she said write it down and bring it in and I said no, and she said why, and I said because the second it is in a diary it is in a system, and she said: whose system, and I said yours, and she did not argue and she has not asked me again.**"

"And the two names," said Marek Senn.

Rafi Pell turned the card round.

On the front, in the box, under a printed line that said *practitioner*, was a name. Under the four steps, in the fifth line, was what he had written in a booth. And along the bottom edge, in the same biro, small and neat and separate from the printing, two more names and a line and a word.

"They are on the front," he said. "**Gus Haldane. He shouts keep it up the rail at people and he has been shouting it at me since I was nineteen and he is the only person in my life who has ever given me a practice and did not write it down, and he works at the fish market every day of the week and if you want to end this you go and stand in front of him at about seven in the morning and he will not know what you are talking about and you will have to explain it, and that is on purpose.**"

"And the second."

"**Nia Bramhall. She is at the depot and she books them and she gave me the card in four seconds and she told me a card is not a certificate that says you cannot work without it and she has never once asked me for a reason for anything, including this.**"

Marek Senn did not say anything.

"**Say the thing,**" said Rafi Pell.

"I have not got a thing."

"**You have got a thing and it is that you are not one of them, and I have known that since the Wednesday of week twenty-four when you stood in a shop doorway and I had to say that you are the man I told and not the fourth thing, and you have never once been in a room where you were not the man somebody told, and it is the only sentence anybody has ever offered you and you have never used it.**"

"I work for the company that sells the card."

"**I know you do.**"

"**Then you have got the reason and you do not need me to say it.**"

"No, I do not need you to say it," said Rafi Pell. "**I have worked out the reason myself on a Saturday, on a wagon, at about half past five in the morning, counting to two. If you are one of the two people who can end it, then in about four years somebody in that building can ask what it is, and you will have to write it down, and it will be a company thing with a reference number on it, and the two names on the front of the card become a company's document about a practice, and I have spent a fortnight learning what happens when two people are a document.**"

"**That is right,**" said Marek Senn. "That is exactly right and I want you to know that I have got a sentence about it and the sentence is: a practice with two named people who can end it is not a card, is not a certificate, is not a Working Share, is not in a system, and cannot be produced to anybody, and it is the only one of the four things anybody has built in this term that can be ended by two people without a form.**"

"Then say the rest of it."

"The rest of it is that I could not do it anyway, and I want to be accurate about why, because I have spent a term being accurate about why. **The reason is not the company. The company is a piece of a reason I could survive.** The reason is that you have written the fifth line and I have not written anything, and in about four years there is going to be a room and one of the two men in it is going to be able to say where the fifth line came from, and it is going to be me, because I have been in a term of rooms, and the day it is me you have got a document about a practice with a man's name on it who was paid by a company that sells the thing on the other four lines."

Rafi Pell looked at the bench.

"**Right,**" he said. "**Then that is the fifth decision this term you have made by not saying something, and I have now been in five, and I want to say that I am not angry, because you are the only man in my life who has ever been honest with me about the cost of a thing and every one of the five has cost me something and none of them has cost me the card.**"

"That is a good line."

"**I have got nine of them and nowhere to put them, and I am telling you that as a fact and not as a complaint, and you are the man I tell, and that is a category and I made it up in a doorway.**"

He put the card in the rucksack, and then took it out again and put it on the bench.

"**You are going to keep this on your shelf, and I know you are going to keep it on your shelf, and I have counted to two about it and I have decided that is allowed, because there are nine of us and a lock-up and a van and a bench, and one of those nine is a man who cannot get a job at the moment and does not want one, and the shelf in a repair shop is the only one of the ten places it could go that nobody has voted for either.**"

Marek Senn was at the depot at ten to one on the Friday when Ellis Marsh rang the out-of-hours line and Marek Senn took it in the corridor because it was the corridor, because his office is a corner of a corridor.

"M-M-M-Marek. It's E-E-Ellis Marsh. I got the number off the b-b-b-board."

"Go on."

"I'm sorry, I want to say this w-w-whole. I've practised it. I've had it for a fortnight." A breath. "**They put the word NONE in a box about a hand. And nobody has ever asked me about my hand.**"

Marek Senn stood in a corridor with a lanyard on.

"Yes," he said.

"**I s-signed it.**"

"You signed it."

"**I signed it on the W-W-Wednesday, and I rang them back because Rae said she wasn't going to, and I needed the hours, and they asked me if the note was accurate and I said yes, and it is accurate, and that is the whole of what is true about it and I have never said it out loud before.**"

Marek Senn looked down the corridor. A young man in a fleece with a company badge was putting envelopes in a bin and the badge was in range.

"Ellis."

"**That's it. That's the whole thing. I put the s-shift back on for myself, and the h-hand is in a file in a company for g-good, and I have got to be able to s-say that was a trade and not a tragedy, and I am not going to be one of the ones who got p-proud of it, and if anybody comes and tells me it was b-b-brave I am going to say something I have not got ready.**"

"Nobody is going to come and tell you that."

"**My old fella will,**" said Ellis Marsh. "**He'll do it at Christmas.**"

Marek Senn stood there for four seconds with a receiver against his ear and nine true sentences in his mouth and one of them, only one of them, that he was allowed to say.

"I'm not going to say anything about that," he said. "**You're right about what it was, and you're right about what it cost, and both of those are yours and I'm not going to take either of them off you or put a nicer word on them.**"

"That's it, is it."

"That's it."

"**Right.**" A pause of about two seconds. "**The m-man who made the h-hand thing. The w-weekly. I have got it on a s-sheet. I have got it on a sheet with a m-m-man on it in a m-m-market.**"

Marek Senn did not breathe.

"**And I have read it about nine times and I have not put it anywhere, and I have got it in a f-file in my k-k-kitchen under a t-t-tin of tea, and I have not decided what to do with it, and I have not got anybody, and I know you are the fourth thing in it and I am not going to put you in it, and I am telling you that it exists and I am putting the phone down now.**"

Click.

Marek Senn put the receiver back on its cradle and stood in the corridor for about a minute, and then he went and did a job on a gate in Nine Bridges that took him twenty minutes, and the twenty minutes were the best twenty minutes of his week, and he has written that down in this entry and not anywhere else.
