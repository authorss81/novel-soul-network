# Chapter 91 — Nineteen Years

The out-of-hours line rang at ten past eight on the Monday morning and Marek Senn picked it up on the first ring because it was the line and he was the one who had it that week.

"Out of hours."

"**It's Dai Pritchard.**"

He put his hand flat on the bench. He had four things in the drawer under it with reference numbers on two of them, and one of the four was a piece of A4 in his own handwriting with no date on it that he had put there on the Tuesday of week twenty-four because a woman of sixty-eight had told him something about dates and he had used it on a second person inside a week without asking her.

"Right," he said.

"I'm not going to talk for long and I want to get it in the right order, because I've had it since Sunday night and I have said it out loud to my wife nine times and she has been all right with it, which is more than I expected." A chair. A television somewhere behind it, not turned up. "**I decided I would answer the telephone. That's it. That's the whole of the decision. Not a grievance and not a resignation and I'm not saying I've been hard to work with, because I haven't, and if anybody tells you I have, you tell them I did my days.**"

"That is not a decision anybody has to make on a Sunday," said Marek Senn.

"It's a decision I had to make on a Sunday, because the alternative was to keep deciding it every Wednesday, and I have been doing that since the Wednesday of week twenty-one, and it has stopped being a decision and started being a job." A pause of about a second and a half. "**And there's a second bit and I'm going to say it before you can say anything helpful, because you'll have something helpful and I don't want it yet.**"

"Go on."

"**I want the card.**"

The extractor was on. Neither of them said anything for about four seconds.

"Which card," said Marek Senn.

"The one with my name on it. Module one and two. The free one." The chair again. "Nineteen years. I've never had one, nobody's ever asked me for one, and on the Wednesday morning of the twenty-first I stood in a canteen off the Meridian with a tea in my hand and read a line on a piece of paper that somebody else had printed, and the line had my name on it and the word NONE in a box, and I sat in my van for about an hour. **And here is the thing I have not said out loud to anybody, and I am saying it to you because you are the man who answered it, and I have worked out that being unfindable is the expensive one. It isn't the wall. It is that nobody rings you.**"

Marek Senn sat down on a stool.

"Dai."

"**I am not asking you to get it for me. I would be ashamed of asking you to get it for me.** I am telling you that I have spent a fortnight not answering a telephone because I did not want to be a man anybody could find, and it turns out that not being findable is not a position. It is just being alone with a decision, and I have been alone with it since the Wednesday, and on Sunday night I worked out that I would rather have a card and be findable than have neither.** And I am not going to say any more about the paper, and I am not going to say why, because a reason given to a technician in a shop turns into a story in about four days, and I have decided I would rather have *he never said*."

"That is the right decision," said Marek Senn, "and I am not going to argue with a word of it."

"**I know. I have been on hold with about four people this month who argued with me.**"

"Here is the part I am bad at, and I am going to say it instead of sitting on it." He turned the phone round so it was flat on the bench. "A fortnight ago I told you a sentence on this telephone and you have just used it, and I want you to know that you have used it correctly and I am not happy about it. I said a company is not a wall. I said it is four men on a rota and a woman in an office. **And what you have done with it, in ten days, worked out that the way out of a wall is to be issued a card by it. I gave you a true sentence and you have made a document out of it, and the document is correct, and I have spent a term in this city telling people that the whole of the trouble is a document.**"

Nothing on the line for about two seconds.

"**You're the fourth thing,**" said Dai Pritchard.

"I am not the fourth thing in this. I am the man you told, and I have been told the difference by a man of twenty-one in a shop doorway and I have thought about it every day since, and it is the only sentence anybody has offered me this term that I could actually use, and this is not it." Marek Senn put his hand over his face for a second and then took it off again because there was nobody to see it. "**And I cannot give you one argument against the card, and that is the first time in a term that has happened, and it is because every argument I have got needs a document and I am not going to be the man who hands you one.**"

"Then don't," said Dai Pritchard. "**I'm not asking you for an argument. I'm ringing you because the rota says my name on it on Monday and I have got a decision to tell somebody about, and I have decided that if nobody in that building knows that I decided it, then in four years there'll be a note that says the fourth man stopped answering, and that note will be true, and it will be the only true thing anybody will have.**"

"Right," said Marek Senn. "Then say it properly, and say it to the rota, and say it on the telephone to me now, and I will write it in the book with the time on it, and there is no name on it and no reference on it and no company on it."

"You are writing that down?"

"**I am writing that a man telephoned the out-of-hours line on the Monday morning of week twenty-five at ten past eight and said he was answering it and had not resigned and had not stopped doing his days. I am not writing that he wants a card, because that is between him and a training clerk, and if it is ever in a book it will be in a company's book, and it will be the fourth thing in a room and he will not have asked for that.**"

There was a pause on the line of about three seconds.

"That," said Dai Pritchard, "is the second time."

"Which?"

"**Nobody has said the right thing first.**"

The depot is on the far side of the city from the bench in Lattice and it takes fifty minutes in the light, and Marek Senn was in it at about ten to ten with a job sheet for a customer in Nine Bridges that would take nine minutes.

Nia Bramhall had the training diary open on the corner of the desk with about nine hundred of them in it between now and the end of the term, and she had a telephone handset in one hand and a pen in the other and she said, before he got to the desk, "I cannot book you."

"You have not tried."

"I have tried twice and I cannot do it a third time because the third time is the one where I am the person who has done it on purpose." She turned the diary round. "**Release module. Tuesday and Wednesday, eight till four, on the clock, a card at the end of the second day. Every technician out of this depot is booked on it. I have got four hundred and six of them in a hall for module three and there is a rule that says nobody takes a release course without a release card, and I have tried to book you twice, and the system will not let me, and it will not tell me why in a way I can put in a return.**"

"Then it will tell me why."

"It will tell you because there is a course exception against your name, and a course exception is four fields, and the fourth field is reason, and it is in your handwriting." She turned the diary back. "I am not going to read it out in a depot office. **I am going to tell you that it exists, because I have been asked twice by somebody who is not me and I would rather you heard it from me than from a system.**"

Marek Senn stood there for about four seconds.

"It says I have not got a reason I am willing to give," he said.

"Yes."

"That is what it says."

"Yes, and I have read it about four times this week and every time I have read it I have thought the same thing and I am going to say the thing." She put the pen down. "**You are on grade two. Grade two does not go out on a release callout. Grade two does not get the release rate, which is two and a half times the shop rate, and grade two does not get the Saturday, and if you are grade two for the whole of next term then the only thing that has happened to you this term is that you have been given a lanyard.**"

"I know what grade two is."

"I am not finished, because I have booked four hundred and six of these and I have watched about nine hundred of them come through this desk in three years and there is not one of them who could tell me the difference between a thing that is safe and a thing that is somebody's job, and I do not mean that unkindly, I mean that the difference is the entire content of the course and the course does not contain it." She picked the pen up again. "And I am going to say the other half. **You have written in a box in our system, in your own hand, that you have not got a reason you are willing to give, and a man who will not say why is a man who has not got a reason, and a man who has not got a reason is a man who is going to be in a room on his own one day with a card in his pocket and a thing he cannot do, and there is nobody in this building whose job it is to notice that, and I have noticed it, and I am a training clerk with a diary.**"

"Right," said Marek Senn.

"That is all. Go and do your nine minutes."

He did the nine minutes. He did them in eight and a half and he did not write anything on the sheet about the conversation, and in the van on the way back he did the arithmetic he has done nine times this term, which is that a release callout at about two and a half times the shop rate is about eleven pounds an hour instead of about four and a half, and four days a week is sixty-two pounds instead of twenty-five, and the difference over a term is about nine hundred pounds, and nine hundred pounds is a rent, and his mother's shift pattern, and a card for a course, and he has known the figure since the Monday of week nineteen and has not once written it down in the book.

Denny Molloy came into the shop at about eleven with a sheet of paper in a plastic wallet that he did not take out of the wallet.

"I'm not on a job," he said. "I want you to read a bit of paper and I'm not going to leave it with you."

"Sit down."

"I'd rather stand, because if I sit down it's a meeting." He turned the wallet round. "That's company paper. There's a form reference at the top and a date and a paragraph. It's asking me to confirm that a note they hold about me is accurate, and there's a tick box, and it came to my house on Friday."

Marek Senn read it with his hand flat on the bench so that the man could see him not taking it.

"Right," he said. "**This is good of them. This is genuinely good of them and I am not going to stand here and tell you it is a trap, and I want you to hear the reason before I say anything else, because the reason is why it is good.**"

"Go on."

"They found out from a copy of the bundle that about two hundred people in this district are holding a line about eleven of their own workers, and one of those lines has got your name on it and a shoulder on it, and there are about two hundred people who have read it. **A company cannot leave an unverified note in a file with two hundred people holding a copy of it. That is not a company being brave. That is a company being frightened, correctly, and doing the only thing available to it.**"

Denny Molloy did not sit down.

"And here's the cost, and I'm not going to dress it up. To get a note changed you have to confirm it, and if you confirm it then your name goes against a note that says a shoulder, and that note is in a company for good. **And you are thirty-nine and you are behind on your rent and that note is the only thing in the world that is going to give you your shift back, so you cannot refuse it, and if you refuse it you keep the shoulder out of the file forever and you do not get the shift.**"

"Then it's a good job for me," said Denny Molloy.

"Yes. That's exactly what it is."

The shop was very quiet for three seconds. Somewhere out in the street a shutter came down.

"**Say that again,**" said Marek Senn.

"**A company has written to a man and asked him a question, and the honest answer takes four seconds, and every honest answer I have ever given in nineteen years has cost me about a month.** That's the whole of it. I've been in a canteen, I've been in a car park and I've been in a doorway, and the answer is always the same, and it always costs a month, and the company has finally written it down and asked me for it, and I have come here to have it read out by somebody before I sign it."

Marek Senn put both hands on the bench.

"**I am going to say a thing to you and I am going to say it badly, and you are entitled to tell me it is not any use, and it will not be.** A company is not a wall. A company is four men on a rota and a woman in an office and about nine hundred other people who have never read a page of anything, and a piece of paper with your name on it is a decision about whether a company can send an invoice."

Denny Molloy looked at him.

"That is the third time somebody has said something like that to me this month," he said, "and it is the third time it has been said by somebody who works for them."

"Yes," said Marek Senn.

"**It is a true sentence. I want to say that first, before anything else, because you have gone the colour of a wall and you have not said a false one. It is true and it is no use to me at all, and I have had three weeks of true sentences coming at me from every direction and not one of them has changed anything, and I would like somebody to work that out for me.**"

"Sit down," said Marek Senn.

Denny Molloy sat down.

"Right. Here is the rest of it and it is four sentences and you can do with them what you like." He turned the sheet round so it was the right way up for both of them. "**One. There are about two hundred copies of that line and eleven of the people who have it put it down somewhere and cannot remember where, and there is nobody who is going to come and take it off a counter, so the piece of paper is not going away, ever, and you are not going to be able to make that better.**"

"That is not four sentences of good news."

"**Two. A note you have confirmed is a true note and it is in there for good. A note you have not confirmed is a note somebody has to come and argue with, and a note somebody has to argue with is a thing that can go wrong in your favour, and I have watched about nine men find that out in twenty years and I have never once watched a man find it out by saying no.**"

"That is the part I have never heard anybody say."

"**Three. The arguing has to be done by a person, in the first person, and it cannot be done by the company, because a company arguing with itself about a page is a page, and it cannot be done by me, because I work for them and my handwriting on the back of that sheet is in a company in about nine days whether I sign it or not.**"

"And four."

"**Four is that you have got a sheet of paper with a shoulder on it and a tick box on it and you have come to a repair shop on a Monday morning to have it read, and there is nobody in this city you could have gone to, and that is the reason you are sitting in that chair, and the reason is not that nobody will read it. It is that there is nobody whose job it is.**"

Denny Molloy sat with that for four seconds.

"**Then write it for me,**" he said. "That's all. Four sentences, on the back, in your hand, and I sign it and I send it and I've got my shoulder in a file either way and at least the file has got the truth in it."

"No," said Marek Senn.

"It'd take you a minute—"

"**It would take me a minute and it would be the first thing in a term I have done that I could not take back, and you would be signing a piece of paper written by a man in a lanyard who works for the company that wrote the paragraph, and in about four years there will be a person in a room somewhere who reads that and says: well, the technician said so.**" He said it quickly and then he said the rest of it more slowly, because the rest of it was the true reason and he had decided in the space of a second that he was going to say the true reason out loud. "**And I have spent fourteen weeks learning that a document is only ever a receipt, and a receipt is a thing you show to a company that has stopped answering its telephone, and the second your shoulder is written down by me then there is a document about your shoulder with my hand on it, and the document is the only part that lasts.**"

Denny Molloy took the plastic wallet off the bench, and turned it over, and took a pencil out of his coat pocket — a carpenter's pencil, flat, with the lead worn to a chisel — and put it on the counter and wrote on the back of the company's sheet, in pencil, in a slow block hand that took him about a minute and a half:

*Nineteen years on the same rig. Nobody has ever asked me about my shoulder. I found out I had one from a piece of paper I did not write. I am not asking anybody for anything.*

He did not sign it. He did not put a date on it. He folded the sheet once, along the line the company had printed for its own fold, and put it back in the wallet.

"**You're going to ask me why there's no date,**" he said.

"Yes."

"Because I've got a mate who's been at the same firm nineteen years and he puts a date on everything and he got done out of about six weeks of work over one, and I thought about that in a canteen on Friday and I decided I was not going to be that." He put the wallet in his pocket. "**Keep it or bin it. I have not got anywhere to put it. My wife's got a drawer and if I put a thing like that in a drawer she will find it in about four years when we're clearing out and she will say, what's this, and then I'll have to explain it, and I'm not doing that to her either.**"

He was at the door in about a second and a half after that.

"Denny."

"I'm not stopping."

"**The true sentence works. It is not any use to you today and it is going to be the only sentence in the file in four years, and I have not got a better one and neither has anybody else in this city.**"

Denny Molloy put his head round the door.

"**You keep it in a drawer,**" he said, "**and in about four years you'll be the fourth thing in a room, and I'll be dead, and my shoulder'll be true.**"

The door went.

Marek Senn stood behind a bench in a shop in Lattice Ward holding a piece of company paper in a plastic wallet with a pencil line on the back of it and no name and no date, and it was the fourth thing he had been given in three weeks that he could not file, hand over, or refuse, and he had a drawer under the bench with three documents in it and none of them could be produced to anybody, and this one was not even a document, it was a note on the back of a document, written by a man who had been owed an answer for nineteen years and had given it in pencil to a stranger on a Monday morning.

The site was off the corridor and the gate did not ask him anything, because the booking had already done it, and it was a printed page in a folder in the cabin of a van with three columns and his company in the middle one, and he had been through that gate four times by now and it had never once cost anybody an afternoon.

Micky Faul was on the hard standing with two of his nine and the barrier was up and the packer lamp on the far lane was flickering in a way that a ballast does about eleven days before it stops.

"That your fault then," he said, coming over. "You booked it. Don't tell me you didn't."

"**It is my fault and it has been my fault since the Thursday of week twenty-two, and I want to explain why, and it is a bad reason and I am going to give it to you anyway.** I did not have a fault to book. I had a tray with a printout in it and a line in a grid with a date on it that I had put in myself nine weeks ago, and a tray that is not mine and is not the company's and goes at the end of the year, and a man on a wagon who asked where the gear came from two years ago. **I booked a lamp.**"

Micky Faul looked at the lamp.

"Right," he said.

"I want that in the room. **A company technician goes to a site because there is a fault. If a man comes back to a site with no fault, then somebody in a depot gets told there was a fault at that address on that date, and that is a piece of paper with my name near it. So I made a fault.** And it is a real fault, and the ballast has been going for about a fortnight now, and I am not sorry about the fault, and I would like you to know exactly what I did and not to find out about it in a year."

"You'll get sacked for that."

"Probably not. It is not against anything. It is the most careful thing I have ever done and it is the only thing I have done this term that I could actually be called for, and I have told nobody, and I am telling you because you have kept that tray for me for nine weeks without asking me a single question about it, and if I do not say it out loud to somebody I am going to be doing it twice a year for the rest of my working life."

Micky Faul took the ladder off the wall and handed it to one of his nine.

"**You lot are quick this year,**" he said. "That's all. You've not asked me a thing I couldn't answer and you've not written down a thing I didn't say, which is more than the last three of your lot managed between them, and one of them wrote down a fault that was a hinge." He looked up at the lamp. "**And the ballast's cheap, and it came in on the same wagon as the gate, and if that lamp kills somebody in about four months I will have to explain it to a woman from a leasing company, so change it and change it properly and don't tell me you booked it.**"

He changed it in about nineteen minutes and it was the first job in a term that went right the first time and took him no longer than it should have.

Then he went into the prefab office with the heater on and the kettle and the filing tray, because he had four minutes, and the tray was still there, and the printout was still on top of it because the tray was still in the way, and page nine had a blank field on it in a typeface that a system uses, and *Meridian consolidation point, lot onward* typed two inches underneath in a stores clerk's hand, and a box number written in biro in a different hand by a different person two years ago because a man on a wagon had asked where the thing came from.

He read it four times. It was the fourth time. He put the pencil back in his pocket and it was the fourth time.

*94. Monday of week 25, about ten at night, and it is the ninth page of a printout and I have read it four times now and I am not going to write it down on the fifth, and there is a man in this entry who did the same thing to me this morning with a carpenter's pencil.*

*Dai Pritchard rang the out-of-hours line at ten past eight and told me a decision he made on Sunday night, and it was the decision he said he was going to make on the telephone at about two o'clock on the Tuesday of week twenty-four, and he got it in the right order, which is a thing I did not expect from anybody about the telephone. **He is answering it. That is the whole of it. Not a grievance and not a resignation and not one bad word about anybody, and he said the words not a grievance and not a resignation himself, which means he had thought about them, which means he thought somebody was going to accuse him of both and decided to arrive before they did.** Nineteen years, no card, never asked for one, and the reason he stopped answering on the Wednesday of week twenty-one is a line in a canteen with his name on it and a box somebody else typed. **And here is what I have not written down for a fortnight, which is that the second half of the decision is that he wants the card, and he told me that on a Monday morning at ten past eight in about eleven seconds flat, and he wants it from a company and not from me, and he said he would be ashamed of asking me to get it for him, and I believe him, and he is the only person in four weeks who has believed me about anything.***

*And the sentence he used to get there, which is mine, and which I gave him on a Tuesday and have now heard come back out of a man's mouth with a document in it.* **A company is not a wall. A company is four men on a rota and a woman in an office and about nine hundred people who have never read a page of anything.** I said that to him believing I was handing a man a way of not being afraid of a piece of paper, and what he did with it in ten days was work out that the way out of a wall is to be issued a card by it, and he is going to be issued a card by it, and the card is a portable dependency, and about four hundred people in this district are going to hold one by the spring for the same reason he is. **I gave a true sentence to a frightened man and he has made a document out of it and the document is correct and I have spent fourteen weeks arguing that the whole of the trouble is a document, and I cannot argue with him because every argument I have got needs one, and the one thing I am not going to do is be the man who hands him one.** That is the third time this term a true sentence has gone out of my mouth and come back as a piece of paper, and I have no mechanism for stopping it and I have not looked for one, and I have decided that not looking is allowed. It is not a good decision. I have had four of those this month.

*And Nia Bramhall told me about a course exception in a depot office at about ten to ten, and she was right to, and I want it on the page that she was right to and I have not thanked her, because the whole of what I did about it was write a paragraph in a book.* **Four fields on company paper in my own handwriting, and the fourth field is reason, and it says I have not got a reason I am willing to give, and she has read it about four times this week and has worked out from a diary that a man who will not say why is a man who has not got a reason.** And then she did the thing that training clerks do and that nobody in a case ever notices, which is she told me the price of it out loud before she told me the rule: **I am grade two, grade two does not go out on a release callout, grade two does not get the release rate, and if I am grade two for the whole of next term then the only thing that has happened to me this term is that I have been given a lanyard.** About two and a half times the shop rate, four days a week, about nine hundred pounds over a term. I have known that figure since the Monday of week nineteen and I have never once written it in this book and I have written it tonight in a sentence about somebody else's shoulder, which is exactly the wrong place for it and it is the only place I have got for it.

*Denny Molloy, thirty-nine, came into the shop at about eleven with a sheet of company paper in a plastic wallet, standing up, and would not sit down because if he sat down it would be a meeting.* **A paragraph asking him to confirm the accuracy of a note they hold about him, on company paper, with a form reference and a date and a tick box, and it is good, and I said so to his face in the first ninety seconds and I meant it: a company cannot leave an unverified note in a file with about two hundred people holding a copy of it, and the eleven lines in that bundle are lines about eleven of their own workers, and the reason they have written to him is that they are frightened, correctly, and this is the only thing available to a frightened company.** And the cost, and I gave it to him straight, because he is a man who has been in a canteen and a car park and a doorway being told the truth by everybody and has had about a month off for it every time: **to correct a note you have to confirm it, and confirming it puts his name against a shoulder for good, and he is behind on his rent, and the note is the only thing that is going to give him his shift back, so he cannot refuse it, and if he refuses it he keeps the shoulder out of a file forever and does not get the shift.** He said: then it's a good job for me. That is the whole of what nineteen years of not being asked has produced, in one sentence, on a Monday morning, standing up.

*And I gave him four sentences and the fourth one is the one that has been in this book for a term and I have never once heard it come out of my own mouth in a room.* **There is nobody in this city whose job it is.** I have said that nine times to nine people out loud, on company time, in markets, in a stairwell, and I have never said it to the man it was about, and I said it to him at eleven o'clock on a Monday morning standing behind a bench while he held a piece of paper in a plastic wallet, and he wrote the four sentences down in pencil on the back of it in about a minute and a half, **and he did not sign it and he did not date it**, and the reason he gave for the date is that he has got a mate at the same firm who dates everything and got done out of about six weeks over one, and the reason he gave for the signature is that his wife has a drawer.

*And that is eight, and I am not counting it, and here is why I am not counting it, and the reason is not the one I have used for the other seven.* The other seven made a thing to keep a thing out of a company. **Denny Molloy did not make a thing. He was handed a piece of paper by a company and he wrote an answer on the back of it and gave it to a stranger and told him to keep it or bin it, and the stranger cannot bin it because binning it would be destroying a man's answer to a question about his own body, and cannot keep it because there is nowhere to keep it that is not a drawer in a shop, and a drawer in a shop is where I keep a woman's five lines with no date on them, and if I put his in the same drawer then in about four years there are two pieces of paper in my hand that I chose to hold, and I did not choose either of them, and I have told one woman I would not be a person she came to for things and I have now been the reason a second person is carrying something and neither of them knows about the other and I am not going to put them in a room.**

*And the site, and the ninth page, and the fourth time.* Micky Faul said you lot are quick this year, and he took the ladder off the wall himself, and he said the ballast was cheap and had come in on the same wagon as the gate, and he said: change it and change it properly and don't tell me you booked it. **A foreman of about forty-four has now told two men in a term not to tell him things and has meant it kindly both times, and I have never once worked out whether that is a system working or two men in a yard who are tired.** The tray is still in the way, the printout is still on top of it, and the field is still blank, and a man on a wagon asked where the gear came from about two years ago and a clerk typed a lot reference and did not know that is not an address. **I have read it four times. The tray goes at the end of the year and I have told nobody I am going back and the lamp is now a real fault and I have changed it properly, and the reason I went back today is a piece of paper and the reason the piece of paper is a problem is a man on a wagon, and a fortnight after that I am going to go back a fifth time for a gate and there is going to be nothing at all wrong with it.**
