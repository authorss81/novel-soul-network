# Chapter 96 — Four Hundred

The hall was on the second floor of a building on a trading estate and it had been a shop of some kind before, and there were about nine hundred chairs in it in rows of ten with a gap down the middle and two aisles, and the carpet had been lifted at the front in a rectangle about the size of a table.

Marek Senn got there at about ten past ten on the Monday morning of the last week of the term because he is on the rota Monday to Thursday and because he wanted to see it before anybody was in it.

A banner went across the front wall, about four metres long, with a company's name on it in the same typeface as the lanyard round his neck and a strapline under it that he read nine times and could not finish: *Standards that work.* There was nothing on the walls, no poster, no photograph of a person, no manual on a table, no laminated card of any kind on any surface in the room, and he noticed that four times in about twenty minutes and did not work out why it had bothered him until the Wednesday.

At the door there was a table with a printout on it in a plastic wallet, and the printout was a list of four hundred and six names in the order they had been entered, with a tick column and a column that said *card issued*, and there was a woman of about twenty-six behind it with two pens and a box of cards that was still in its shrink wrap.

"You're early."

"I'm on the clock," said Marek Senn.

"**Everyone's on the clock,**" she said. "**That's the thing about it. It's on the clock for four hundred and six people and that's why the room's full.**"

He looked at the printout.

"Can I ask you a question about the room and not about the case."

"You can ask me a question about anything, I'm on a desk."

"Why is it happening."

She did not look up, and then she did.

"**Because cancelling it is nine thousand two hundred pound and running it is two,**" she said. "**There's a hall at two thousand a day and this hall is booked either way. There's nine hundred of lunch tokens ordered, and if you cancel the lunch you don't get the lunch money back, and if you don't cancel the lunch then four hundred people turn up and there is nothing for them, and there is a woman upstairs who has been asked to cancel it three times and has not done it, and I know that because I have been asked twice and I did not do it either, and I have a desk.**"

"Somebody could cancel it."

"**Somebody could, yes,**" said the woman at the desk. "**That's the part nobody will say out loud. It's not that it can't be cancelled. It's that everybody who could cancel it is one person who is not in a room with anybody, and cancelling it would be that person deciding four hundred people were wrong, and there is no meeting where that decision gets made, so the decision does not get made, and the room happens.**"

She put a card on the table and turned it round.

"That's yours. You're the second one on the list. Third row, aisle."

Marek Senn looked at the card. It had a photograph and a name and a number and a word at the bottom that he had read about nine hundred times this term and had never got used to.

"**I have not got a release card,**" he said. "**I am on the programme but I have not got a card, and the fifth clause of my own contract says I take the company's own course in my first month and I have not, and the reason I have not is four fields in a course exception in a system with my own handwriting in the last one, and I am telling you that because you have put a card in my hand and I would like the first thing that happens to it to be me telling you, and not you finding out at two o'clock.**"

The woman at the desk looked at him for about two seconds.

"Sorry," she said. "**That's not my desk. Cards are not my desk.**"

"Then whose desk is it."

"**Somebody in an office and I don't know who and I am not going to find out, because when I did that in the spring about a room I was told I was not to bother and I have decided that is a good rule.**" She pushed the card back across the table. "**Take it or don't. It is not mine and I am not going to be the one who has your name on a list.**"

He did not take it, and she did not insist, and it went in the box with the other four hundred and five in the afternoon and he has thought about that more than he has thought about the programme.

---

He walked the room about four times between half past ten and half past one, which is what he does, and which he has done in about nine hundred rooms in about nine years, and which nobody in that room was doing.

The exits are four. Two at the front, one at each end, and one at the back of the room on the left, and the one at the back of the room on the left has a fire door with a bar on it and about nine leaflets wedged in behind the glass, and the fire door is the one you would go out of.

He counted them four times and on the third pass, at about twenty past one, a man of about fifty-one was standing about four metres from the one at the back looking at it, and Marek Senn walked past him and the man said, without turning round, "It's a one-way if the front's blocked."

"I know."

"**You counted.**"

"I count them," said Marek Senn.

"**Everybody counts them,**" said the man, "**and nobody says, and if you say it in this room you are a safety officer, and if you say it in a canteen you are a worrier.**" He was about fifty-one and he had a card in his wallet, and Marek Senn knew who he was and knew where the wallet was, and had known both since the Wednesday of week twenty-eight. "**There is a fire marshal coming at about three. He will do it. You will not be the one who did it.**"

Nobody else said anything to him about the exits for the rest of the day, and he counted twice more anyway, and on the fifth pass he found that somebody had put a piece of masking tape on the frame of the back door at about eye height, which is not a regulation and is not a marking, and which told him that somebody else in that room had counted and had not wanted to say so.

---

The programme was a single side of A4, printed, with nine items and times, and there was a copy on every chair, which he did not know was normal and which he thought about for a long time afterwards.

*0930 Registration. 1000 Module three, parts one and two. 1130 Break. 1200 Module three, part three. 1330 Card issue. 1400 Card issue. 1500 Maintenance sequence without a supervisory cue. 1530 Close.*

**There is no four minutes on the printout.** There is a slot from three o'clock to half past and the slot has got the item in it and nothing else, and the four minutes are in a running order on a clipboard in a woman's hand, and the clipboard has got about nine items on it and eight of them have got times against them, and the one that has not is his.

He read the item about nine times. There is no name on it. There is no name on any of the nine. There is a man's name in the small print at the bottom, which is the operations director's, and there is a name against every row of the printout at the door, and the one on the third row aisle is his, and it is his because Nia Bramhall put it there in the last week before the summer holiday in a diary with nine hundred bookings in it, and she did not put it there as a decision about him.

At about ten past two a woman with a lanyard of a different colour came round the back of row nine and stood beside him and he knew before she spoke that she had the programme in her hand, because everybody who has the programme in their hand on the Monday is a person who has been told about the Tuesday.

"**You're the four o'clock,**" she said.

"Yes."

"Right." She was about forty and she was not unkind and she was doing four things at once. "**I've got nine hundred and eleven of these to hand out and about six hundred are done and you have not read the small print.**"

"What does the small print say."

"It says the session is a demonstration of a company standard and that attendance does not constitute a certification of competence and that the sequence shown is illustrative." She looked at him properly for the first time. "**It also says that the technician is a company employee and that nothing shown constitutes an endorsement of any practice not issued by the company, and that line is in every one of these and I have read it about four times today because I did not write it and I did not know it was on there.**"

"That is a good line."

"**It is a bulletproof line,**" she said, "**and it is there because somebody in legal read a complaint about a Saturday course in the spring, and it has been on the programme ever since, and it means that on Tuesday at three o'clock you can stand up there and do absolutely anything at all and every person in this room has been told in writing that it means nothing.**"

"Yes."

"**So do not bother arguing with me about the item,**" she said. "**I've got six hundred and eleven to hand out and I have a woman upstairs who has not told me whether the four o'clock is happening.**"

"It is happening."

"**Then you are the only person in this building who has said so today, including me,**" she said, and went away, and did come back, and put a rolled poster in a tube against the wall by the back door, which is the poster, and the tube is where she keeps them between sessions, and Marek Senn stood there for about a minute after she had gone looking at a cardboard tube.

They came in from about twenty past two.

That is the part nobody in a company plans for and it is the part that made a hall of nine hundred chairs into a room with four hundred and six people in it, and it took about an hour and ten minutes, and it was the loudest thing Marek Senn had stood near in a term.

They came in threes and fours and in ones. They were not the case. **Not one person in that hall on the Monday afternoon was there because of anything that had happened in a room on the second floor of the Ninth Street block, or on a hard standing off Fell Road, or in a canteen off the Meridian, or in a market in the Saltmarket, and Marek Senn stood at the back of the left-hand aisle and worked that out in about nine minutes by asking about eleven of them the way you ask, which is by asking what they are here for.** A man of about twenty-seven was there because his company had sent him and it was on the invoice. Two women of about forty were there because the module was on their course and the course was on their day off. A man of about fifty-five was there for the lunch. A young man of about nineteen was there because his employer's card rate is different on a Tuesday and he had worked that out before anybody else in the history of this case. A woman of about thirty-four was there with a hip and had told nobody at work why and had come on her own time and had a piece of paper in her hand with about four lines on it that was not a form.

Two people counted the exits. One of them was a man of about fifty-one at the back, and one of them was a boy of twenty-one in the fourth row, who was on a Saturday crew at twelve pounds, who had a card in his wallet and a grey transit sequence card in a rucksack with two names and no date on it, and who did not look at Marek Senn once in four hours and told him afterwards that he had not looked on purpose.

**He looked for the man who had spoken to him about the doors at about twenty past one, and he was at the back with his back to the wall and he had not moved, and Marek Senn stood about four metres off him for about nine seconds and then went and stood somewhere else. The man had asked a question in a room in the Saltmarket three weeks ago and had been told nothing, and being told nothing by Marek Senn in a corridor is the same thing, and he was not going to do it twice in a fortnight.**

And in row nine, on the aisle, on the left, thirty metres away, with a pencil in his hand and a company form on his knee, was a man of thirty-nine with a shoulder.

Marek Senn saw him at about twenty past three and Denny Molloy did not see him or did not turn round, and neither of them moved, and the whole of the distance between them was thirty metres of a room with four hundred and six people in it, and Marek Senn stood there and understood the sentence a woman of sixty-eight had given him on a Sunday in week twenty-two, which was that there is no system in this city, and the second half of it, which is that the only route between any two people is that one of them gets lucky and the other one is not proud, and **this week the first one got lucky and it did not help at all.**

At about three o'clock a woman on a microphone said that the session would resume at three and asked people to be in their pairs, and about four hundred people got up and moved and made a noise like weather, and Marek Senn stayed where he was, and a man in the ninth row, three rows behind the one with the pencil, turned round and looked at him for about a second and a half and then turned back.

At four minutes past three he went up the side aisle and out through the back of the room and down a fire stair and across a landing and out into the car park, and the door shut behind him, and he stood on a slab of concrete in a car park on a trading estate for about nine minutes.

Then he did not do a job. He sat in the van with the engine off and the door open for about nine minutes and then about four more, and then he drove to the shop in Lattice and the shutter was up and the lamp was on and there were about eleven hours of the last week of the term left and one of them was going to have his job in it, and he wrote four things down that he had not known that morning and decided nothing at all about any of them.
