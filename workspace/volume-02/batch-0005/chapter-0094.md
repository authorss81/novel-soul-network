# Chapter 94 — The Poster in a Corridor

The three weeks after a man telephones a repair shop and says he is going to answer a telephone were the quietest three weeks Marek Senn had had in a term, and he had had a term with nine flood rounds in it.

He did twenty-eight jobs in fifteen days and got twenty-six of them right first time. A latch solenoid in Nine Bridges. Two roller shutters. A lamp ballast in a stairwell in Crown Terrace that he wrote up as *ballast at end of life* in about eleven words because that is what it was. A packer gate in a yard in the Lower Wards that came back four times in one morning for four supposed faults, all four of them the same fault, a prox switch with a cracked ferrite clip behind the gland, and on the fifth visit a foreman of thirty-six said *you lot are quick this year* in a voice Marek Senn had now heard three times in six weeks from three different men, and he wrote it on the docket and did not think about it for the rest of the day, which was wrong of him and he knew it at the time.

Nobody asked him anything. He noticed that on the Wednesday of week twenty-six and wrote four words about it in the corner of a docket, and this time he wrote the fifth word, and the fifth word was *and*, and then nothing came after it again.

On the Friday of week twenty-seven a young man in a depot corridor in a fleece with a company badge on it that was not in range stopped him and said, "Are you the technician on the kit."

Marek Senn did not understand the question and said so.

"It's on the poster," said the young man. "**There's a poster. In the canteen and on the board by the parts cage and nine more in the offices. It's module three, open session, the Tuesday of the last week of the term, the hall on the trading estate, four hundred places. And it says on the bottom line: maintenance sequence without a supervisory cue, the technician on the kit.** And I have got two of the brown envelopes left and nobody has told me if I should still be handing them out, and the hall is on Tuesday and I have got nine boxes in the back of a van."

"Who put the poster up."

"Somebody in communications. It came down on a pallet." The young man shifted the box. "It's got your job on it. Not your name. Your job."

"**I know what my job is,**" said Marek Senn.

"Sorry."

"No. Say it again, slower. What is on the bottom line of the poster."

"Maintenance sequence without a supervisory cue. The technician on the kit." The young man read it off the envelope in his hand, which had the poster printed on it, in a box near the bottom. "That's it. That's the last line. There's no name on it anywhere. I looked, because I was going to ring somebody and I couldn't work out who, and there isn't anybody, because it's a job and not a person."

He went back to the van.

Marek Senn stood in a corridor in a depot for four seconds.

---

He did four things about it over the next two days and three of them were the wrong thing and the fourth was the right thing and the fourth was the worst thing he did in a term.

**The first was a telephone.** He rang the training diary at about ten to nine on the Monday morning and Nia Bramhall told him, without being asked and without a pause, that the item was a company item, that the copy sat with the communications people, and that the session was on the Tuesday of the last week of the term, which is twenty-one days away, and that a change to a programme item goes on a form.

"Which form."

"The one that says what the item was and what it is now and why." She did not soften it. "**And Marek, the why is a box with about four lines in it and about nine hundred people have put a reason in that box in three years and I have read four hundred of them and not one of them has ever been a person saying I did not agree to be in this.**"

"I did not agree to be in it."

"I know you didn't. **Write it in the box.**"

"What would I write in the box."

"That is not my decision and it is not yours either, and I have a diary with nine hundred bookings in it and four hundred and six of them are in that hall on the Tuesday, and I have been asked to cancel it three times this week by three people who are not me, and I have not done it, and I have not done it because I do not know that I am allowed to and I would like somebody to tell me whether I am.**"

**The second was the form.** He filled it in on the Monday night in the shop, on company paper, which has happened to him four times this term. Name, department, date, item. New item. And then the box with about four lines in it, and he wrote *I did not agree to this and I have not been asked*, which is true, and then he sat looking at it for a minute and a half, and put the pen down, and picked it up and wrote the rest of it: *and I am not able to say who put me in it, and I do not think anybody knows, and if the item is removed there will be no session and if the session is removed four hundred people will not be told anything, and I do not know which of those is worse and I have not got anybody to ask.*

He put it in the tray on the Monday night. On the Wednesday morning it came back with a line on it in somebody's hand: *no action — item is a company item*.

**The third was a conversation with a man who is unremarkable and correct.** Wyn Dunleavy is fifty-two and is the district service manager and is Petar Vance's line manager and has never in his life had an opinion that was not correct, and he came out to the parts cage on the Wednesday morning at about eleven without being asked, which told Marek Senn that the form had gone further than a tray.

"Sit down," said Wyn Dunleavy.

"I am working."

"Sit down anyway, because I am going to say four sentences and I have not got the whole morning." He stood rather than sat. "**The item is on the programme because the programme is a company document, and a company document has a slot for the thing it is about, and a slot is cheaper than a name, and I am going to give you the reason and then you are going to be angry with me and I am going to let you.**"

"Go on."

"**A name on a programme is a person who can be asked whether they agreed to it. A job on a programme is not a person and cannot be asked anything, and I have been in this trade thirty years and I have never seen a programme with a name on it that the person had agreed to, and I have seen hundreds with a job on it that they had not.**"

"You are saying you would rather it said *technician* than my name."

"I am saying I would rather it said technician than your name, **and I am saying the reason, which is that a programme with your name on it is a programme you can be removed from, and a programme with a job on it is a programme that happens.** And the session happens either way, Marek, and the four hundred and six are in the hall either way, and the difference is that on Tuesday there is a man on that stage or there is a gap with a line under it."

Marek Senn did not say anything.

"**And that is the whole of what I have come out here to say, and I am not going to pretend I do not know what it is like to be the fourth thing in a room, because I have been the fourth thing in about four hundred rooms in thirty years and I have never once been asked whether I wanted to be.**"

"Who is the item for."

"It is for the kit. That is what the sequence is about." Wyn Dunleavy put his hands in his pockets. "The line is a maintenance sequence without a supervisory cue. The kit is the thing that has a supervisory cue in it. **The two sentences are about nine inches apart on a printed page and about eleven years apart in the world, and the poster has put them next to each other because a person in a building wrote *without a supervisory cue* and thought it was a selling point.**"

"And nobody has checked it."

"Somebody has checked it," said Wyn Dunleavy. "I have. I checked it on Monday and I have not put my name on anything and I have not told you that I checked it, and I would like to be able to tell you that I checked it, and I am telling you that I checked it, and if you use that I will deny it and it will be true, because I checked it and I cannot do anything about it, and that is the whole of what a manager is in this city."

He walked off up the corridor and stopped about nine metres away and came back.

"**Four minutes,**" he said. "**The programme gives your item four minutes. I have read it. I have read the programme and I have read the time slot and I have not done anything about either, and I want you to know that I know what four minutes is.**"

---

The fourth thing he did was nothing at all, and it was on the Thursday afternoon, and it was the worst thing he did in a term, and it was the right thing to do.

He let his name stay off the programme.

He sat on a stool with the shutter half up and did the arithmetic, and the arithmetic was this: if the item came off the programme then the sequence came off the programme, and if the sequence came off the programme then a company that had spent three weeks and about nine thousand pounds on a hall and a hall full of people was going to have a hole where the reason was, and the hole would get filled, because a hall of four hundred and six people with a gap in the programme at four o'clock is a hole that a district safety and training supervisor fills in about eleven seconds, and what goes in the hole is the eleventh-parts sentence, and it goes in with a lanyard on.

And he wanted that not to happen, more than he had wanted anything in a term.

That was the discovery, and it arrived at four o'clock on the Thursday afternoon in a shop in Lattice Ward, and it was this: **he had stopped wanting a room in which to say a true sentence and started wanting an audience to be given one, and there is a difference, and he could not have told anybody the difference before the Wednesday, and the difference is that a room is a thing he is in and an audience is a thing he is doing to.** He had spent fourteen weeks refusing to be the man in a lanyard in a room and he had spent four of them building a rig on a hard standing with a hole in it the shape of himself, and he had told a foreman of fifty-eight there is no such thing as a non-hinged maintenance sequence, and he had written it on a card and torn it in half, and then he had let a poster go up with his slot on it, and he was going to stand in front of four hundred and six people on the Tuesday of the last week of the term and say the thing he had said to one man on a hard standing, and he wanted that, and he could not make it not want that, and the reason he could not was that the thing he wants has changed shape while he was busy refusing it.

He sat there until about half past four.

Then he rang a number in Crown Terrace and a woman said he was in a meeting and would ring back, and he said it was not urgent and it was not about a job, and she said she would tell him that, and he put the phone down.

She rang back at ten past five.

---

"**You said it was not about a job,**" said Oren Vey.

"It isn't."

"Then it is about nine seconds and I am going to spend them and then I am going back to a meeting about a lease." He was not being kind and he was not being short. "Go."

"The Wednesday of week twenty-four. A back room in the Saltmarket, ten past one, about eleven people, and a man of fifty-two in a district jacket said there is no such thing as a district, there is a technique and it is being handed round and there is nobody putting their name to it. Three rooms in a fortnight, a college in the summer, a district on the Saturday, a company on the Saturday, a man on his crew and a woman on his crew going on one in a fortnight. **About eleven people went two seconds quieter and nobody said a word and I was in that room and I could not say one word of the answer.**"

"That is the whole report?"

"That is the report. **I am not asking you for a favour and I am not giving you one, and I am not going to say this to a room, and you have spent a term telling me why and you have never once said it out loud to me and I have worked out that you do not have to.**"

"I did say it to you," said Oren Vey. "On the Friday of week twenty-one. In a shop. You did not answer it."

"I did not answer it because you were right and I was in a lanyard."

"I was right and you were in a lanyard and those are two different sentences, and I have been waiting about six weeks to find out whether you are a man who cannot do anything without a lanyard or a man who cannot do anything with one." A chair, and a sound of a room. "**Go on then. I have been owed this since the twenty-first and you have rung me on a Thursday afternoon and told me a man in a district jacket is wrong, and now tell me why he is wrong, and do not be careful, because I have four hundred and six people booked into a hall and I cannot un-book them and I have been not thinking about that for about four days.**"

Marek Senn put his hand flat on the bench.

"**Because there is no group,**" he said. "**There is no such thing as a name for it, and the reason there is no name for it is that it is not a thing anybody did. A woman of thirty-one made a card at her own kitchen table in about four nights out of nine years of doing the work and nobody sent it to her. A man of thirty-four on a kitchen line in Nine Bridges was asked in the Wednesday of week twenty-two what the word was for and he said it was for the pallet that is not ready, and he was not asked where he learned it and he will never be asked and he will say it correctly to a stranger for the rest of his life. A woman of sixty-eight has got a list of what a plate took off her on a piece of A4 in her front room and she has told one person and she has asked not to be told about it again. **Those four people have never met and they are not members of anything and they have not passed it to each other and if you put them in a room tonight they would not recognise each other and two of them would be frightened and one of them would be annoyed.**"

"That is four people."

"That is four people and I have met six and I have not counted them in a book twice because a pattern gets a reference number and a reference number tells the people who wrote in it that they wrote in it."

"So what is the belief," said Oren Vey.

"**The belief is a category. Somebody wants a category because a category can be written on.**"

Oren Veny was quiet for about two seconds.

"**Then I am going to tell you the thing I told you in week twenty-one and you did not answer, and I am going to tell you it on a Thursday afternoon in about nine seconds and then I am going to go back to a meeting.**" His voice did not change at all. "**In about three weeks there is going to be a second person who says it out loud, in a different room, to a different audience, and he is going to say it because he has been asked to say it, and he is going to be paid, and it is not going to be me, and I will know who it is and I am not going to tell you. That is the whole of it. And the reason I am telling you now, unasked, on a telephone, is that I have been wrong about a thing for about two years and I have just worked out that I was wrong, and the thing I was wrong about is that I thought the belief would go away when the people who are harmed by it were told the truth.**"

"Why are you telling me."

"Because you are the only man in this city who has just said the true version out loud to me without asking me for anything, and because I have a meeting about a lease, and because **I am going to be able to use what you have just said and you are never going to know whether I used it or not, and I have decided that is the correct way for this to go and I am not going to pretend it is decent.**"

"Do not."

"I am not going to." A pause. "**And Marek. One more thing and it is not a favour and it is not a gift, it is a fact you are entitled to and I am giving it to you on a Thursday because I do not want you hearing it from a woman with a diary.**"

"Go on."

"**There are four hundred and six names on that list.**"

The extractor was on in the shop and Marek Senn could hear it and he could hear nothing else for about four seconds.

"**Four hundred and six,**" said Oren Vey. "Modules one, two and three, on the clock, and about nine hundred of those four hundred and six have never been asked a question about a practice by a human being and will not be asked one tomorrow either. It is in a diary in a depot and she did not put it there and she books it, and I am the operations director and I can see every number in the building, and I am telling you the number because you are about to walk into a room with it in it and I would rather you were not surprised in front of four hundred people. **And now you know it, and I cannot un-tell you, and I have thought about that for about nine seconds and I have decided that a man who is going to be ambushed is not a safer man. Good afternoon.**"

Click.

Marek Senn sat in a shop in Lattice Ward for about a minute and a half with his hand on the bench and a number in his head, and then he got up and counted the exits, which he does, and there are two, and he stood in the second one for a second and a half and then went and did a job on a latch solenoid in Nine Bridges because the job was on the sheet and the sheet was the reason he had a wage.

There was a truck at the gate off the corridor on the Wednesday that was not a company's and had not been on the Wednesday before, and a foreman said two men had come through about ten past four and asked for the office and one of them had a boy with him, and Marek Senn wrote *ask* on a docket and then wrote the word out, because a docket with the word *ask* on it is a promise, and he did not know what the question was and had not been told.

*97. *Oren Vey, forty-one, operations director, and the only man in this city who has been owed a true sentence by me for seven weeks.* **I rang him.
