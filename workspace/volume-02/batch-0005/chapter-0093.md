# Chapter 93 — Forty-Two Lines

The poster came into the common room on the Ninth Street block in a brown envelope with about nine of them in it and Toby Ansell put it on the table face up because he had been told to and not because he wanted to, and it was a company's poster and it was about a session and it was the only thing in the room that anybody looked at.

Asha Reed said her sentence standing up, at the front, with nine first years in it and one voice in it, and she said it the way she says it.

"**The case is Senn's and the technique is nobody's yet.** Five times. I am not going to vary it, because the day I vary it is the day somebody in this room can tell which half of it I think is true."

Nobody said anything, because it was the fifth time, and the fifth time is a thing a room has heard.

"**And the other thing, which is the last thing I am going to say about it in this term.** In four weeks five separate people have asked me whether they think they should be in a room about this. Five. Two wanted a room, one wanted to know whether it was safe to be in one, one wanted to know whether it was safe not to be in one, and the fifth was a man who wanted to put his mother's name in it. I have said no five times and I have said the same no five times and the sixth person to be asked in four weeks is me. I am nineteen. I am a student. I am not a body in a room for anybody."

A chair went over at the back and somebody picked it up.

"Thank you. That is the whole of it and I have said it in ninety seconds and there is a poster on the table with a date on it and I would like to spend the rest of it on the poster, because that is a thing that is happening and not a thing anybody has asked me about."

She picked it up.

"**Company name, top right. District service. Module three, open session. The Tuesday of the last week of the term, a hall on a trading estate, four hundred places, no charge, bring a form of identity. And at the bottom, on a line by itself, in the same size as everything else: *maintenance sequence without a supervisory cue — the technician on the kit*.**"

The room did that thing.

"There is no name in it," said Asha Reed. "**Not on the poster. There is no name on the session. There is no name on the sequence and no name on the technician. It is the only thing in nine months I have seen written down that has a hole in it the shape of a person and no person in the hole at all.** And that is not a company doing something sinister, and I want to be careful, because there are about four of you who have already decided it is. It is a company that has printed a poster and the printer has a line and the line says *the technician on the kit* and the printer did not write that, the poster did, and posters get written by about four people in a building and none of them has ever met the technician."

"So it is not about anybody," said Toby Ansell.

"**It is about a slot,**" said Asha Reed. "**And the day a company makes a room public about a technique that nobody owns, then the room does not belong to the case and it does not belong to the technique. The room belongs to whoever printed the poster. That is the whole of it and I have got about two minutes left and I am going to spend the rest of them on the number of people, and then somebody else can have it.**"

She put the poster down.

"**Four hundred places. That is not a class. I have been to a class of forty and I have been to a hall of about nine hundred for a thing in the second term and both of those were people somebody had sent. Four hundred places is four hundred people who were told it was on. If you have got a card, you are one of the four hundred. If you have not got a card, you are one of the four hundred. And there is not one person in this room who has been asked whether you want to be in it, and I have asked five this month and I have said no five times, and I would like somebody in this room to notice that the four hundred is the same sentence with the volume turned up.**"

Rhoda Ilesanmi put her hand up.

"Go on."

"**They have told me I am on a list for the last Friday of the term,**" she said, "**and there is no list. I have been looking for it since Monday and it is not a document, it is not a system, and the woman who told me could not tell me who wrote my name, and she was not being difficult, she was telling me the truth and she works in an office.**"

"Nobody can tell you," said Asha Reed. "That is not a complaint about the woman. **That is the finding. I have said the case is Senn's five times and I have never once been able to make it mean anything, and I have worked out this month why, and it is that *Senn's* is a word that stops the room. As long as it is his, nobody has to look at it, because there is a man, and he is twenty-two, and he can be the fourth thing in it. And the day that stops then the four hundred people are going to be in a room and there is going to be nobody to point at, and I do not know what happens then and I am not going to guess in front of nine people.**"

The room went about two seconds quieter and the clock over the door went past the hour.

"That's time," said Asha Reed.

The Exchange is on the first Wednesday of the month at ten past one in a back room in the Saltmarket with the trestle tables pushed together and the shutters half up, and about nine people came, and Bea Nunn had a tin, and the book was at forty-one lines.

She did not do a photograph and she did not do a round. She took the cards out of the tin and put them face down in a row and turned them over one at a time.

"**Five,**" she said. "Four minutes each. Same as always. The rule is the caller's words, the first person, a signature at the end, and my name in the fourth column, and I have not changed the rule since the Wednesday of week twelve and I am not going to change it this month, and if anybody in this room has a thing they want changed about the book they can put it in the tin and it will be read out at the next one in four minutes like everything else in here, including the complaints.**"

**One.** A boiler. Four minutes, nobody argued, and the man who brought it had written it in the third column before he came, which is against nothing and is what Bea calls being prepared, and it did not go in as a line because he had brought it to the last one as well and it is the same words.

**Two.** She turned the card over and put it in front of her and read it out in full, in her own flat voice, and it took about four minutes and nobody interrupted it.

"*There is a card on a control post at a site off the Meridian that nobody in twenty years has asked where it came from, and there is a card on a table in Bower Street that a woman made at her own kitchen table, and they are the same card, and there is nobody in this city whose job it is to say so. M. Senn.*"

She put it down.

"**That is the first card in this tin in nine years that has come in from a man with a job, and I am not going to read anything into that, because a man with a job is a man who can be found, and I would rather he had put it in here than in a corridor.** It goes in the book. My name in the fourth column, his in the first, in his own words. **And there is no date on it and I am not going to ask him for one, and I said that in this room a fortnight ago and I have thought about it every day since and I am not changing my mind about it in front of a room.**"

She wrote it in. The book went from forty-one lines to forty-two.

And then, before she put the pen down, a man at the back said, "Who do I ring."

The room turned round.

"**Who do I ring,**" he said again. He was about fifty-one and he had the hands of a man who installs and maintains lifting equipment and he had a card in his wallet, and everyone in the room could tell he had a card because of how he was holding the wallet. "I have had one for six years. I have been on nine courses. I have never once been asked where I learned the step, which I do not care about, and I have never once been asked what I am supposed to do if the word does not come, and I have been thinking about that every working day for about six years, and there is nobody to ask, and that is why I am asking in here."

Marek Senn, who was sitting at the end of the trestle table on the side with the light behind him, put both hands on the table and did not say one word, and the reason he did not say one word is that he has an answer and it is about four hundred and six people, and the answer would come out of his mouth in a lanyard in a room with a market outside the shutters, and the two hundred people who have put cards in this tin for nine years would find out in a fortnight that the man with a card is the source.

"So we are not doing that," said Bea Nunn.

"I did not say we were—"

"**You did not say anything, love, you asked a question, and the question is good and it goes in the tin, and the reason it goes in the tin is that it is the only instrument in this room that has ever worked.**" She had a card in her hand that was not in the tin. "Write it in your own words and sign it and put it in there, and it will be read out at the next one in four minutes, and there is no column on that page for the answer, and there has never been a column on that page for the answer, and I have been the woman with the tin for nine years and I have never once been asked that question out loud before today and I have thought about it for about ten seconds and I am not going to think about it for longer because there are four minutes on this card."

He wrote it in his own words and signed it and put it in the tin and did not sit down for about a minute afterwards.

**Three.** A woman of about thirty-four with a hip and a list of her own on a piece of paper, four minutes, and the list was not about anybody else's hip, and it went in the third column and not as a line because it was on the table from the last one.

**Four.** A man who was angry, and had been angry in this room before. He wanted the tin emptied into a district office and he wanted the two hundred names given over and he had brought a printed list with him with about eleven lines on it, and Bea Nunn read the eleven lines out and then said no, and the reason she gave was the same reason she gave a fortnight ago and she gave it in the same words because she is sixty-one and she has decided that saying a thing properly the first time is worth more than saying a new thing.

"**And that is the fourth card I have had in that tin about it in about two years and I have never read one of them out, and I am not going to start today, and I am not going to tell you why either, because the reason is that a frightened person is the easiest thing in the world to point at and I have never once pointed.**"

"You've just read one out," said the man.

"I have read a man with a card out, and he is not frightened, and he is the first one in nine years." She put the card face down. "**Item five.**"

**Five.** Bea Nunn's own, which she had written on a card before the meeting and had not put in the tin and was not going to hand to the taker, and she read it out herself in about ninety seconds and then wrote three words on the back of it in her own hand and did not enter a line, for the second time this term, and the reason she gave is at the end of it.

"**Last time I told you I do not know what to do with a card in this book that somebody is annoyed about rather than frightened about, and I have had a fortnight to find out, and I have not found out, and I have decided that not finding out is the correct position and I am going to hold it.** And I am not putting this one in either, because a thing I write about this book and then put in this book is a line that gets read out at the next one like everything else in here, and in about four years somebody is going to want to know what this room thought about itself and I am not going to be a document about it. **And the other reason, which is the one I actually care about, and I am sixty-one and I will not be here to find out: this morning I rang the woman who types the minutes of the college and asked her what the word *determination* means on a form, and she said she did not know, and she has typed about eleven thousand of them.**"

She turned the card over and wrote the three words and nobody in the room could see what they were and she did not read them out, and four people said so out loud, and she let them.

"Cards," said Bea Nunn, and put the tin on the table.

There was one card in it that had not been in it at the start of the afternoon, and she did not have it in her hand, and she knew.

"**That one came in on Saturday, in the market, at about ten past eleven, in an envelope, and the woman on the stall gave it to him and he gave it to me, and he did not want his name read out and I am not going to read his name out, and I am going to read the card.**"

"What's the rule," said somebody.

"The rule is the caller's words, the first person, a signature at the end, and my name in the fourth column. **He signed it. And he has not put a date on it, and I am not going to ask him for one, and I have now said that in this room twice in three weeks and I have not had a single person thank me for it and I did not do it for that.**"

She read it out.

"*Nineteen years on the same rig. Nobody has ever asked me about my shoulder. I found out I had one from a piece of paper I did not write. I am not asking anybody for anything. D. Molloy.*"

The market went on outside the shutters and a man went past with a pallet and nobody in the room said a word for about four seconds.

"**Forty-third line,**" said Bea Nunn, and wrote his name in the first column and her own in the fourth, and then she stopped with the pen on the page and did not write the date, and did not ask, and did not look up for about two seconds. "**And that is the last line this book gets this term.**"

Marek Senn looked at her.

"**The next one is the first Wednesday of the new term and it is twelve days after the last Friday of this term, and I am not opening this book in between, and I am not opening it on a Friday, and I am not opening it because a room in another building has told me a thing happened and I would like a line about it.**" She put the pen down. "**So that card sits in that tin for twelve days with a shoulder on it, and every one of you is going to leave here and go and do a term, and he is going to go and do a term, and about four years from now somebody who was not in this room is going to read this book and find two names in a row in a fortnight who have got a body in it, and they are going to be the only two, and they will not be the first two and they will not be the last two, and that is the correct record and it is not a good one.**"

Nobody argued with her, which is not the same as agreeing with her.

"Then what happens to it," said Roy Amiss, the man with the card in his wallet, "if it sits there."

"**It happens to it that a man of thirty-nine with a shoulder finds out that a market knows,**" said Bea Nunn, "**and that is the price of this room, and he put it in him self, and I have known him about forty minutes.**"

"I did not say it would be good."

"No," she said. "**You did not. And I have been doing this for nine years and I have never once read a card out that was good, and I have never once read one out that was bad either, and the difference between the two is about four minutes, and about four hundred times somebody has been the right thing to do and about four hundred times somebody has been the fourth thing in a room, and the book cannot tell you which was which, and I have stopped being able to.**"

She picked up the tin and put it in a drawer under the table and shut the drawer.

"**And the other thing, which is the last of it, and it is for the man with the card, and I am not saying it nicely.** You have been thinking about what you would do if the word did not come, every working day, for six years, and you have had a card the whole time, and you did not know that is what a card was for. **And there is a man in this district who has been doing the same thinking for twenty-seven years and has not got a card, and you have both been doing it alone, and neither of you knows the other exists, and I have got two hundred people in this tin and I am not going to be the one who puts you in a room together, because the day this room starts doing that it is a scheme, and a scheme is a thing that can be asked to produce a list.**"
