# Chapter 92 — There Is No Sequence

He got the rig out of the district yard at about seven in the morning on the Tuesday of week twenty-five because the yard keeps two of them on trailers between jobs and the man who looks after them had been in the same queue behind him at the chip shop nine times in a term and had stopped asking why a technician wanted a rig on a Tuesday.

The rig was a gate, a control post, a lamp column, a radio and nine metres of barrier, on a trailer, with a petrol generator that had to be started twice. He had it on the hard standing off Fell Road by half past seven and it was the fourth time in three weeks he had asked for a thing and been given it in four seconds, and he noticed that number, and he wrote it down, because the whole of the term had taught him that a number you have not written down is a number you have decided not to have.

He was not demonstrating anything. He had checked that at about half past six in the morning and he wants it on the record that he checked it, because the condition is on a piece of company paper in a system in a depot and it is the fourth condition and it is unlimited by hours and it covers Institute premises and Institute equipment and Institute insurance, and a hard standing off Fell Road with a customer's gate on it is none of the three. He had gone through it twice and the answer had come out the same both times, and the reason he had gone through it twice was that he wanted a different answer.

---

By about nine he had the barrier up and the lamp column in and the radio working off the generator, and he had written a card of his own and put it in his pocket, and the card had eleven lines on it and it was not a sequence.

He did three versions.

**The first one** was a person. He stood in front of the barrier and called the four steps out loud, and then he stood in front of it and said the word *clear*, and the barrier came down, and it worked, and it took him nine minutes, and when he had done it four times in a row without stopping he wrote on the card, in his own small upright hand, the sentence *four out of four*.

Then he stood there and looked at the card for about a minute and a half, and he read the eleven lines again, and the eleven lines were his own, and they were good, and the reason they were good is that he had written them at about half past six in the morning off about nine years of doing this on rigs at eleven at night for a company that does not ask what the steps are.

And the fourth line of the eleven was a line that said *hold for release*, and the word *release* in it was doing a job, and the job it was doing was the job of the whole exercise, and he had written it, and he had taught it to himself, and it was a slot about nine years old with a man-shaped hole in it and his own handwriting in the hole.

**The second version** was a card. He printed four steps and a line at the bottom, and he laminated nothing, because he had no laminator and no kitchen table, and he put it on the control post with two cable ties, the way somebody has done to a post on another site about twenty years ago, and it looked right, and that was the trouble with it, and he took it down inside four minutes because he could not stand next to it.

A card on a post is a company, is what he had been telling people for a term. A card on a post is a thing anybody who can reach the post can read, and the moment it is up, the person who says the word is anybody who has been there. It was cheaper than a person and it never went on holiday and it never got tired at eleven at night on a Friday, and it was worse than the person, and he knew exactly how much worse and in what order.

**The third version** was a recording. He had a voice memo on his phone and he recorded the eleven lines into it, and he played it into a little speaker he had clamped to the post, and the barrier came down when the recording got to the word, and it worked, and it was the best of the three, and it took eleven minutes.

A recorded voice is a company. It is not a man's voice any more, it is a file on a telephone, and it belongs to whoever has the telephone, and in about four years it is going to be on a card that somebody downloads, and the thing that a woman of thirty-one made at her own kitchen table with a strip of tape across a corner is exactly this, and she made it herself out of nine years of doing the work, and nobody sent it to her, and it cost her four nights and eleven pence, and it is going to be in about four hundred pockets by the spring and nobody has to sell one to anybody.

He wrote four sentences on the back of the card and put the pen down and stood at the barrier with his hands on the top rail.

---

"**That's a good rig,**" said a voice behind him.

He had not heard anybody come up the hard standing, which is a thing that has happened to him exactly twice in his working life and both times it was a dog.

The man was fifty-eight or thereabouts, in a fleece with the arms pushed up, with a pencil behind his ear and a piece of white paint on the back of one hand, and he had a way of standing that was nine years of leaning on a fence.

"Ta," said Marek Senn.

"**Aurel Kosta.**" He did not put a hand out, and Marek Senn did not either, and they looked at the barrier for a second. "**A man in a lanyard rang me on Friday and said there'd be a technician at this yard today and asked me to have a look at it. I said I'd have a look at it. I did not say I would have an opinion and I have not got one yet.**"

"Right," said Marek Senn.

"You are the fourth one this year."

"So I'm told."

"The last three wrote down a fault that was a hinge, and one of them wrote down a latch that had been adjusted." Kosta walked the four paces to the control post and looked at the two cable ties and the bracket where the card had been. "**There was a card on that post an hour ago.**"

"There was."

"You've taken it down."

"Yes."

"Why."

Marek Senn thought about lying to him and worked out in two seconds that a fifty-eight-year-old foreman with a pencil behind his ear and a piece of white paint on his hand would hear it, and would not be unkind about it, and would remember it, and would be right.

"**Because a card on a post is a slot with a hole in it the shape of anybody who can reach the post,**" he said, "and I have spent a term telling people that, and then I laminated nothing and put one up anyway, and I could not stand next to it."

Aurel Kosta turned round and looked at the barrier, and then at the generator, and then at Marek Senn, and the look took four seconds.

"**Show me the other one,**" he said.

---

The third version was the best of the three and it was best for the wrong reason, and it took eleven seconds to run and worked on the first time, and Aurel Kosta watched it twice and did not say anything at all the first time.

"Go on," he said after the second.

"That is a recording of me saying eleven lines, played into a speaker, and the barrier comes down when I get to the word."

"And where's the file."

"On my telephone."

"**On your telephone,**" said Aurel Kosta.

"On my telephone."

"Right." The foreman put the pencil that was behind his ear onto the bracket of the control post, which is a thing men do when they have decided they are going to need it. "**Son, I have been a foreman since I was thirty-one and I have been on a gate since I was twenty-four, and I am going to tell you four things and then I am going to go and do my own job, and I am not going to charge you for any of them because you have not asked me anything.**"

"Go on."

"**One. It works, and it is better than the three men I have had this year, and if you had turned up here on a Friday with a card and a clipboard and told me it was a company standard I would have believed you and I would have been wrong and I would never have known.**"

"Thank you."

"**Two. There is nobody in it.**"

He tipped his head at the speaker.

"**There is a file and a speaker and a wire and a barrier, and none of them has ever been tired, and none of them has ever wanted anything, and in about four years you are going to have a card on that post that says the maintenance is the same whether anybody is standing there or not, and it will be true, and about nine men on my crew are going to believe it, and I am going to be sixty-one and I am going to be standing on a yard at eleven at night on a Friday for about four years still.**"

Marek Senn did not say anything.

"**Three,**" said Aurel Kosta, "**and this is the one that matters and I am going to say it badly. You have spent a fortnight building a hole in a thing the shape of somebody with a lanyard on, and you have done it out of nine years of work at eleven at night, and it is a beautiful hole and I have not seen one before.**"

"I have seen one every day for a term."

"You have not, because I do not think you have been the one standing in it." He took the pencil back off the bracket. "**You are going to stand in a room and do that thing in front of four hundred people, and there is a hole in it, and the hole is the shape of you, and about four hundred people are going to learn in about four years that the fourth beat is somebody in a lanyard, and the day they learn it, you will not be in the room, and the hole will still be there, and the next man through it will not know what shape it is.**"

"What shape is it."

"That is not a question you ask a man you have known for nine minutes."

"It is the only question I have got," said Marek Senn, "and I have got a room in two weeks and four hundred people in it and no sentence."

"Then say that sentence now and say it to me and get it out of your mouth, because that is the last place it is cheap."

Marek Senn looked at the barrier.

"**There is no such thing as a non-hinged maintenance sequence,**" he said.

"Right."

"**There is a thing where a person hands over the release and a thing where a card hands over the release and a thing where a recording hands over the release, and all three of them have a person in the middle of them, and the one I built this morning has me in it, and the only difference between mine and a company's is that mine is cheaper and I have not put a price on it.**"

Aurel Kosta said nothing for four seconds.

"**Then why are you building it,**" he said.

"Because I have not got a better thing to put in the room."

"You have not got a better thing to put in the room, or you have not got anything at all."

"The second one."

"Right." He put the pencil back behind his ear. "**I asked you in week twenty-one, at a fence, and you did not answer me then either, and I want to say the thing I said at the fence again and I want to say it in nine words, because I have said it about four times since and to three different people and I have never once been asked where I got it, and that is the whole reason I am telling you.**"

Marek Senn waited.

"**They have taken the voice out and left the shape of the hand,**" said Aurel Kosta, "**and the hand is still doing what it was told.**"

He looked at his own left shoulder as he said it, and did not explain it, and Marek Senn did not ask, and the not-asking was the hardest thing he did all day and it took a second and a half.

"**Which shoulder,**" said Marek Senn, and then heard himself, and put his hand flat on the barrier.

"No," said Aurel Kosta. "**You don't get to ask me that, and I want you to hear that I am not saying it because of the man, I am saying it because you have been on company time all morning building a hole for a room, and if you start collecting people's shoulders you will end up in a room with nine of them and you will not be able to do your job.**"

"Understood."

"You are twenty-two."

"I am."

"Then do not collect them," said Aurel Kosta, and went to find a ladder that did not need finding.

---

The woman with the clipboard came at about half past eleven and she came the way they come, which is slowly, and she stood at the edge of the hard standing for a minute and a half before she said anything, and she had a district sheet on a board and a pen on a string and a lanyard of a colour that was not his company's.

"Morning," she said. "I'm from the district. I'm not going to say which part of the district because it's a Saturday form and I do not fill in the Saturday forms." She looked at the barrier. "**I'm here to write down that a technician came.**"

"Right."

"That's it. That's the whole of my page. Date, site, technician, and then a box that says *work observed* and I have not put anything in the box because I have not observed any work."

Marek Senn put the barrier down with the switch on the post and came over.

"You can watch," he said.

"**I could,**" said Dawn Treloar, "**and I want to say something to you first and then you can decide, and it is not about you and it is not unkind.** I have stood in nine hundred yards like this one and I have written *work observed* in that box about nine hundred times, and there has never once been a technician who was doing anything that a person in a room could tell you was safe or not safe. Not one. In nine years. **I have been sent here today to write down that a man came, and I have got a form with a box on it, and the box is what I am for.**"

"Then write down that a man came," said Marek Senn.

"That is not the whole of it and you know it is not." She wrote something on the board and turned it round so he could see, and it said *technician attended site, rig in use, no fault identified*, and it was the truest thing on the board and it was a third of what had happened. "**I am going to say the other thing and then I will go and stand by the gate. In about two weeks there is a session in a hall with four hundred people in it, and I am on the list to go, and I have been on about nine of those lists in three years, and there is a woman at that company who books them and she has never once asked me whether I want to be on one. And when I asked her in the spring why we do it that way, she said it was easier.**"

"Easier than what."

"**Easier than cancelling them,**" said Dawn Treloar.

She wrote two more lines on the board and then she took the pen off the string and put it in her pocket and stood by the gate for an hour and forty minutes and watched a man of thirty-four on a kitchen line in Nine Bridges, on Marek Senn's telephone at about ten past one, do the thing he had been told about on the Wednesday of week twenty-two.

He put it on the speaker because Aurel Kosta was eleven feet away on a ladder and Marek Senn wanted both of them to hear it and could not think of a way of doing that which was not dishonest.

"**It is Ozzie,**" said the voice. "**I have got nine minutes before the dinner rush and I am going to use about nine of them, so do not thank me.**"

"Go on."

"**The word is for the pallet that is not ready.**"

That was all of it. Nine words, and the man was gone, and neither of them made a connection and neither of them ever was going to.

Marek Senn stood on a hard standing off Fell Road with a telephone in his hand and the two of them looked at the barrier.

"**Say that again,**" said Aurel Kosta from the ladder.

"I cannot, it is on a telephone."

"Then say the shape of it." He came down. "**Because that is what he said, is it not. He said the word is for a pallet that is not there. And the pallet is not there. And so he waits. And nobody told him to wait and nobody is watching him wait and there is no card on the wall and there is no man in a lanyard and there is no lecture and he is not being anybody's fourth thing.**"

"Yes."

"**Then what is he doing.**"

"He is not doing the fourth beat," said Marek Senn. "**He is doing the fourth beat. It is the same thing. It is the same thing with the slot not cut in it, because nobody has ever cut it in, and that is the only difference and it is not a small one and it is the whole of it, and I have spent a term trying to work out how to put it in a room and I cannot put it in a room, because the moment you put it in a room you cut the slot, and you cut the slot by making a person watch, and the making a person watch is the cut.**"

Aurel Kosta stood about four feet away with a piece of white paint on the back of his hand and did not say anything for long enough for it to be a decision.

"**Twenty-seven years,**" he said.

"Twenty-seven years of a gate."

"**And I have not written down the tenth time.**"

"No."

"**About nine times out of ten the wait is the right call and I have been a foreman since I was thirty-one and I have never been able to write down one of the others, and do you know why, and it is not because I forget.**"

"No," said Marek Senn. "It is because a man who writes down the tenth time has to be able to say what he did instead, and you have never known."

"Right," said Aurel Kosta. "**And that is the thing I have been trying to say to about four people this month and I have not said it to one of them, and now I have said it to you, and I have still not got a way of writing it down, and I am going to think about it on Monday.**"

He put his hand up at that point, which he had not done before.

Marek Senn took the card out of his pocket and looked at the eleven lines on it and then took the pen out and wrote one more line at the bottom, underneath everything, in his own small upright hand, and it was not a step and it was not a beat and it was not a sequence.

He wrote: *there is nothing here that a person has to hand over.*

Then he tore it in half, in front of both of them, and put the two halves in his pocket, because he had understood while he was writing it that he was not going to be able to stand next to it either.

*95. Tuesday of week 25, about half past nine at night, and this one is not about me, and it is the first time in a term I have written an entry that is about a man who came to a yard, and I did that on purpose, because he is fifty-eight and he has a pencil behind his ear and he is the fourth foreman this term who has been sent to look at a thing by a man in a lanyard, and three of the four wrote down a fault that was not a fault, and the fourth wrote down a true sentence to me before I had asked him anything.*

*Aurel Kosta, fifty-eight, on a hard standing off Fell Road at about half past nine in the morning, a fleece with the arms pushed up, white paint on the back of the left hand, and a way of standing that is about nine years of leaning on a fence.* **He came because a man in a lanyard rang him on the Friday and asked him to have a look and he said he would have a look and he said so out loud, and he did not say he would have an opinion, and he had not got one when he got here, and I want that on the page because a foreman who tells you what he has agreed to and nothing else is the reason this whole volume has not gone wrong somewhere.** He looked at my rig for four seconds and said: that is a good rig. Then he said: there was a card on that post an hour ago, and I said yes, and he said why, and I did not lie to him, and I have decided that not lying was not a virtue and was arithmetic.

*And then he asked me why I was building it, and I gave him the true answer, which is that I have not got a better thing to put in the room, and then I gave him the true answer, which is that I have not got anything at all, and a fifty-eight-year-old man on a ladder with a pencil behind his ear looked at me and said: right.* And then he said the thing I have been carrying, and he said it in nine words, and he has said it about four times since week twenty-one to three different people and nobody has ever asked him where he got it, and that is the whole reason he told me. **They have taken the voice out and left the shape of the hand, and the hand is still doing what it was told.** He looked at his own left shoulder while he said it. **I asked him which shoulder, and I heard myself ask, and I put my hand on the barrier, and he said: you do not get to ask me that, and I want you to hear that I am not saying it because of the man, I am saying it because you have been on company time all morning building a hole for a room, and if you start collecting people's shoulders you will end up in a room with nine of them and you will not be able to do your job.** He was right. I have not written his shoulder down. I am not going to, and I want that on the page in my own hand so that in about four years I cannot pretend to have forgotten that I was asked and refused.
