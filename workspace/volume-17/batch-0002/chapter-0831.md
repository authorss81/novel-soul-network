# Chapter 831 — A Monday, A Bench Until Two And A Counter Until About Six, And A Room Over A Shop In A Second District Where About Nine Men Who Drive Vans Went Up A Stair To Hear A Place Name Read Out Loud, About Four Of Them Said They Had Heard It Before, About Four Said They Had Not, And The Man Who Read It Out Keeps A Wall Map And Has Never Been To The Place It Names

**A Monday was a bench until two and a counter until about six, and it finished in a room over a shop in a second district where about nine men who drive vans went up a stair to hear a place name read out loud. About four had heard it before and about four had not, and the man who read it out keeps a wall map and has never been there.**

---

Those four rooms off that service road stand at one thousand five hundred and thirty-three days, two hundred and nineteen weeks to the day, and the card on end in the rail by that door is four days further on at one thousand five hundred and thirty-seven, two hundred and nineteen weeks and four days, and the rail takes two.

**Line sixteen is one thousand three hundred and twenty-three days, a hundred and eighty-nine weeks to the day. The nine hand copies of the front of one page are one thousand and ninety-nine, a hundred and fifty-seven weeks to the day, one corner torn, eight unfinished, and not two compared. The separation stands at nine hundred and thirteen days, a hundred and thirty weeks and three days, in a one-line box about sixteen months old.**

---

**And the man of about thirty-four who owns four vehicles brought a clipboard into that room with him and put it on a table, and the reason he brought it is that the box at the bottom of it wants the name of a place and he cannot get one out of anybody in his own office.**

He said, in about nine seconds, that a driver's papers do not take a country a man cannot name, and that his office has a wall map on a wall in it, and that the map says the runs finish in Aldermere, and that the map is the only thing anybody in that office has ever been given to go on.

**He said that he had asked two men in that office and one had said Aldermere and one had said Ashlade and that neither of them had been to either and that the two of them had been arguing about it for about four years.**

A man of about forty-four said that a map on a wall is a thing a man reads and does not argue with, and that the arguing has been about which of the two names is the older one.

Nobody in that room said which of the two is the older one.

---

**And they sent for the man who keeps the map, and he came up that stair at about half past six and he is about thirty-one and he has not been asked a question about the map in about four years.**

He works in an office that moves parcels for about nine vans. He does not drive. He has a key to a cupboard the maps are kept in and he takes them out when they are wanted and puts them back.

**He said that he had never been to Aldermere and that he had never been to Ashlade, and that he had put that map up himself about four years ago out of a cupboard where it had been in a tube since before the spring before that.**

He said the tube had a label on it with a date on it and the date was the year the map was printed and he read the date out and nobody in that room wrote it down.

**He said that Aldermere was the name on the map and that he was not saying it was the name of anything else, and that he would write it down if somebody gave him a piece of paper.**

They gave him a piece of paper.

---

**He wrote it in capitals about four inches apart and he spelled it twice before he was satisfied and he read it out loud once, in about four seconds, to about nine men in a room over a shop.**

A L D E R M E R E.

**About four of the men in that room said they had heard that name before, and about four said they had not, and nobody in that room said where the four who had heard it had heard it.**

A man of about thirty-eight said that he had heard it at a school in a second district about nine years ago from a man who had come back from somewhere, and that the man had not said where, and that nobody in that school had asked.

A man of about forty-four said that he had heard Ashlade twice and never Aldermere, and that both times had been from a printed thing and not from a person.

A man of about twenty-nine said that he had never heard either and had come because he drives into that country on a run twice a month and has never known what to write on a box.

---

**And the man of about fifty-two said the thing that everybody in that room was carrying, and he said it in about nine seconds and he said it to the paper and not to anybody.**

He said that he has heard two names for that stretch of country and has heard both of them in this city and has never once heard either of them from anybody who has been standing in it, and that about four people in this city use one of them and about four use the other and that none of those people has been there.

He said that about four people in this city have been using a name for somewhere on the strength of a sheet of paper, and that he does not know which sheet, and that he has wanted to know for about two years.

**Nobody in that room asked him which sheet and nobody in that room has asked him since, and about two people in that room have said afterwards that they had it in front of them and had not asked.**

---

**And then the paper went round the table and about nine men looked at it, and nobody in that room said the name was wrong, and the reason nobody said it is not that nobody knew.**

The man of about thirty-one said, to the room, that the word Aldermere is the name of a town, and that the town is in the middle of the stretch on the map and the map names the whole stretch after it.

A woman of about thirty-nine who drives for the man of about thirty-four said, in about nine seconds, that her mother's side is from that town.

**Nobody in that room asked her a question and nobody in that room has asked her one since, and she said it to the table and then said she had not meant to put it in the middle of it.**

A man of about thirty-eight said that a town and a stretch are not the same size of thing and that the map was made by somebody who wanted one name on it.

The man of about thirty-one said that he agreed, and that he had said so at the time, and that nobody in that office had asked him then either.

**Nobody in that room asked him what he had said at the time and about four people in that room have said since that they would have liked to hear it and did not ask.**

---

**And nobody in that room had any standing to say the name was wrong, and the reason is a thing about that room and not a thing anybody was told.**

There is nobody in that room who goes there. There is nobody in that room who has a family there. There is a woman of about thirty-nine who has said one sentence about her mother's side and has not said another and is not going to, and there are about eight men who have come in vans.

**About four people in that room worked out during that hour that the only person who could say the name was wrong was about nine days away on a bus, and about four of them looked at each other about nine minutes before anybody said so.**

The man of about thirty-four said it, in about nine seconds, and he said it to the room and not to the man of about thirty-one, and he said that there is nobody to send.

He said that this is a thing this city has done about nine thousand times and has never once written down.

Nobody in that room asked him how he knows that and he did not say.

---

**And about four men in that room copied the name onto about four clipboards before they left, and one of them wrote it on the back of his hand, and the man of about thirty-four took the paper away with him folded in four.**

The man of about thirty-one went back down the stair to an office with a map on a wall in it. **He has said since that he looked at the map for about nine minutes after he got back and that he has looked at it every working day since and that he has not taken it down.**

Nobody has asked him to take it down.

---

**And the rest of that Monday was a counter until about six and about two hours of work in a second district, where a run of display shelving in a shop had been fixed through a fascia that had been replaced about nine years ago and not the shelf.**

He took the run off, filled the nine holes in the old fascia, cut four lengths to the depth of the rafter tail, let them into the plumb of the rafters, and hung the run off those.

**Six pounds. He told the man that a shelf through a replaced fascia is a shelf carried on about nine years of weather and about nine inches of board, and that four runs of display shelving in that street are on replaced fascias, and that one of them has come down about four inches on its left end and been propped up with a length of timber.**

---

**And a door closer on the shop's own back door had been set to slam hard enough that the latch could not be lifted from the yard without a hand round the edge.**

He came off the closer, took a pin out of the spindle, turned the body a quarter on its back plate, and put it back.

**Nine pounds. He told the man that a closer set to slam is a door that has to be hit to be opened, that four closers on that yard's doors are set the same way, and that one of them has a strike plate worn to about a third of what it was.**

---

**And two floor springs at a shop entrance had been packed with mortar by a builder who thought they were thresholds.**

He pulled both out, cleared the mortar out of the pockets with a chisel, bedded them again on the original grease, and set the second one back about a quarter inch so that the first closes and the second does not.

**Seven pounds. He told the man that a spring packed with mortar is a spring that does not come back, that four entrances on that street have theirs buried, and that one of them has a door that drops about half an inch onto the frame every time it lets go.**

---

**And a length of guttering on a lock-up keeper's hut had been fixed with four brackets into a fascia that was not over anything at all.**

He took the gutter off, filled the four holes, cut four lengths to the rafter depth, let them into the plumb of the rafters, and hung the gutter through those.

**Four pounds. He told the man that a bracket into a fascia that carries nothing is a gutter hanging off four screws and four inches of board, and that four lengths of guttering in that district are on that kind of fascia, and that one of them has pulled about half an inch away at the end nearest the door.**

---

**From half past six there has been a woman of about thirty at her chair in the fourth of those four rooms with her door shut behind her, and on the back shelf behind her the eighth of eight has lain for three hundred and twenty-two days.**

**Nobody has asked her one question about it and nobody is going to, and this page does not say what is written on it.**
