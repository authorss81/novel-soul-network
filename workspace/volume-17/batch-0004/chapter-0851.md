# Chapter 851 — A Monday, A Bench Until Two And A Counter Until About Six, And A First Floor Over A Parcel Office In A Third District Where Somebody Read Out Loud For The First Time A Wording Meant To Be Printed On A Sheet And Put In Front Of A Person Before They Agree To Anything, And A Man Of About Thirty-Six Asked In About Nine Seconds Who Reads It To The Four Hundred, And Nobody In That Room Could Answer

**The reading out began at about ten past seven on that Monday in a first floor over a parcel office in a third district, and about nine people were in the room and about four of them had come about the wording rather than about anything else. Nobody decided anything that evening. Somebody read nine lines aloud that had been written to go on a sheet.**

---

Eleven names went onto that Monday's book and eleven dates went against them, the last of those dates at about twenty to five, and the shutter came down at about ten.

**Four rooms off that service road, and one of them standing behind the other three, are one thousand five hundred and sixty-one days, two hundred and twenty-three weeks to the day. The sixteenth line on the board on two nails is one thousand three hundred and fifty-one days, a hundred and ninety-three weeks to the day. The nine hand copies of the front of one page stand at one thousand one hundred and twenty-seven days, a hundred and sixty-one weeks to the day, and not two of the nine have been set beside each other.**

---

The bench in the Lattice Ward repair shop took him until two, and the counter took the rest of it until about six, and on that Monday the counter was quiet in the way a counter is quiet when the weather has not decided.

He fixed a bench leg that had been packed with a folded length of cardboard, and he told the man that cardboard is a packing for a week and that about four benches in that shop have been packed with it and one of them has a leg that has been moving under a man's weight for about four years and has been reported as a wobble.

---

The room was over a parcel office and it had been lent for the evening by a man who runs the counter downstairs, and he was not told what the room was for and did not ask.

About nine people came up the stair between about half past six and about eight. About four of them had come about the wording. The other five came because about nine people had been going up these stairs on weeknights for a month and a person who is on their own most evenings will go.

The wording was on a sheet of typing paper and it was nine lines long and it had been typed twice, because the first typing had a smudge in the fourth line that the woman who typed it would not have.

**A woman of about thirty-six read it out in a flat voice, standing, with the sheet in both hands, and she got to the end of the ninth line and stopped, and nobody in that room said anything for about nine seconds.**

The nine lines were meant to sit at the top of a sheet, above everything else, above the boxes and above the line a person signs, and they were meant to say what a person is agreeing to stay inside of.

They said what the room was called. They said how often it met and at what hour. They said how many people sat in it. They said that anybody in it could stop coming and that nobody would ask them why.

---

**And then a man of about thirty-six said the thing that has no answer, and he said it in about nine seconds, and about nine people in that room heard all of it.**

He said: who reads that to the four hundred people, and how, and on what day.

He said it in the tone of a man asking where a form is filed, and he said it to the sheet and not to anybody, and then he turned round and looked at the woman who had read it.

**Nobody in that room answered him and nobody in that room has answered him since.**

A woman of about thirty-two said in about nine seconds that the four hundred are not this body's and not anybody's in this city and that a room of nine people cannot go and read a sheet to four hundred people in four towns.

The man of about thirty-six said that was not what he had asked, and he said it in about four seconds, and what he had asked was who.

---

**And a man of about thirty-three, who had written the wording over about two evenings, said in about nine seconds that the nine lines do not say one word about the four hundred and are not meant to.**

He said that the ninth line is about anybody who sits in that room and stops coming, and that the ninth line is the only line anybody in that room argued about for longer than a minute, and that the eight lines above it are the address of a building.

He said that a person who is handed a sheet in a shop in this city wants to know what is in the shop, and that a person who is handed one on a bus two days from here wants to know what is on the bus.

**Nobody in that room said whether those nine lines were the ones wanted and nobody in that room said they were the wrong ones, and the sheet stayed on the table where it was read from until the shutter came down.**

A woman of about thirty-eight, who has asked a question in five rooms in about nine days, said in about nine seconds that she has never wanted a form to say less than this and has never wanted one to say only this.

She said that the four hundred are not going to read a sheet in a shop in this city, and that the four hundred will read whatever a person says to them at a stop or at a door, and that this city has spent about nine years learning what to say at a door and has not spent any of it on a sheet.

---

**And a man of twenty-two was in that room and was not asked anything and did not say anything for about the whole of it.**

He is not the person who signs anything in this stretch of days and was not the person that sheet was put up to, and he had spent the evening thinking about one sentence in the third line and had worked out that it is the only one of the nine that a person can check for themselves.

He said, later and not in that room, that the third line is the only one of the nine that can be found out to be true, and that it is the one nobody will check, because a person who has been told what a room is in advance is not going to go and count the chairs.

**Nobody in that room asked him what he had worked out and he did not say it there.**

He said one thing about how the sheet would travel, and he said it to the man of about thirty-three and not to the room: that the only way anything gets to that country in this city is on a bus that somebody has paid for, and that he is not that person and has not got nine hundred pounds for it.

---

**And the woman of about forty-one who keeps a laundry on a road in a first district was not in that room, and nobody in that room knew she had ever written anything at all.**

Her book is on a shelf behind her own counter and this is not a thing anybody in a first floor over a parcel office has been told.

---

**And then at about twenty to seven the sheet was not folded and was not taken away, and the man of about thirty-three put his hand flat on the top of it and asked whether anybody in that room thought the nine lines were wrong.**

About four people in that room said no and about four said nothing, and a woman of about thirty-one said in about nine seconds that wrong is a word for a thing somebody has decided and nothing in that room had decided anything yet.

**Nobody thanked anybody for reading it out and nobody thanked anybody for the question, and the shutter came down at about ten.**

The one decision of that Monday was made by the man of about thirty-six who had asked, and it took about nine seconds: he will be at a counter in this city on the Tuesday, and he will ask the man of about thirty-three one question, which is whether every one of those nine lines is already printed on a form this city sends out.

---

---

**And the rest of that Monday was about two hours of work in a fourth district.**

**A pump gland in the same yard had been repacked with a rope and had been leaking about a spoonful a minute onto the concrete, which had been walked on until it had a shine on it.**

He took the gland apart, cleared the rope out of it, repacked it with the flat ring that belonged in it and brought it up to the stuffing box properly.

**Twenty-four pounds. He told the man that rope in a gland is a plug that pays water back at a rate nobody can see, and that about four glands in that yard have been packed with rope, and that one of them has been packed that way twice and has been described as a weeping pump for about four years.**

---

**A bench in a workshop in the same building had two of its legs bolted through the seat rail instead of through the frame, so that the whole thing had racked.**

He took the seat off, took out the four bolts, drilled the frame properly, plugged the old holes, and refitted the seat so the frame took the load.

**Twenty-six pounds. He told the man that a bolt through a seat rail is a bolt into end grain, and that about four benches in that building have been made that way, and that one of them has a leg that has been reported as a loose frame for about four years and has been packed at the foot instead.**

---

**A length of rail in the same yard had about a fifth of its chairs packed with split timber instead of a steel shim, so that the joint worked loose under a wagon and the timber crushed and set again every time it was packed.**

He took that fifth up, packed it with shims, and brought the joint up to where the fastenings could carry it.

**Twenty-six pounds. He told the man that split timber in a chair is a chair that has to be packed every week, and that about four lengths in that yard have timber in them, and that one of them has been reported as a gauge fault twice and blamed on a fitter for turning a chair the wrong way round.**

---

**A stack of sheets in the same building had been standing against a hot pipe on a wall, and about four of the sheets at the back had gone soft and had curled.**

He took the stack down, cut the four off, put a bearer across so nothing stood against that pipe, and stacked them back on that.

**Twenty-four pounds. He told the man that a stack against a pipe is a stack that is warmed on one side and cools on the other, and that four stacks in that building stand against a wall with a pipe in it, and that one of them has sheets that have been cut off twice in about four years and put back the same way.**

**That Monday came to a hundred pounds, exact, which is the four of them together.**

**From half past six there has been a woman of about thirty at her chair in the fourth of those four rooms with her door shut, and on the back shelf behind her the eighth of eight has lain for three hundred and fifty days.**

**Nobody has asked her one question about it. Nobody is going to. What is on that page is not given here.**
